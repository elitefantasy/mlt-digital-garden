# HIV Infection

## Overview

Human immunodeficiency virus is an enveloped RNA retrovirus. The important types are:

- HIV-1, responsible for most infections worldwide.
- HIV-2, found mainly in West Africa but also reported in other regions.

HIV infects mainly CD4 T lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. The viral RNA is reverse-transcribed into ==DNA== and integrated into the host-cell genome.

The presence of anti-HIV antibody indicates infection and immune response; it does **not** indicate immunity.

## Transmissibility

HIV can be transmitted through:

- Transfusion of infected blood or blood components.
- Sharing contaminated needles or syringes.
- Sexual exposure.
- Mother-to-child transmission during pregnancy, delivery, or breastfeeding.
- Occupational exposure to infected blood.

Blood transfusion can transmit a large viral dose. WHO describes the estimated infectivity of infected blood products as approximately 95%, which is higher than many other exposure routes

The virus may remain present in stored blood and blood components. Therefore, screening and prevention of donation by high-risk individuals are essential.

## HIV Screening

Blood-donation screening may detect:

1. Anti-HIV-1 and anti-HIV-2 antibodies.
2. HIV p24 antigen.
3. HIV RNA.

The preferred serological test is generally a **fourth-generation HIV antigen–antibody immunoassay**, which detects both ==p24 antigen and anti-HIV antibodies.==

Common techniques include:
- ELISA or EIA.
- Chemiluminescent immunoassay.
- Electrochemiluminescence immunoassay.
- Validated rapid immunochromatographic tests in appropriate settings.
- NAT for HIV RNA.


## What does each HIV test detect?

| Test                           | Detects                             | Approximate appearance                                      |
| ------------------------------ | ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| HIV NAT                        | HIV RNA                             | About 7–11 days after infection                             |
| p24 antigen                    | ==HIV capsid protein==              | About 3–10 days after HIV RNA                               |
| Fourth-generation test         | p24 antigen plus anti-HIV antibody  | May become reactive approximately 2–4 weeks after infection |
| Third-generation antibody test | Anti-HIV-1/2 antibody               | Approximately 3 weeks or more after infection               |
| Rapid antibody test            | Anti-HIV antibody, depending on kit | Usually later than laboratory Ag/Ab tests                   |

These are approximate periods. The exact time varies with the assay, viral load, specimen type, and individual immune response. A negative result soon after a possible exposure does not always exclude infection.

## When is HIV antibody detected?

HIV antibody is often detectable approximately **==three weeks== after infection**, although some individuals seroconvert later. WHO notes that antibody may appear about six days after p24 antigen becomes detectable.

A practical interpretation is:

- HIV RNA may become detectable first.
- ==p24 antigen== becomes detectable next.
- Anti-HIV antibody develops later.
- Fourth-generation testing reduces, but does not completely eliminate, the diagnostic window period.

## HIV NAAT

### Principle

HIV nucleic acid amplification testing detects HIV RNA in plasma or other validated specimens.

The general process is:
1. HIV RNA is extracted from the specimen.
2. RNA is converted into complementary DNA.
3. Target sequences are amplified.
4. Amplified product is detected using fluorescent probes or another signal system.
5. A positive result indicates the presence of HIV nucleic acid and requires appropriate confirmation according to the laboratory algorithm.

For blood screening, NAT may be performed as:

- Individual-donation NAT, or ID-NAT.
- Minipool NAT, in which specimens from several donations are pooled before testing.

### Uses

HIV NAT is used for:

- Detecting very early HIV infection.
- Screening donated blood.
- Reducing the window period.
- Resolving certain indeterminate serological results.
- Confirming suspected acute infection.
- Monitoring viral load after HIV infection is diagnosed.
- Detecting infection in infants, where maternal antibody makes antibody testing difficult.

HIV RNA may be detectable approximately **7–11 days after infection**, sometimes while antigen–antibody tests are still negative. Detecting HIV RNA in donated blood can therefore reduce transmission from donations collected during the serological window period

### p24 antigen

p24 is a structural capsid protein of HIV.

Important points:
- It appears before most HIV antibodies.
- It indicates active viral replication or the presence of virus.
- It may become detectable approximately 3–10 days after HIV RNA.
- It is detected in fourth-generation HIV Ag/Ab assays.
- Its concentration may decrease after antibodies develop because immune complexes form.
- It helps shorten the window period compared with antibody-only testing.

## How HIV testing reduces transmission

HIV testing reduces transfusion transmission by:

- Identifying reactive donations before release.
- Detecting antibody-positive donations.
- Detecting p24 antigen during early infection.
- Detecting HIV RNA before serological markers appear.
- Removing reactive units and associated components from inventory.
- Improving donor selection and deferral.
