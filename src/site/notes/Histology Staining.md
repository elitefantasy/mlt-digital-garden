general procedure
1. Drying: slide is dried completely in hot air oven
2. Deparaffinization: slide is placed in xylene for 5 min and again for additional 5 minute in another xylene path
3. Hydration: slides are passed through decreasing concentration 90,80,70%. finally these slide are washed in distilled water 
4. Staining: Slide immersed in hematoxylin solution (5-15 min). What happens here: Hematein-mordant complex enters tissue Binds to nuclei Nuclei become red-purple initially
5. Washing (Rinsing) : Tap water wash Removes excess stain
6. Bluing reaction (VERY IMPORTANT) : Slide placed in alkaline solution: • Ammonia water OR • Scott's tap water substitute . Chemical change: Hematoxylin converts from reddish-purple → blue . This stabilizes nuclear staining
7. Differentiation (optional in regressive staining) Acid alcohol dip Removes excess stain from cytoplasm Leaves only strong nuclear stain
8. Counterstaining (if H&E method used):  Eosin applied . Stains cytoplasm pink
9. Dehydration again : Alcohol series removes water
10. Clearing : Xylene used again
11. Mounting : DPX or resin added Coverslip placed Permanent slide ready

## Hematoxylin Stain

- **Hematoxylin** is a natural dye extracted from the heartwood of the logwood tree **_Haematoxylum campechianum_** 
- By itself, hematoxylin is **not** the active stain. It must be **oxidized** to **hematein**, which is the true chromogen that binds to tissue.
- Hematein is a weak anionic dye that needs a **metal mordant** (usually **aluminum**, **iron**, or **tungsten**) to form a **dye–mordant–tissue complex**, giving strong, stable nuclear staining. 

### Key terms

- **Mordant:** Metal ion (Al³⁺, Fe³⁺, etc.) that links hematein to tissue, especially nuclear chromatin.
- **Hemalum:** Term for **aluminum–hematein** complexes (most routine nuclear stains). 
- **Progressive vs regressive:** Way the stain is used, not a different dye.
    - **Progressive:** Stain until nuclei reach desired intensity; no differentiation.
    - **Regressive:** Deliberately overstain, then **differentiate** in acid alcohol to remove excess from cytoplasm/background, leaving crisp nuclei.

### Steps
1. oxidation: Hematoxylin → Hematein (active form) This oxidation can occur: . Naturally (slow aging) . Chemically (using sodium iodate, mercuric oxide, etc.)
2. Step 2: Formation of dye-mordant complex : Hematein binds with a mordant (metal ion) such as: Aluminum (Al) → most common Iron (Fe³+) . This forms a hematein-mordant complex
3. Step 3: Nature of dye : This complex acts as a: Basic dye (cationic dye)
4. Step 4: Binding mechanism : Cell nuclei contain nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) These have negatively charged phosphate groups The positively charged dye binds electrostatically This is called basophilic reaction
### 2) Classification of hematoxylin stains

#### A) By mordant (most important for exams)
1. **Alum hematoxylins (Aluminum mordants)** – “hemalums”
    - Most commonly used in routine H&E.
    - Give **blue‑purple nuclei** after bluing.
    - Examples: **Ehrlich’s, Harris’s, Mayer’s, Gill’s, Delafield’s, Carazzi’s, Cole’s**. 
2. **Iron hematoxylins (Ferric mordants)**
    - Very intense, black‑blue nuclear staining; often used for **special techniques** and when strong contrast is needed.
    - More resistant to decolorization; often used in combination with other stains.
    - Examples: **Heidenhain’s, Weigert’s** (iron hematoxylin used for connective tissue/fibrin stains). 
3. **Tungsten / other metal hematoxylins**
    - Less common in routine work; used in some specialized protocols. 

#### B) By staining method
- **Progressive hematoxylins**
    - Lower dye concentration; selective for nuclei.
    - Examples: **Mayer’s, Gill’s (some formulations), Carazzi’s**. 
- **Regressive hematoxylins**
    - Higher dye concentration; stain nuclei + cytoplasm intensely, then differentiated.
    - Examples: **Harris’s, Ehrlich’s (often used regressively), some Heidenhain protocols**. 

### 3) Major alum hematoxylins: composition and features
#### 1) Ehrlich’s hematoxylin (1886)
- **Type:** Alum hematoxylin; traditionally **natural ripening** (slow oxidation by air/light). Often used **regressively**.
- **Mordant:** **Potassium alum** (or ammonium alum) → provides **Al³⁺**.
- **Typical composition (classic formula):**
    - Hematoxylin
    - Potassium alum (or ammonium alum)
    - Glycerol
    - Ethanol (alcohol)
    - Distilled water
    - Sometimes **glacial acetic acid** to sharpen nuclear detail
    - No chemical oxidant originally (ripens naturally over weeks). 
- **Features / uses:**
    - Deep, stable nuclear staining.
    - Good for H&E and some special stains.
    - Long shelf life once ripened. 

#### 2) Delafield’s hematoxylin
- **Type:** Alum hematoxylin; **naturally ripened**, then stabilized.
- **Mordant:** **Ammonium alum** or **potassium alum**.
- **Typical composition:**
    - Hematoxylin dissolved in **alcohol**
    - Saturated solution of **ammonium alum** (or potassium alum) in water
    - Mixed, allowed to ripen, then filtered
    - Often contains **glycerol** and sometimes **methanol/ethanol** as preservative.
- **Features:**
    - Clear nuclear detail; used in both progressive and regressive modes.
    - Historically important; less common now compared to Harris/Mayer/Gill. 
#### 3) Harris’s hematoxylin (1900)
- **Type:** Alum hematoxylin; **chemically ripened**; classically **regressive**.
- **Mordant:** **Potassium alum** or **ammonium alum** (Al³⁺).
- **Oxidant:** **Mercuric oxide (HgO)** (traditional); some modern variants use sodium iodate to avoid mercury. 
- **Typical composition (classic Harris):**
    - Hematoxylin (dissolved in alcohol)
    - **Potassium alum** or **ammonium alum** in distilled water
    - **Mercuric oxide** (added while heating to oxidize hematoxylin to hematein)
    - Sometimes a small amount of **glacial acetic acid**
    - May contain **ethylene glycol** or glycerol in modified formulas as preservative. 
- **Features / uses:**
    - Very **intense, crisp nuclear staining**; ideal for **regressive H&E**.
    - Widely used in histopathology and in classical **Papanicolaou (Pap) stain**.
    - Requires **differentiation** in acid alcohol and **bluing** in alkaline solution.

#### 4) Mayer’s hematoxylin (1903)
- **Type:** Alum hematoxylin; **chemically ripened**; typically **progressive**.
- **Mordant:** **Potassium alum** or **ammonium alum**.
- **Oxidant:** **Sodium iodate (NaIO₃)** – fast, controlled oxidation. 
- **Typical composition (classic Mayer’s):**
    - **Hematoxylin:** ~1 g/L
    - **Potassium (or ammonium) alum:** ~50 g/L
    - **Sodium iodate:** ~0.2 g/L
    - **Citric acid:** ~1 g/L (acidifies, sharpens nuclei)
    - **Chloral hydrate:** ~50 g/L (preservative, also helps clarity)
    - Distilled water to volume. 
- **Features / uses:**
    - **Progressive** nuclear stain: stain time controls intensity; usually **no differentiation** needed.
    - Gives **clean, crisp nuclei** with relatively light cytoplasmic background.
    - Very popular for **IHC slides** where minimal background is desired. 

#### 5) Gill’s hematoxylin (Gill I, II, III)
- **Type:** Alum hematoxylin; **chemically ripened**; can be used **progressively** (especially Gill I & II) or more strongly (Gill III).
- **Mordant:** **Aluminum sulfate** (Al₂(SO₄)₃) → Al³⁺.
- **Oxidant:** **Sodium iodate**.
- **Preservative:** **Ethylene glycol** (instead of chloral hydrate).
- **Typical composition (per liter; approximate):** **Gill I (weaker, progressive cytology):**
    - Hematoxylin: **2 g**
    - Sodium iodate: **0.2 g**
    - Aluminum sulfate: **17.6 g**
    - Glacial acetic acid: **20 mL**
    - Ethylene glycol: **250 mL**
    - Distilled water to 1 L
    **Gill II (intermediate):**
    - Hematoxylin: **4 g**
    - Sodium iodate: **0.4 g**
    - Aluminum sulfate: **35.2 g**
    - Glacial acetic acid: **40 mL**
    - Ethylene glycol: **250 mL**
    - Water to 1 L
    **Gill III (stronger, histology):**
    - Hematoxylin: **6 g**
    - Sodium iodate: **0.6 g**
    - Aluminum sulfate: ~**54 g**
    - Glacial acetic acid: **60 mL**
    - Ethylene glycol: **250 mL**
    - Water to 1 L 
- **Features / uses:**
    - Very **stable**, long shelf life; does not form surface “scum” easily.
    - Gill I/II: often **progressive**, good for cytology and some IHC.
    - Gill III: stronger; can be used regressively for routine histology.
#### 6) Carazzi’s hematoxylin
- **Type:** Alum hematoxylin; **chemically ripened**; generally **progressive**.
- **Mordant:** **Potassium alum** or **ammonium alum**.
- **Oxidant:** **Sodium iodate**.
- **Typical features:**
    - Contains **glycerol**, alcohol, and sometimes **chloral hydrate** or other stabilizers.
    - Designed for rapid, progressive nuclear staining with good clarity.
- **Uses:**
    - Routine H&E where a progressive stain is preferred.
    - Often used in cytology and some histology labs.
#### 7) Cole’s hematoxylin
- **Type:** Alum hematoxylin; less commonly emphasized in basic exams but appears in advanced texts.
- **Mordant:** Aluminum salt (alum).
- **Features:**
    - Formulated for stable, progressive nuclear staining.
    - Used in some specific protocols and research settings.


### 4) Iron hematoxylins (high‑yield concept)
#### General features
- **Mordant:** **Ferric ions** (Fe³⁺), usually from **ferric ammonium sulfate** or **ferric chloride**.
- Produce very **intense black‑blue** nuclear staining; highly resistant to decolorization.
- Often used in **special stains** and techniques requiring strong nuclear contrast. 
#### 1) Heidenhain’s iron hematoxylin
- **Type:** Iron hematoxylin; typically **regressive**.
- **Mordant:** **Ferric ammonium sulfate** (iron alum).
- **Procedure (conceptual):**
    - Sections are **mordanted** first in iron alum solution.
    - Then stained with **hematoxylin** (often in alcohol/water).
    - Differentiated in **iron alum** or acid alcohol until only nuclei (and sometimes specific structures) retain stain. 
- **Uses:**
    - Detailed nuclear and cytoplasmic studies; some muscle and connective tissue stains; research histology. 

#### 2) Weigert’s iron hematoxylin
- **Type:** Iron hematoxylin; very stable in acidic conditions.
- **Mordant:** **Ferric chloride** (or other ferric salt) + hematoxylin in alcohol.
- **Key feature:** Resists decolorization by acid alcohol, so it can be used before acidic stains (e.g., **Van Gieson**, trichrome).
- **Uses:**
    - Nuclei in **connective tissue stains** (e.g., **Weigert’s iron hematoxylin + Van Gieson** for collagen).
    - Some fungal and elastic fiber protocols.

### 5) Progressive vs regressive hematoxylins (exam‑style summary)

|Feature|Progressive hematoxylins|Regressive hematoxylins|
|---|---|---|
|Dye concentration|Lower|Higher|
|Staining pattern|Selective for nuclei; cytoplasm lightly stained|Intense staining of nuclei + cytoplasm|
|Control of intensity|By **time in stain**|By **differentiation** in acid alcohol|
|Differentiation step|Usually **not required**|**Required** (acid alcohol)|
|Examples|Mayer’s, Gill I/II, Carazzi’s|Harris’s, Ehrlich’s (often), Heidenhain’s|
|Typical use|IHC, cytology, some routine H&E|Routine H&E in histopathology, Pap stain|
