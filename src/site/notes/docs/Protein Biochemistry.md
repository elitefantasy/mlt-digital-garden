## Definition of Protein

A **protein** is a large, complex biomolecule (macromolecule) made up of one or more long chains of amino acids linked together by peptide bonds. 

- Proteins are **polymers of amino acids** joined by **peptide bonds**.
- There are **20 standard amino acids** commonly found in human protein
- Protein function depends on **three-dimensional structure** (primary → secondary → tertiary → quaternary

## Overview of Protein Functions

| Type of Protein                   | Function                                                                      | Examples                                                                |
| --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Enzymatic proteins**            | Catalyze (speed up) biochemical reactions in cells and digestion              | Amylase, lipase, pepsin, trypsin, lactase                               |
| **Structural proteins**           | Provide support, shape, and strength to cells and tissues                     | Collagen, keratin, actin, tubulin, elastin                              |
| **Transport proteins**            | Carry substances (e.g., oxygen, ions, nutrients) in blood or across membranes | Hemoglobin, albumin, carrier proteins, channel proteins                 |
| **Hormonal (messenger) proteins** | Act as chemical messengers to coordinate body activities                      | Insulin, glucagon, thyroxine                                            |
| **Defense (immune) proteins**     | Protect the body from pathogens and foreign substances                        | Immunoglobulins (antibodies), lysozyme, complement proteins             |
| **Contractile (motor) proteins**  | Enable movement and muscle contraction                                        | Actin, myosin                                                           |
| **Storage proteins**              | Store amino acids, ions, or nutrients for later use                           | Ovalbumin (egg white), ferritin (iron storage), legume storage proteins |
| **Receptor/signaling proteins**   | Receive and transmit signals across cell membranes                            | Hormone receptors, neurotransmitter receptors                           |
| **Gene regulatory proteins**      | Control gene expression by binding to DNA                                     | Transcription factors                                                   |

## Protein Folding

### Definition
**Protein folding** = process by which a linear polypeptide chain acquires its functional 3D (native) structure.
Driven by amino acid sequence; stabilized by H-bonds, hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonds, van der Waals forces, and disulfide bonds.

***

### 1. Diffusion–Collision Model

- **Idea:** Protein = series of **microdomains** (short segments that form secondary structure independently).
- **Steps:**
  1. Microdomains form **native-like secondary structures** early.
  2. Microdomains **diffuse** and **collide**.
  3. If collision is in native orientation → **coalesce** into stable intermediate.
  4. Repeated collisions → **full tertiary structure**.
- **Key points:**
  - Secondary structure forms **before** tertiary contacts.
  - Involves **molten globule** intermediate.
  - Typical in **multi-helix proteins** (e.g., apomyoglobin, protein G). 

***

### 2. Nucleation–Condensation Model

- **Idea:** Folding starts with a small **folding nucleus** (cluster of residues with native-like contacts).
- **Steps:**
  1. Few residues form a **weak nucleus** (secondary + tertiary).
  2. Rest of chain **condenses** around nucleus cooperatively. 
  3. Secondary and tertiary structures form **together**, not stepwise.
- **Key points:**
  - No stable secondary structure before tertiary contacts.
  - Transition state resembles native structure. 
  - Typical in **small globular proteins** (e.g., S6, CI2, SH3). 



> Many proteins show **mixed features**; models represent a **continuum**, not strict alternatives. 

***

### Chaperones (Molecular Chaperones)

#### Definition
**Chaperones** = proteins that assist other proteins in folding correctly without becoming part of the final structure. 

#### Functions
- Prevent **aggregation** of unfolded/misfolded proteins. 
- Provide **protected environment** for folding (e.g., GroEL cavity). 
- **Refold** misfolded proteins. 
- Assist **translocation** across membranes. 
- Upregulated during **stress** (heat shock proteins, HSPs). 

#### Major Chaperone Families

| Family | Examples | Key Features |
|--------|----------|--------------|
| **HSP70** | DnaK (bacteria), HSP70 (eukaryotes) | Binds hydrophobic segments; works with HSP40 (DnaJ); ATP-dependent cycle.  |
| **HSP60 (Chaperonins)** | GroEL/GroES, HSP60 | Barrel-shaped; isolated folding chamber; ATP-driven.  |
| **HSP90** | HSP90 | Folds/activates specific clients (receptors, kinases).  |
| **Small HSPs** | HSP27, α-crystallin | Prevent aggregation; ATP-independent.  |

#### Mechanism (Brief)

- **HSP70 cycle:**
  1. HSP40 delivers unfolded protein to HSP70. 
  2. HSP70 binds in **ADP state**.
  3. ATP binding → conformational change → **release** → protein folds. 

- **GroEL/GroES:**
  1. Unfolded protein enters GroEL cavity. 
  2. GroES caps cavity → **isolated chamber**.
  3. ATP hydrolysis → conformational change → **release** folded protein.

## Protein Digestion

Protein digestion begins in the stomach  and is completed in the small intestine by sequential action of gastric, pancreatic, and brush-border peptidases, yielding free amino acids, dipeptides, and tripeptides for absorption.


### 1. Mouth: Mechanical only (no proteolysis)

- **No proteolytic enzymes** are present in saliva; protein is **not chemically digested** here.
- **Teeth** mechanically break large food particles into smaller boluses; **tongue** mixes with saliva to form a swallowable mass
- Salivary **amylase** and **lipase** act on carbohydrates and fats only, not proteins

---

### 2. Stomach: Initial chemical digestion

#### Key conditions

- **Gastric HCl** denaturing proteins and activating pepsinogen
- Denaturation exposes peptide bonds, making them accessible to enzymes

#### Enzymes and actions
- **Pepsinogen** (chief cells) → **pepsin** (active endopeptidase) in acidic pH
- **Pepsin** hydrolyzes internal peptide bonds->  **large polypeptides and some oligopeptides**
- Accounts for only **~10–15%** of total protein digestion

#### Notes

- Gastric proteolysis is limited by the need for low pH; activity falls as chyme is neutralized in the duodenum.



### 3. Small Intestine: Major digestion and completion

#### A. Pancreatic phase (luminal digestion)

Pancreatic juice delivers **bicarbonate** (neutralizes acid) and **protease** into the duodenum

##### Activation cascade
1. **Enteropeptidase (enterokinase)** on brush border converts **trypsinogen → trypsin**.
2. **Trypsin** then activates:
    - **Chymotrypsinogen → chymotrypsin**
    - **Proelastase → elastase**
    - **Procarboxypeptidase A/B → carboxypeptidase A/B

##### Enzyme types and actions
- **Endopeptidases** (cut internal bonds):
    - **Trypsin**: cleaves after basic AA (Lys, Arg).
    - **Chymotrypsin**: cleaves after aromatic AA (Phe, Trp, Tyr).
    - **Elastase**: cleaves after small neutral AA (Ala, Gly, Ser).
- **Exopeptidases** (cut terminal bonds):
    - **Carboxypeptidase A/B**: remove C-terminal amino acids
Result: Proteins → **oligopeptides, dipeptides, tripeptides, and some free amino acids**.

#### B. Brush-border and intracellular phase (final digestion)
- **Brush-border peptidases** (aminopeptidases, dipeptidases, tripeptidases) on enterocyte microvilli further hydrolyze oligopeptides
- **Dipeptides and tripeptides** are absorbed via **PepT1** transporter into enterocytes
- **Intracellular peptidases** inside enterocytes hydrolyze di/tripeptides to **free amino acids**.

---

### End products available for absorption
- **Free amino acids** (major form)
- **Dipeptides and tripeptides** (absorbed, then hydrolyzed intracellularly)

## Proteinuria
Proteinuria is the abnormal excretion of protein in urine, defined as **>150 mg/24 h** (or >150 mg/L in a random sample); it indicates glomerular, tubular, overflow (overload), or post-renal pathology.


### 1. Types of Proteinuria (by Mechanism)

#### A. Glomerular Proteinuria
- **Mechanism**: due to Increased glomerular permeability → increased filtration of plasma proteins, especially **albumin** and larger proteins (IgG, transferrin)
- **Amount**: Often **>2 g/day**; **nephrotic-range** = **≥3.5 g/day**.
- **Key lab clue**: Urine **albumin : β2-microglobulin ratio >1000** (albumin predominates).
#### B. Tubular Proteinuria
- **Mechanism**: Normal glomerular filtration but **impaired proximal tubular reabsorption** of low molecular weight (LMW) proteins → LMW proteins appear in urine
- **Protein pattern**: 
    - **β2-microglobulin**, α1-microglobulin, retinol-binding protein (RBP), light chains, post-γ globulin
- **Amount**: Usually **<2 g/day** (often 0.5–1.5 g/day).
- **Key lab clue**: Urine **albumin : β2-microglobulin ratio <15** (LMW proteins predominate).
#### C. Overload (Overflow) Proteinuria
- **Mechanism**: Excessive production of **low molecular weight plasma proteins** that exceed tubular reabsorptive capacity → “overflow” into urine.
- **Protein pattern**: Abnormal LMW proteins, classically **immunoglobulin light chains (Bence Jones proteins)** in multiple myeloma; also myoglobin (rhabdomyolysis), hemoglobin (hemolysis)
#### D. Post-Renal Proteinuria
- **Mechanism**: Proteins added to urine **after the kidney**, from inflammation, infection, bleeding, or neoplasia in the lower urinary tract; may also include proteins secreted by tubules (e.g., Tamm–Horsfall protein)
- **Protein pattern**: Mixed; often associated with **WBCs, RBCs, bacteria, mucus**, and inflammatory exudate.

### 2. Determination of Proteinuria

#### A. Screening Tests
##### 1. Dipstick (Reagent Strip)
- **Principle**: Protein error of indicators (e.g., tetrabromophenol blue at pH ~3); color change from yellow → green → blue with increasing protein.
- **Detects**: Primarily **albumin**; poor sensitivity for globulins, light chains, Bence Jones proteins.
- **Sensitivity**: ~150 mg/L albumin; graded as trace, 1+, 2+, 3+, 4+ (≈10 mg/dL to >500 mg/dL).
- **False positives**: Alkaline urine, concentrated urine, hematuria, contamination (mucus, semen, WBCs), some disinfectants.
- **False negatives**: Dilute urine, non-albumin proteins (e.g., light chains).
##### 2. Sulfosalicylic Acid (SSA) Turbidity Test
- **Principle**: Acid precipitation of all proteins → turbidity proportional to protein concentration.
- **Method**: Mix urine supernatant with 3% SSA; assess turbidity visually or spectrophotometrically (~500 nm).
- **Detects**: **All proteins** (albumin + globulins + LMW proteins).
- **Interpretation (visual)**:
    - Clear = 0 mg/dL
    - Opalescent = 1–10 mg/dL
    - Print readable = 15–30 mg/dL
    - Only black lines visible = 40–100 mg/dL
    - No black lines = 150–400 mg/dL
    - Flocculent = >500 mg/dL
- **Use**: Confirm dipstick results; detect non-albumin proteinuria (e.g., Bence Jones).

---

#### B. Quantitative Tests
##### 1. 24-Hour Urine Protein
- **Gold standard** for total protein excretion.
- **Normal**: <150 mg/24 h
- **Microalbuminuria**: 30–300 mg/24 h (albumin only)
- **Overt proteinuria**: >300 mg/24 h
- **Nephrotic-range**: ≥3.5 g/24 h
##### 2. Spot Urine Protein/Creatinine Ratio (UPr/Cr) or Albumin/Creatinine Ratio (ACR)
- **Convenient alternative** to 24-h collection.
- **Normal UPr/Cr**: <0.2 mg/mg (adults & children >2 years)
- **Microalbuminuria (ACR)**: 30–299 mg/g creatinine
- **Overt albuminuria**: ≥300 mg/g creatinine

### 3. Microalbuminuria
- **Definition**: Persistent urinary albumin excretion **30–300 mg/24 h** (or 20–200 µg/min; or ACR 30–299 mg/g).
- **Normal**: <30 mg/24 h (ACR <30 mg/g)
- **Clinical significance**:
    - Earliest sign of **diabetic nephropathy** (type 1 & type 2)
    - Marker of **endothelial dysfunction**
    - Predictor of **CKD progression**, **cardiovascular disease**, and mortality in diabetes and hypertension
- **Detection**: Not detected by routine dipstick; requires **specific albumin assays** (immunoturbidimetric, ELISA, or ACR).


## CSF Protein
Normal CSF total protein in adults is ==**15–45 mg/dL** (0.15–0.45 g/L)==; values are higher in neonates and infants and increase slightly with age.


### 1. Sources of CSF Protein
- Most CSF protein comes from **plasma ultrafiltration** across the **blood–CSF/blood–brain barrier**.
- A small fraction is produced **intrathecally** (e.g., immunoglobulins in inflammation).
- CSF protein concentration reflects:
    - **Integrity of blood–brain barrier (BBB)**
    - **Rate of CSF formation/turnover**
    - **Intrathecal protein synthesis**

### 2. Causes of Increased CSF Protein
#### A. Marked elevation (>100–500 mg/dL or >1 g/L)
- **Acute bacterial meningitis** (often 100–500 mg/dL; can be higher)
- **Tuberculous meningitis** (almost always high)
- **Fungal meningitis** (e.g., cryptococcal)
- **Subarachnoid hemorrhage / cerebral hemorrhage** (blood contamination, RBC lysis)
- **Spinal cord tumors**
#### B. Mild to moderate elevation (≈50–100 mg/dL or 0.5–1.0 g/L)
- **Viral meningitis / encephalitis** (usually <100 mg/dL)[
- **Neurosyphilis** 
- **Multiple sclerosis** (may be normal or mildly raised; oligoclonal bands more specific)
- **Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS)** (elevated protein with normal/near-normal cells = albumino-cytological dissociation)
- **Brain tumors / CNS neoplasms**
- **Ischemic stroke / cerebral infarction**
- **Autoimmune / inflammatory CNS disease** (CNS vasculitis, autoimmune encephalitis)
- **Traumatic tap / blood contamination** (artificially increases protein)

### 3. Clinical Significance of CSF Protein Determination

CSF protein is a **sensitive but non-specific** marker of CNS pathology; interpreted with cell count, glucose, and clinical context.

#### A. Indicator of Blood–Brain Barrier (BBB) Dysfunction
- Elevated CSF protein suggests **increased BBB permeability** or **impaired CSF flow**.
- Used with **CSF:serum albumin quotient (Q<sub>Alb</sub>)** to assess BBB integrity:
    - High Q<sub>Alb</sub> → BBB damage (e.g., meningitis, vasculitis, tumors).
#### B. Differentiation of CNS Infections
- **Bacterial meningitis**:
    - Protein: **high** (often 100–500 mg/dL or more)
    - Glucose: **low**
    - Cells: **neutrophilic pleocytosis**
- **Viral meningitis/encephalitis**:
    - Protein: **normal to mildly elevated** (<100 mg/dL)
    - Glucose: **normal**
    - Cells: **lymphocytic pleocytosis**
- **TB/fungal meningitis**:
    - Protein: **markedly elevated**
    - Glucose: **low**
    - Cells: **lymphocytic**
#### C. Support for Specific Neurologic Syndromes
- **Guillain–Barré syndrome**:
    - **Albumino-cytological dissociation**: ↑ protein, normal/near-normal WBC.
    - Supports diagnosis in appropriate clinical setting (ascending paralysis).
- **Multiple sclerosis**:
    - Protein may be normal or mildly raised; more important are **oligoclonal IgG bands** and **IgG index**.
- **Spinal block / Froin’s syndrome**:
    - Very high protein, xanthochromia, spontaneous clotting → suggests **subarachnoid block**.
#### D. Detection of Hemorrhage and Tumors
- **Subarachnoid hemorrhage**:
    - Elevated protein + xanthochromia + RBCs in all tubes.
- **CNS tumors** (brain or spinal):
    - Often mild–moderate protein elevation; may be marked with spinal block or meningeal carcinomatosis.
#### E. Monitoring and Prognosis
- Serial CSF protein can help monitor response to therapy in **meningitis**, **TB meningitis**, and some **inflammatory CNS diseases**.
- Very high or rising protein may indicate **worsening inflammation**, **block**, or **poor CSF dynamics**.
