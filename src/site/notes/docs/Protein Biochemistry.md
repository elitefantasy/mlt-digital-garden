### Structural Levels of Proteins

> 1. **Primary:** Linear sequence of amino acids joined by peptide bonds.  
> 2. **Secondary:** Alpha-helices and Beta-pleated sheets held by hydrogen and disulfide bonds (e.g., Collagen).  
> 3. **Tertiary:** Three-dimensional spatial folding of a single polypeptide chain (e.g., Myoglobin).  
> 4. **Quaternary:** Complex assembly of multiple polypeptide sub-units (e.g., Hemoglobin with 4 polypeptide chains).

### Compositional Classification

> * **Simple Proteins:** Yield only amino acids upon hydrolysis (e.g., Albumin, Globulin).  
> * **Conjugated Proteins:** Protein joined to a non-protein prosthetic group (e.g., Hemoglobin \= Globin \+ Iron; Glycoprotein \= Protein \+ Carbohydrate).  
> * **Derived Proteins:** Intermediate products of protein hydrolysis (e.g., Peptides, Peptones).

### Major Plasma Proteins & Clinical Ranges

| Protein Component | Normal Concentration / Ratio | Primary Function / Notes   |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Total Plasma Protein** | 6.0 – 8.0 g/dL | Overall vascular oncotic pressure and buffering. |
| **Albumin** | Maintains \~80% colloidal osmotic pressure and blood viscosity. | Most abundant plasma protein. |
| **Globulins** | 2.0 – 3.5 g/dL | Includes 5 classes of Immunoglobulins acting as antibodies. |
| **Albumin to Globulin (A:G) Ratio** | 1.2 : 1 – 2.0 : 1 | Normally Albumin exceeds Globulin. |

### Specific Functional Proteins & Enzymes

> * **Lectin:** Protein with high affinity for carbohydrates; also present in milk to bind milk proteins.  
> * **Amylase:** Pancreatic enzyme catalyzing carbohydrate breakdown.  
> * **Protease / Pepsin:** Enzymes responsible for protein degradation and digestion.  
> * **Lactase:** Enzyme responsible for breaking down lactose/lipids.  
> * **Renin:** Enzyme component of the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS) for arterial blood pressure regulation.


# Study Notes: Proteins

---

### I. Introduction & Definition
*   **Word Origin:** The word protein is derived from the Greek word **"Proteios,"** which means primary or of prime importance [1].
*   **Definition:** Proteins are large, complex macromolecules (biomolecules) that serve as the fundamental building blocks of all living cells [2, 3]. 
*   **Cellular Composition:** They are the most abundant organic molecules in living systems, making up about **10% to 15% of a cell's total composition** [4, 5].
*   **Chemical Make-up:** Proteins consist primarily of **Carbon (C), Hydrogen (H), Oxygen (O), and Nitrogen (N)**. Some proteins may also contain Sulfur, Phosphorus, Iron, or Copper [6, 7].
*   **Distinguishing Feature:** The presence of **Nitrogen** is what chemically distinguishes proteins from carbohydrates and fats [8].

---

### II. Nutritional Value & Dietary Requirements
*   **Caloric Value:** 1 gram of protein yields **4 kilocalories (kcal)** of energy [9].
*   **Daily Requirement:**
    *   **Adults:** According to the WHO, the standard requirement is approximately **0.8 to 1.0 grams per kilogram of body weight per day** [9, 10].
    *   **Pregnant/Lactating Women:** Require an additional amount (approx. 1.1 to 1.3 extra grams per day) to support fetal development and milk production [10].
    *   **Infants:** Require much higher amounts proportionally (e.g., 1.16 to 1.69 g/kg/day) due to rapid cellular growth [10].

---

### III. The Building Blocks: Amino Acids
Proteins are polymers composed of monomer units called **amino acids** [11].
*   **Amino Acid Structure:** Every amino acid contains a central alpha-carbon bonded to an **amino group** (basic), a **carboxyl group** (acidic), a hydrogen atom, and a variable **"R" group (side chain)**. The R-group dictates the specific properties of each amino acid [12-14].
*   **Peptide Bonds:** Amino acids link together via **peptide bonds**. This bond forms when the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of another, releasing a molecule of water (a condensation reaction) [15]. Long chains of these are called **polypeptides** [16].
*   There are **20 standard amino acids** required to build human proteins [14]. They are categorized based on dietary necessity:

1. Essential Amino Acids (9 total):
These cannot be synthesized by the body and **must be obtained from the diet** [17]. 
*   *Mnemonic to remember (PVT TIM HILL):* **P**henylalanine, **V**aline, **T**hreonine, **T**ryptophan, **I**soleucine, **M**ethionine, **H**istidine, **L**eucine, and **L**ysine [18].

2. Non-Essential Amino Acids (11 total):
These can be naturally synthesized by the body from other compounds, so dietary intake is not strictly mandatory [17]. *(Note: Some like Arginine and Histidine are considered "semi-essential" or conditional, meaning they are required from the diet during rapid growth phases like infancy or illness)* [18].

---

### IV. Sources of Protein
*   **Complete Proteins:** These contain all **9 essential amino acids** in adequate amounts. They are typically **animal sources** (e.g., egg, milk, meat, fish, cheese). *Note: Egg protein is considered the "reference protein" due to its high biological value and digestibility.* There are also rare plant exceptions, such as **soy and quinoa**, which are complete proteins [19].
*   **Incomplete Proteins:** These lack one or more essential amino acids and are generally **plant sources** (e.g., cereals, legumes, pulses). For instance, cereals are often deficient in Lysine, while pulses are deficient in Methionine. Combining them (like eating rice and lentils together) yields a complete amino acid profile [20, 21].

---

### V. Structural Levels of Proteins
A functional protein is not just a straight chain; its function is entirely dependent on its 3D folded shape [22, 23]. Protein structure exists in four hierarchical levels [24]:

1.  **Primary (1°) Structure:** 
    *   The linear sequence and specific order of amino acids held together by covalent **peptide bonds** [25, 26]. 
    *   This sequence acts as a cryptic code that entirely dictates how the protein will fold and function [27].
2.  **Secondary (2°) Structure:** 
    *   The polypeptide chain twists or folds due to **hydrogen bonding** between the amino and carboxyl groups of the peptide backbone [28, 29].
    *   *Common forms:* **Alpha-helix** (coiled, e.g., keratin) and **Beta-pleated sheet** (folded parallel/anti-parallel strands, e.g., silk fibroin) [30, 31].
3.  **Tertiary (3°) Structure:** 
    *   The overall **3D globular or fibrous shape** of a single polypeptide chain [32].
    *   It is driven by interactions between the variable **R-groups** (side chains). Bonds involved include hydrophobic interactions, ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, and disulfide bridges [33, 34].
4.  **Quaternary (4°) Structure:** 
    *   Found only in complex proteins composed of **two or more polypeptide chains (subunits)** that assemble together [35].
    *   *Example:* **Hemoglobin** consists of 4 subunits (two alpha chains and two beta chains), and **Insulin** consists of 2 chains [36].

*   *Note on Folding:* Specialized barrel-shaped proteins called **chaperonins** assist other newly formed proteins in achieving their correct folded shape [2, 37]. Extreme heat or abnormal pH can **denature** a protein, destroying its 3D shape and function [38].

---

### VI. Classification of Proteins
Proteins can be further classified based on their composition and shape:

**A. Based on Composition:**
1.  **Simple Proteins:** Composed exclusively of amino acids (e.g., Albumin, Globulin) [39].
2.  **Conjugated Proteins:** Composed of a protein bound to a non-protein prosthetic group (e.g., Hemoglobin = globin protein + heme iron; Lipoprotein = protein + lipid) [40].
3.  **Derived Proteins:** Formed when native proteins are broken down by acids, heat, or digestive enzymes (e.g., Peptones, smaller polypeptides) [40].

**B. Based on Shape:**
1.  **Fibrous Proteins:** Long, thread-like structures that are **insoluble in water** and highly resistant to enzymatic breakdown. They provide structure and support (e.g., Keratin in hair/nails, Collagen in connective tissue, Actin/Myosin in muscles) [41].
2.  **Globular Proteins:** Oval or spherical in shape, tightly coiled, and **soluble in water**. They usually perform active metabolic functions (e.g., Albumin, Insulin, Hemoglobin) [42].

---

### VII. Key Biological Functions
Proteins are involved in nearly every biological process [2]:
*   **Structural Support:** Collagen rebuilds connective tissues and heals wounds; keratin builds hair and nails [43].
*   **Enzymatic Activity:** Most biological catalysts (enzymes) are proteins that speed up metabolic reactions (e.g., amylase, lactase) [44].
*   **Transport:** Hemoglobin transports oxygen in the blood; albumin transports fatty acids [44].
*   **Immune Defense:** Antibodies (immunoglobulins) that fight infections are made of proteins [44].
*   **Movement & Contraction:** Actin and myosin proteins drive muscle contraction [44].
*   **Hormonal Regulation:** Many hormones are proteins that regulate bodily systems (e.g., insulin regulates blood sugar) [45].
