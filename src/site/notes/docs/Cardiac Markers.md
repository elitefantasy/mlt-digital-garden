# Cardiac Biomarkers and Myocardial Assessment

## 1. Introduction to Cardiac Biomarkers and Clinical Pathophysiology

These hormones increase heart rate and contraction strength, potentially exacerbating myocardial stress. Under such stress, we must differentiate between **Myocardial Ischemia** (hypoxia-driven cellular stress without cell death) and **Myocardial Infarction (MI)** (irreversible necrosis resulting from prolonged blood flow cessation).

As we discuss in our biochemistry sessions, these lipids are characterized by their stability, being exceptionally rich in carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen bonds. When these stable plaques rupture and occlude an artery, or when a **Pulmonary Embolism** causes acute right-sided heart strain, the resulting cellular damage leads to a predictable "staircase" of biomarker release. The specific kinetics of these markers—their onset, peak, and clearance—dictate their diagnostic window and ultimate clinical value.

## 2. The Five Pillars of Cardiac Biomarkers: Profiling and Kinetics

### 2.1. Myoglobin: The Early Responder

Myoglobin is a heme-containing protein found in muscle tissue that serves as the "first responder" to cardiac injury. Due to its small molecular size, its appearance in the blood follows a rapid "step" pattern immediately following muscle damage.

- **Kinetics:**
    - **Onset:** 30 minutes – 4 hours
    - **Peak:** 6 – 10 hours
    - **Normalization:** Within 24 hours

It is vital to remember the clinical trade-off: myoglobin possesses **high sensitivity** for early rule-out protocols, but it suffers from **poor specificity**. Because it is also present in skeletal muscle, any trauma to those tissues will cause an elevation. Its rapid clearance from the system means it must always be followed by more specific markers.

### 2.2. Creatine Kinase (CK) and CK-MB: The Specificity Standard

Creatine Kinase (CK) is an enzyme essential for energy metabolism. By analyzing its isoenzyme distribution, we can pinpoint the source of tissue damage with greater accuracy.

|   |   |   |   |
|---|---|---|---|
|Isoenzyme|Name|Primary Tissue Source|Standard Laboratory Reference|
|**CK-1**|CK-BB|Brain|-|
|**CK-2**|**CK-MB**|**Heart**|**0-25 U/L**|
|**CK-3**|CK-MM|Skeletal Muscle|-|

- **Total CK Normal Ranges (Standard Laboratory Reference):**
    - Male: 15-100 U/L
    - Female: 10-80 U/L

The diagnostic "So What?" for CK-MB is clear: if the fraction exceeds 5% of total CK, myocardial injury is highly likely. 

Its kinetics follow a distinct curve: 
- **Onset** (3-6 hours), 
- **Peak** (12-24 hours), 
- **Normalization** (36-72 hours). 

The area under this peak correlates to the total infarct size. Furthermore, because CK-MB clears within three days, a secondary rise is a definitive indicator of a **re-infarction** event.

### 2.3. Cardiac Troponins (cTnI and cTnT): The Gold Standard

In modern Clinical Pathology, Troponins have surpassed enzymes as the "Gold Standard." These are not enzymes, but structural regulatory proteins of the myofibril. The troponin complex includes:

- **TnC:** Calcium-binding subunit.
- **TnI:** Inhibitory subunit; highly cardiac-specific.
- **TnT:** Tropomyosin-binding subunit; also cardiac-specific.

**Kinetics and Reference Ranges (Standard Laboratory Reference):**

- **cTnI:** Onset (3-4 hrs), Peak (14-24 hrs), Duration (3-5 days). Range: **0-0.49 ng/mL**.
- **cTnT:** Onset (4-10 hrs), Peak (72 hrs), Duration (10-14 days). Range: **0-0.01 ng/mL**.

Due to its prolonged presence in the circulation, cTnT acts as a "late marker," essential for diagnosing patients who present **several days post-event.**

### 2.4. Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH): The Legacy Marker

LDH is a tetrameric enzyme once central to late-stage MI diagnosis. We focus on the relationship between LDH-1 and LDH-2, which are most prevalent in the heart and Red Blood Cells (RBCs).

- **The "Flipped Pattern":** Normally, LDH-2 > LDH-1. In the event of an MI, this ratio reverses, and **LDH-1 > LDH-2**.
- **Kinetics:** Onset (6-12 hrs), Peak (30-48 hrs), Normalization (2-6 days).
- **Reference Range (Standard Laboratory Reference):** 100-200 or 0-250 U/L.

**CRITICAL HEMOLYSIS WARNING:** As medical laboratory scientists, you must be vigilant. LDH is **100 times more concentrated in RBCs** than in plasma. Even minor hemolysis in a sample will cause a massive, artificial spike in results, leading to a clinically dangerous false-positive.

### 2.5. Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST/SGOT): The Non-Specific Indicator

AST is an enzyme involved in transamination. While historically part of the "cardiac profile," its diagnostic weight has shifted.

- **Kinetics:** Onset (5-8 hrs), Peak (24-48 hrs), Normalization (3-6 days).
- **Reference Range (Standard Laboratory Reference):** 8-40 U/L.

It is important to note that AST is found extensively in the **liver and skeletal muscle**, making it a secondary, non-specific marker in the context of the heart. In modern practice, we rely on the superior specificity of Troponins and CK-MB.

## 4. Endocrine Functions of the Heart: ANF

The heart is more than a mechanical pump; it is an endocrine organ that secretes hormones to maintain cardiovascular homeostasis and respond to volume overload.

- **Atrial Natriuretic Factor (ANF):** Also known as **ANP**, this peptide hormone is synthesized and secreted by the cells of the atrial wall.
- **Mechanism of Action:** When the atria are stretched—typically due to increased blood volume or high blood pressure—ANF is released into the bloodstream. It acts as a powerful vasodilator, relaxing the blood vessels to increase their diameter.
- **Clinical Result:** By inducing **vasodilation**, ANF effectively reduces systemic blood pressure, thereby protecting the heart from the stress of excessive volume.
