Multicellular parasites.
- Highly evolved organisms with complex body systems.
- Bilateral symmetry.
- Have excretory and nervous systems.
- Digestive system:
	- Complete in roundworms.
	- Incomplete in flukes.
	- Absent in tapeworms.

**Reproduction**
1. **Dioecious:** Separate male and female sexes.
   - Example: Nematodes.
1. **Monoecious:** Male and female reproductive organs occur in the same organism.
   - Examples: Cestodes and trematodes.

## Ancylostoma

**Ancylostoma duodenale** is the _Old World hookworm_, a small-intestinal nematode and an important cause of **iron-deficiency anemia**. Its infective stage is the **filariform (L3) larva**, which enters humans through intact skin.
![Pasted image 20261124185304.png\|436](/img/user/Attachments/Pasted%20image%2020261124185304.png)

### Key facts

| Feature             | Ancylostoma duodenale                       |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Common name         | Old World hookworm                          |
| Type                | Intestinal nematode (roundworm)             |
| Definitive host     | Humans                                      |
| Habitat             | Small intestine, especially jejunum         |
| Infective stage     | Filariform larva (L3)                       |
| Mode of infection   | Skin penetration, usually bare feet         |
| Main disease effect | Chronic blood loss → iron-deficiency anemia |
| Diagnostic stage    | Oval egg with 4 blastomeres in stool        |

### Morphology

- Adult worms are small, greyish-white, cylindrical nematodes; the anterior end bends dorsally, giving the name **hookworm**. 
- The buccal capsule has **6 teeth**: 4 hook-like teeth ventrally and 2 knob-like teeth dorsally. 
- Adults attach to the intestinal mucosa using their mouth parts and suck blood. 

### Male vs female

|Feature|Male|Female|
|---|---|---|
|Size|Smaller: 5–11 mm × 0.4 mm|Larger: 9–13 mm × 0.6 mm  |
|Posterior end|Expanded **copulatory bursa**|Tapering; no bursa  |
|Genital opening|Posterior, opens with cloaca|At junction of posterior and middle thirds  |

### Egg: most important diagnosis

- **Oval, colorless, non-bile-stained** egg. 
- Size: approximately **65 µm × 40 µm**. 
- Thin, transparent **hyaline shell**. 
- Contains a segmented ovum with **4 blastomeres**; a clear space is present between the shell and ovum. 
- Eggs **float in saturated salt solution**. 
- One female lays about **25,000–30,000 eggs/day**. 
- Eggs appear in feces about **4–7 weeks after infection**. 

**Exam line:** _Hookworm egg = oval, thin-shelled, colorless, 4–8 blastomeres, and floats in saturated salt solution._

### Life cycle

1. **Adult worms** live in the small intestine and attach to mucosa. 
2. Female releases eggs, which pass in **feces**; eggs are initially **non-infective**. 
3. In warm, moist soil, eggs hatch into **rhabditiform larvae (L1)** within 24–48 hours. 
4. Larvae molt twice, on days 3 and 5, becoming **filariform larvae (L3)**. 
5. **L3 is infective** and penetrates unbroken skin, commonly toes, dorsum of foot, or medial sole. 
6. Larvae enter circulation, reach the lungs, then migrate up the respiratory tract and are swallowed. 
7. In the small intestine, they undergo the fourth molt and mature into adults. 
8. Adults become sexually mature after about **6 weeks**, fertilization occurs, and egg-laying restarts the cycle. 

**Memory aid:** **Egg → L1 rhabditiform → L3 filariform → skin → lungs → swallowed → small intestine → adult → eggs.**

### Pathogenesis

- Infection begins when the **filariform larva penetrates skin**. 
- Larval migration can affect skin and lungs. 
- Adult worms attach to the small-intestinal mucosa and cause ongoing blood loss; _A. duodenale_ sucks about **0.2 mL blood/day per worm**. 
- Worms may migrate, leaving small bleeding points in the mucosa. 
- Chronic blood loss causes **microcytic, hypochromic iron-deficiency anemia**. 

### Clinical features

Skin: ground itch
- Occurs at the site where L3 larvae enter the skin. 
- Intense itching and burning, followed by erythema and edema. 
- May develop papular and vesicular eruptions. 
- Usually resolves within **1–2 weeks**. 

Lungs: larval migration
- Cough, dyspnea, bronchitis, or bronchopneumonia may occur. 
- **Marked eosinophilia** is a key finding. 

Intestine: main disease
- Adult worms cause intestinal blood loss and anemia. 
- Major presentation: **iron-deficiency anemia**, often microcytic and hypochromic. 
- Severe or chronic infection may lead to protein loss and malabsorption. 

### Laboratory diagnosis

Direct methods
- **Stool examination** for characteristic hookworm eggs is the main diagnostic method. 
- **Stool concentration techniques** are needed for light infections. 
- If stool remains at room temperature for more than 24 hours, larvae may hatch from eggs. 

Indirect findings
- CBC: **microcytic hypochromic anemia** and **eosinophilia**. 
- Stool may show **occult blood** and **Charcot–Leyden crystals(released when eosinophils or basophils break down)**. 

### Treatment
- **Mebendazole** 
- **Albendazole** 
- **Pyrantel pamoate** 
- Mild anemia: oral iron therapy. 
- Severe infection with protein loss or malabsorption: nutritional support plus oral iron replacement. 

### Prevention
- Proper disposal of feces. 
- Improved personal hygiene. 
- Wearing footwear to prevent skin penetration by L3 larvae. 
- Improved nutrition and dietary iron. 
- Treatment of infected individuals to reduce transmission. 

### High-yield exam points

- **Infective stage:** Filariform larva, L3. 
- **Route of infection:** Percutaneous penetration of skin. 
- **Habitat:** Small intestine, particularly jejunum. 
- **Diagnostic stage:** Egg in stool. 
- **Major complication:** Iron-deficiency anemia. 
- **Blood loss:** About 0.2 mL/day per adult _A. duodenale_. 
- **Eggs/day:** 25,000–30,000. 
- **Eggs appear in stool:** 4–7 weeks after infection. 
- **Anemia type:** Microcytic, hypochromic. 
- **Skin lesion:** Ground itch / Ancylostoma dermatitis. 
- **Lung phase:** Cough, dyspnea, eosinophilia.

## Strongyloide stercoralis
### 1\. General, Habitat & Distribution
-   **Size Fact:** **Smallest nematode** known to cause human infection.
-   **Habitat:** Small intestine (**duodenum and upper jejunum**), embedded in the intestinal mucosa.
-   **Distribution:** Warm moist tropics and temperate regions (common in Brazil, Colombia, Myanmar, Thailand, Vietnam, Malaysia, Philippines).
-   **Related Species:** _S. fuelleborni_ (monkeys; humans in Zaire, Zambia, Papua New Guinea) and _Trichostrongylus_ spp. (sheep/goats; asymptomatic human cases in Africa and Asia including India).
-   **Hosts:** Humans (natural host); dogs and cats can harbour morphologically indistinguishable strains.
-   **Infective Stage:** Third-stage (**L3**) **filariform larva**.
-   **Mode of Infection:** Skin penetration by L3 filariform larva when walking barefoot on contaminated soil (or via **autoinfection**)
### 2\. Morphology (High-Yield)

-   **Adult Female (Parasitic):**
    -   Thin, transparent, ~**2.5 mm** long × **0.05 mm** wide.
    -   Cylindrical oesophagus , intestine 
    -   Vulva ; paired uteri contain **ovoid, thin-walled, transparent eggs**
    -   **Ovoviviparous**; lifespan of individual worm is **3–4 months**, but infection can persist **30–40 years** due to autoinfection.(kisi next host ya outside environment ki need nhi he same host ko waapis reinfect karna)
-   **Adult Male:**
    -   Shorter and broader than female: **0.6–1 mm** long ×\\times× **40–50 μm\\mu\\text{m}μm** wide.
    -   Copulatory spicules lie beside gubernaculum.
    -   **Not seen in human infections** (lacks penetrating power to invade intestinal wall; eliminated after oviposition begins).
-   **Egg:**
    -   Oval, 8–10 eggs per uterus in a single anteroposterior row.
    -   Hatches **immediately** into rhabditiform larva in mucosa/intestine — **larva (not egg)** is passed in stool and detected on stool examination.
-   **Larvae:**
    -   **Rhabditiform Larva (L1):** long; short muscular **double-bulb oesophagus**; most common stage in faeces.
    -   **Filariform Larva (L3):** long slender oesophagus of uniform width and **notched/forked tail**; infective stage.

### 3\. Life Cycle (Parasitic, Free-Living & Autoinfection)
-   Unique among human nematodes due to having both a **parasitic cycle** and a **free-living soil cycle** (persists in moist soil feeding on bacteria across generations).
-   **Parasitic Cycle:**
    1.  Infective **L3 filariform larvae** penetrate skin (barefoot contact) → venous circulation → right heart → lungs.
    2.  Break out of pulmonary capillaries into alveoli → ascend respiratory tract to pharynx → swallowed → reach small intestine.
    3.  Mature into adult worms in **15–20 days**; females burrow into mucosa and lay eggs that hatch immediately into **L1 rhabditiform larvae**.
    4.  L1 larvae enter bowel lumen and are excreted in faeces.
-   **Soil Development (Two Pathways):**
    -   **Direct development:** L1 in soil moults twice into infective L3 filariform larva ( L1→ 1 L3).
    -   **Indirect development (free-living phase):** L1 in moist soil develops into free-living males and females → mate and lay eggs → hatch into next-generation L1 larvae → either repeat free-living cycle or form infective L3 larvae.
-   **Autoinfection:**
    -   L1 larvae metamorphose into filariform larvae during gut passage.
    -   Penetrate **colonic mucosa** (internal reinfection) or **perianal/perineal skin** without soil contact.
### 4\. Pathogenicity, Clinical Features & Hyperinfection
-   **General Course:** <span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)">ASYMPTOMATIC</span> (only blood eosinophilia and stool larvae seen), but can be severe or fatal in **immunocompromised / cellular immune-defective** hosts.
-   **Cutaneous:**
    -   Dermatitis with erythema and itching 
    -   Chronic pruritus and urticaria around perianal skin and buttocks
-   **Pulmonary:** Alveolar/bronchiolar microhaemorrhages, bronchopneumonia, chronic bronchitis, asthma-like symptoms; **larvae may be found in sputum**.
-   **Intestinal:** Peptic ulcer-like or malabsorption-like symptoms, mucus diarrhoea, honeycombed mucosa with sloughing and dysenteric stools in heavy infection, **protein-losing enteropathy**, paralytic ileus.
-   **Hyperinfection:** Extensive internal reinfection in debilitated or cellular-immunodeficient individuals→ massive worm/larval load across intestines, lungs, and other organs/tissues.
### 5\. Laboratory Diagnosis
-   **Microscopy:**
    -   **Direct wet mount of fresh stool:** Key specific test to spot **rhabditiform larvae** (differentiate from hookworm larvae in stale stool).
    -   **Concentration methods:** **Formol-ether** concentration or **Baermann’s funnel gauze method** (exploits active larval migration out of faeces on wire mesh with gauze).
    -   **Other specimens:** Sputum, duodenal aspirates, jejunal biopsies.
-   **Stool Culture:**
    -   **Agar plate culture** or **charcoal culture** (useful when larvae are scanty; free-living larvae and adults multiply and appear in **7–10 days**).
-   **Serology:**
    -   **ELISA** (~**95%** sensitivity) when microscopy is negative; also complement fixation and indirect haemagglutination.
    -   _Limitations:_ Limited availability of larval antigens; **cross-reactions** with other helminths.
-   **Haematology & Imaging:**
    -   **Peripheral eosinophilia** (**\>500/cu mm** blood) — constant finding, though may be **absent in severe hyperinfection**.
    -   Elevated **total serum IgE** in >50% of patients.
    -   Characteristic intestinal/pulmonary **radiological** findings.
### 6\. Treatment & Prophylaxis
-   **Treatment (treat all cases, symptomatic or asymptomatic):**
    -   **Ivermectin:** **200 μg/kg** daily for **2 days** (more effective than albendazole).
    -   **Disseminated strongyloidiasis:** Extend ivermectin for at least **5–7 days**.
    -   **Albendazole:** Alternative (400 mg daily for 3 days).
-   **Prophylaxis:** Prevent soil contamination with faeces, avoid contact with infective soil and contaminated surface water, and treat infected individuals

## Enterobius vermicularis (Pinworm)

### 1. General Overview & Habitat
* **Common Name:** Pinworm  (formerly *Oxyuris vermicularis*
* **Distribution:** Worldwide; more common in temperate and developed countries; predominantly affects children
* **Habitat:** Caecum (lower right quadrant / right iliac fossa), appendix, and ascending colon

### 2. Morphology
Adult Worm
* **Appearance:** Small, white, spindle-shaped with tapered ends (thread-like)
* **Key Features:** Cervical alae near mouth; double-bulb esophagus

| Feature | Female Worm | Male Worm |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Size** | $8\text{--}13\text{ mm} \times 0.3\text{--}0.5\text{ mm}$ | $2\text{--}5\text{ mm}$ (smaller) |
| **Posterior End** | Long, straight, pointed tail | Curved posterior end with copulatory spicule |
| **Lifespan** | $5\text{--}12\text{ weeks}$ | $7\text{--}8\text{ weeks}$ (dies after fertilization) |

Egg
* **Shape & Size:** Oval, **plano-convex (D-shaped)**; $50\text{--}60\ \mu\text{m} \times 20\text{--}30\ \mu\text{m}$
* **Appearance:** Transparent, colourless, **not bile-stained**, <span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)">double-layered sticky shell</span>
* **Contents:** Contains a coiled tadpole-like larva
* **Infectivity & Output:** Becomes infective in **$4\text{--}6\text{ hours}$**; survives up to 2 weeks in moist conditions; gravid female lays $5,000\text{--}17,000\text{ eggs}$

---

### 3. Life Cycle
* **Host:** Monoxenous (single host: humans only; no intermediate host)
* **Infective Stage:** Embryonated egg containing larva
* **Diagnostic Stage:** Egg on perianal skin (or adult worm in faeces)
* **Mode of Transmission:** Ingestion of embryonated eggs via contaminated hands/fingers, fomites, autoinfection, or retroinfection
* **Development:**
	1. Ingested eggs hatch in the small intestine (ileum)
	2. Larvae migrate to the caecum and mature into adults in **$2\text{ weeks} - 2\text{ months}$**
	3. Fertilized females migrate to the rectum at night, exit the anus, and deposit sticky eggs on perianal skin before dying
* **Special Modes of Reinfection:**
	* **Autoinfection:** Scratching perianal skin $\rightarrow$ eggs trapped under fingernails $\rightarrow$ hand-to-mouth ingestion
	* **Retroinfection:** Eggs hatch on perianal skin $\rightarrow$ larvae migrate backward through the anus into the colon to mature
### 4. Pathogenesis & Clinical Features (Enterobiasis)
* **Primary Symptom:** Intense nocturnal perianal itching (*pruritus ani*) caused by nocturnal migration and oviposition of gravid females
* **Secondary Effects:** Excoriation/skin irritation, sleep disturbance, and nocturnal enuresis (bedwetting) in children
* **Complications:**
	* Migration into female genital tract $\rightarrow$ vulvovaginitis, discharge, urinary tract infections (UTIs), or rare cervicitis/salpingitis/peritonitis
	* Invasion of appendix $\rightarrow$ associated with acute appendicitis
### 5. Laboratory Diagnosis
* **Note on Stool Exam:** Routine stool microscopy is **not useful** (eggs are laid on perianal skin, not in faeces; found in $<5\%$ of stool samples)
* **Specimen Collection Timing:** Early morning, **before bathing or defaecation**
* **Diagnostic Techniques:**
	1. **NIH Swab Technique:** Glass rod tipped with cellophane/adhesive film held by a rubber band, pressed against perianal folds, and examined microscopically
	2. **Adhesive / Scotch Tape Test:** Clear cellulose tape pressed onto perianal skin and mounted on a glass slide for microscopy
	3. **Other:** Scrapings from under fingernails (eggs) or visual detection of adult females on perianal skin/faeces

---

### 6. Treatment & Prevention
* **Drugs of Choice:**
	* **Albendazole** (single dose)[cite: 19]
	* **Mebendazole** (single dose)[cite: 19]
	* **Pyrantel pamoate** (single dose)[cite: 19]
	* **Piperazine** (given for ~1 week)[cite: 19]
* **Key Protocol:**
	* **Repeat dose after 2 weeks** to eliminate worms hatched from residual eggs[cite: 19].
	* **Treat all household members simultaneously** (even if asymptomatic)[cite: 19].
* **Prophylaxis:** Strict handwashing (before meals, after toilet), keeping fingernails short, daily morning bathing, and regular washing/changing of nightwear and bed linens[cite: 20].
