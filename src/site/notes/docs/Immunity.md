# Immunity

**Immunity** is the ability of the body to **recognize, resist, and destroy foreign substances (antigens)** such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites, and their toxins.

## Types of Immunity

Immunity is mainly divided into **two types:**

### 1. Innate Immunity

It is the **natural, non-specific defense** present from birth.

**Features:**
- Present from birth
- Acts rapidly
- Non-specific
- Has no immunological memory
    
**Components:**
- **Physical barriers:** skin, mucous membrane
- **Chemical barriers:** gastric acid, lysozyme, antimicrobial substances
- **Cellular defense:** neutrophils, macrophages, NK cells
- **Humoral factors:** complement, interferons
- **Inflammation and fever**

**Mechanism**
- immediate recognition of pathogens
- no antigen spicificity
- uses pattern recognition receptors
    

### 2. Acquired (Adaptive) Immunity

It develops **after exposure to an antigen** and is highly specific.

**Features:**
- Antigen-specific
- Develops after exposure
- Has immunological memory
- Mainly involves **B and T lymphocytes**

**Mechanism**
- antigen presentation via APCs
- activation of B and T lymphocyte
- formation of memory cells

It is further divided into:

#### A. Humoral Immunity
- Mainly mediated by **B lymphocytes**
- B cells differentiate into **plasma cells**
- Plasma cells produce **antibodies (immunoglobulins)**
- Important against extracellular bacteria and toxins
    
#### B. Cell-Mediated Immunity

# Phagocyte Phagocytosis

## 1. Phagocyte

A **phagocyte** is a cell of the immune system that can **engulf and destroy microorganisms, foreign particles, and dead or damaged cells** by a process called **phagocytosis**.

### Important phagocytes
- **Neutrophils** – major phagocytes in acute inflammation
- **Monocytes** – circulate in blood and become macrophages in tissues
- **Macrophages** – long-lived tissue phagocytes
- **Dendritic cells** – can phagocytose and are important in antigen presentation

---

## 2. Phagocytosis

**Phagocytosis** is the process by which a phagocytic cell **engulfs a solid particle or microorganism and destroys it intracellularly**.

It is an important mechanism of **innate immunity**.

### Simple sequence

**Chemotaxis → Adherence → Ingestion → Phagosome formation → Phagolysosome formation → Digestion → Residual body → Exocytosis**

---

## 3. Functions of Phagocytes

1. Destruction of microorganisms
Phagocytes engulf and destroy:
- Bacteria
- Fungi
- Parasites
- Cellular debris
    

1. Removal of dead cells
They remove **dead, damaged, or aged cells** from tissues.

2. Removal of foreign particles
They help eliminate foreign materials such as tissue debris and particulate matter.

3. Defense against infection
They provide an important **first-line cellular defense** against invading microorganisms.

4. Antigen presentation
**Macrophages and dendritic cells** process antigens and present them to **T lymphocytes**, helping initiate adaptive immunity.
5. Release of inflammatory mediators
Phagocytes release substances such as **cytokines and chemokines**, which help recruit and activate other immune cells.

---

## 4. Steps of Phagocytosis

Step 1 – Chemotaxis
The phagocyte is **attracted toward the site of infection or tissue injury** by chemical substances.
Examples:
- Bacterial products
- Complement components, especially **C5a**
- Chemokines
**Result:** Phagocyte moves toward the microorganism.

---

Step 2 – Adherence / Attachment
The phagocyte **attaches to the surface of the microorganism**
Attachment can occur directly or can be enhanced by **opsonization**.
Important opsonins:
- **IgG**
- **C3b**
**Opsonization → easier attachment → easier phagocytosis**

---

Step 3 – Ingestion
The phagocyte extends **pseudopodia** around the microorganism.
The pseudopodia surround and engulf the particle.

---
Step 4 – Formation of Phagosome
The engulfed microorganism becomes enclosed within a membrane-bound vesicle called the **phagosome**.
**Phagosome = vesicle containing engulfed microorganism**

---
Step 5 – Formation of Phagolysosome
The phagosome fuses with a **lysosome**.
This forms a:
**Phagosome + Lysosome → Phagolysosome**
The phagolysosome contains digestive enzymes and antimicrobial substances.

---
Step 6 – Digestion and Killing
The microorganism is destroyed by:
Oxygen-dependent mechanisms
- Reactive oxygen species (ROS)
- Hydrogen peroxide
- Hypochlorous acid (**HOCl**) produced by myeloperoxidase

Oxygen-independent mechanisms
- Lysosomal enzymes
- Proteases
- Lysozyme
- Antimicrobial proteins

The microorganism is broken down into smaller components.

---

Step 7 – Formation of Residual Body
Material that **cannot be digested** remains inside the phagocyte as a **residual body**.

---

Step 8 – Discharge / Exocytosis
The indigestible waste material is released outside the cell by **exocytosis**.

---

### ⭐ Exam flowchart
**Microorganism**  
↓  
**Chemotaxis**  
↓  
**Adherence / Opsonization**  
↓  
**Ingestion by pseudopodia**  
↓  
**Phagosome formation**  
↓  
**Fusion with lysosome**  
↓  
**Phagolysosome**  
↓  
**Killing & digestion**  
↓  
**Residual body formation**  
↓  
**Exocytosis of waste**

# Antigens
An **antigen** is a substance that can be **specifically recognized and bound by an antibody, B-cell receptor (BCR), or T-cell receptor (TCR)**.

**Examples:** proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, toxins, and parts of microorganisms.

> **Antigen = substance recognized by the immune system.**

---

## Epitope
An **epitope** is the **specific portion of an antigen that is recognized and bound by an antibody or lymphocyte receptor**.

It is also called the **antigenic determinant**.

**Example:**
**Antigen → contains several epitopes → antibodies recognize individual epitopes**

📌 One antigen can have **multiple epitopes**.

---

## Paratope
A **paratope** is the **specific antigen-binding site on an antibody** that binds to an epitope.

### Remember:

**Epitope → on antigen**  
**Paratope → on antibody**

The epitope and paratope interact through **non-covalent forces** such as hydrogen bonds, electrostatic forces, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals forces.

---

## Antigenicity

**Antigenicity** is the ability of a substance to **specifically bind with the products of an immune response**, such as antibodies or specific lymphocyte receptors.

> **Antigenicity = ability to bind specifically with antibody/BCR/TCR.**

📌 A substance may have antigenicity **without being capable of inducing an immune response**.

---

## Immunogenicity
**Immunogenicity** is the ability of a substance to **induce or stimulate an immune response**.

> **Immunogenicity = ability to produce an immune response.**

---

## Immunogen
An **immunogen** is a substance that is capable of **inducing a specific immune response**.
### Examples
- Bacterial proteins
- Viral proteins
- Toxins
- Foreign proteins

**Immunogen → induces immune response**

📌 **All immunogens are antigens, but all antigens are not necessarily immunogens.**


## Adjuvant
An **adjuvant** is a substance that is added to an antigen/vaccine to **enhance the immune response** against that antigen.

### Functions of adjuvant
- Increase immune response
- Enhance antigen presentation
- Prolong antigen persistence
- Stimulate innate immune mechanisms

### Example
- **Alum (aluminium salts)**
- Freund's complete adjuvant
- Freund's incomplete adjuvant

📌 **Adjuvant itself generally does not provide antigenic specificity; it enhances the immune response to the antigen.**

## Affinity
**Affinity** is the **strength of binding between a single antigenic epitope and a single antibody-binding site (paratope).**

### Example:
**One epitope ↔ one paratope**
Higher affinity = stronger individual binding.


## Avidity
**Avidity** is the **overall strength of binding between a multivalent antibody and a multivalent antigen**, considering all individual antigen–antibody interactions together.

### Example:
An **IgM antibody** has multiple antigen-binding sites. Even if individual interactions are moderate, the overall binding can be very strong because of multiple interactions.


## TYPES OF ANTIGENS

### Exogenous Antigen

**Exogenous antigens** are antigens that **originate outside the body** and enter the body from the external environment.

Examples
- Bacterial antigens
- Food proteins
- Pollen
- Dust particles
- Extracellular microorganisms

They are generally taken up by **antigen-presenting cells (APCs)** and processed for presentation through **MHC class II**.

---

### 2. Endogenous Antigen
**Endogenous antigens** are antigens that are **produced within the cells of the body**.

Examples
- Viral proteins produced inside infected cells
- Abnormal tumor proteins
- Some intracellular microbial proteins

They are generally processed and presented through **MHC class I** to **CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells**.


### 3. Complete Antigen
A **complete antigen** is a substance that can:
1. **Induce an immune response**, and
2. **React specifically with the antibodies or immune cells produced against it.**

Therefore, a complete antigen is both:

**Immunogenic + Antigenic**

### Examples
- Many foreign proteins
- Bacterial proteins
- Toxins


### 4. Incomplete Antigen – Hapten
A **hapten** is a **small molecule that can react specifically with an antibody but cannot normally induce an immune response by itself**.

Therefore:
**Hapten → antigenic but not immunogenic**

To become immunogenic, a hapten must usually be **attached to a carrier protein**.

Example
**Hapten + Carrier protein → Immunogenic complex**
Examples of haptens include certain:
- Drugs
- Small chemical molecules

📌 **Carrier protein provides the immunogenicity, while the hapten provides the antigenic specificity.**


Simple Hapten
A **simple hapten** is a small chemical group/molecule that can **react with a specific antibody but cannot itself induce antibody production**.
It usually requires attachment to a **carrier protein** to become immunogenic.


Complex Hapten
A **complex hapten** is a hapten that can **react with antibodies and, when associated with a suitable carrier, participate in a more complex immune response**.

### 5. Autoantigen
An **autoantigen** is a **self-antigen of the body that becomes the target of an immune response in an autoimmune condition**.

Normally, the immune system is tolerant to self-antigens.
When **self-tolerance is lost**, immune cells may recognize self-antigens as foreign.

Examples
- Nuclear antigens in **SLE**
- Thyroid antigens in **autoimmune thyroid disease**
- Acetylcholine receptor in **myasthenia gravis**

### SuperAntigen
A **superantigen** is a substance, usually a **microbial protein/toxin**, that causes **non-specific and massive activation of T lymphocytes** by binding to the **outside of the normal antigen-binding site** of the T-cell receptor (TCR).

Unlike a normal antigen, a superantigen **does not need to be processed into a specific peptide** before activating T cells.

#### Normal antigen vs Superantigen

**Normal antigen:**  
Antigen → processing → peptide + MHC → specific TCR recognition → limited T-cell activation

**Superantigen:**  
Superantigen → binds **MHC class II + TCR Vβ region** → massive T-cell activation → large cytokine release

This can cause **fever, hypotension, inflammation and toxic shock**.

---

### Types of Superantigens

They can be classified based on **source** and **target cell**.

#### A. Based on source

1. Exogenous Superantigens
These are superantigens that **come from outside the host**, mainly produced by **bacteria**.
They are usually **secreted bacterial toxins**.

Important examples
- **Staphylococcal enterotoxins**
- **Toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1)** – _Staphylococcus aureus_
- **Streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins** – _Streptococcus pyogenes_

Mechanism
**Bacterial superantigen**  
↓  
Binds **MHC class II on APC**  
↓  
Binds **Vβ region of TCR**  
↓  
Massive T-cell activation  
↓  
Large amounts of cytokines  
↓  
**Fever, inflammation, shock**

---

2. Endogenous Superantigens
These are produced **within the host**, particularly by **viruses**.
A classic example is **mouse mammary tumor virus (MMTV)**, whose viral proteins can act as endogenous superantigens.
They are generally associated with **viral infection** and can stimulate particular T-cell populations based on their TCR Vβ usage.

---

#### B. Based on Target Cell

3. T-cell Superantigens
These are the **most important type**.
They activate a large number of **T lymphocytes** by simultaneously binding:
**MHC class II on APC ↔ Superantigen ↔ TCR Vβ on T cell**
They bind to the **Vβ region of TCR outside the conventional peptide-binding site**.

Examples
- **TSST-1**
- **Staphylococcal enterotoxins**
- **Streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins**
    

Result
Massive T-cell activation → release of cytokines such as:
- **IL-2**
- **IFN-γ**
- **TNF-α**
- Other inflammatory mediators

This cytokine release can produce **toxic shock syndrome**.

---

4. B-cell Superantigens
**B-cell superantigens** bind to **B-cell receptors (BCRs)** without requiring recognition through the normal antigen-binding site.
They can activate a large population of B cells that share particular **immunoglobulin variable-region/light-chain characteristics**.

Examples
- **Staphylococcal protein A**
- **Protein L** from _Peptostreptococcus magnus_
    

Important point

**Protein A** binds immunoglobulins, particularly through the **Fc region**, and is considered a B-cell superantigen because it can interact with certain B-cell receptors independently of conventional antigen specificity.

### SAG mechanism
#### T-Cell Activation Mechanisms
1. **Classical T-cell activation**
2. **T-cell superantigen (SAg) activation**
3. **B-cell superantigen activation**


#### 1. Classical T-Cell Activation
In classical activation, the **T cell recognizes a specific antigen peptide presented by an antigen-presenting cell (APC)**.


Two important signals

**Signal 1 – Specific recognition**

**TCR + CD4/CD8 ↔ peptide–MHC**
- CD4 → recognizes **MHC II**
- CD8 → recognizes **MHC I**

**Signal 2 – Co-stimulation**

**CD28 on T cell ↔ B7 (CD80/CD86) on APC**

Both signals are required for effective naïve T-cell activation.

Result
Activated T cells produce cytokines, proliferate and differentiate.
**Classical mechanism = specific antigen recognition**

---

#### 2. T-Cell Superantigen Activation
This is completely different from classical activation.

A **T-cell superantigen does NOT need conventional antigen processing or presentation in the peptide-binding groove.**

Instead, it binds to the **outside of MHC class II** and the **Vβ region of the TCR**

Mechanism
**Superantigen**  
- Binds **outside of MHC-II** on APC  
- Binds **Vβ region of TCR** on T cell
- This creates a bridge:
	- **APC (MHC-II)**  -> **Superantigen**  -> **TCR Vβ**

Result
**Massive, non-specific activation of many T cells**
↓
Large release of cytokines
- **IL-2**
- **IFN-γ**
- **TNF-α**
- Other inflammatory cytokines
↓
**Fever + inflammation + hypotension/shock**
This excessive cytokine release is called a **cytokine storm** in severe situations.

Key point
Unlike classical activation:
❌ No specific peptide recognition  
❌ No conventional peptide–MHC–TCR interaction  
✅ MHC-II + SAg + TCR Vβ cross-linking  
✅ Massive T-cell activation

Example
- **TSST-1**
- **Staphylococcal enterotoxins**
- **Streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins**
- **YPM**

---

#### 3. B-Cell Superantigen Activation

B-cell superantigens activate B cells by binding to the **B-cell receptor (BCR)/immunoglobulin outside the conventional antigen-binding site**.

A conventional antigen binds to the **antigen-binding site** of the BCR.

A B-cell superantigen can bind to particular **immunoglobulin variable-region structures** without requiring conventional antigen recognition.

Mechanism
**B-cell superantigen**  
↓  
Binds to **BCR/immunoglobulin variable region**  
↓  
Cross-linking/activation of B cells  
↓  
**Polyclonal B-cell activation**  
↓  
Antibody production and cytokine effects

Important examples
- **Protein A** → _Staphylococcus aureus_
- **Protein L** → _Peptostreptococcus magnus_
- **HIV gp120** → reported B-cell superantigen activity
    

Key point
**B-cell SAg → BCR → many B cells activated**
It does **not depend on the normal antigen-specific binding site** in the same way as a conventional antigen.

---

|---|---|---|---|
|Target|T cell|T cell|B cell|
|Recognition|**Specific**|**Non-specific**|Largely **non-specific/polycolonal**|

# Antibodies
![Pasted image 20260913181042.png\|559](/img/user/Attachments/Pasted%20image%2020260913181042.png)

**Antibody** is a specific **immunoglobulin (glycoprotein)** produced by **plasma cells** in response to an antigen. It specifically binds to the antigen and helps in its **neutralization or elimination**.

### Key Points
- Antibodies are also called **Immunoglobulins (Ig)**.
- Produced by **plasma cells**, which are differentiated **B lymphocytes**.
- Found in **blood, lymph, and body secretions**.
- They show **specificity** for a particular antigen/epitope.
- Main functions:
    - **Neutralization**
    - **Agglutination**
    - **Precipitation**
    - **Opsonization**
    - **Complement activation**
    - **ADCC (antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity)**
        
- Five major classes:  
    **IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD and IgE**.

## Structure of Antibody
A typical antibody molecule, especially **IgG**, is **Y-shaped** and consists of **4 polypeptide chains**:
- **2 identical heavy (H) chains**
- **2 identical light (L) chains**

These chains are joined by **disulfide (S–S) bonds**.

1. Heavy chains
- Present as **two identical chains**.
- Larger than light chains.
- Determine the **class/isotype** of antibody.
- Have **variable (VH)** and **constant (CH)** regions.

1. Light chains
- Two identical chains.
- Each antibody has either **κ (kappa)** or **λ (lambda)** light chains.
- Never both in the same antibody.
- Have **variable (VL)** and **constant (CL)** regions.

1. Variable region
- Located at the **N-terminal ends** of heavy and light chains.
- Responsible for **antigen recognition and specificity**.
- Contains the **antigen-binding site/paratope**.

1. Constant region
- More conserved region.
- Determines many biological properties and **effector functions** of the antibody.

1. Fab region
**Fab = Fragment antigen-binding**
- Two arms of the Y-shaped antibody.
- Binds specifically to the antigen.
- Each IgG molecule has **2 antigen-binding sites**.

1. Fc region
**Fc = Fragment crystallizable**
- Stem of the antibody.
- Does **not directly bind antigen**.
- Responsible for effector functions such as:
    - Complement activation
    - Binding to Fc receptors on immune cells
    - Opsonization
    - ADCC

1. Hinge region
- Flexible region between **Fab and Fc**.
- Provides flexibility to the two antigen-binding arms.
- Contains disulfide bonds connecting the heavy chains.
## IgM
**IgM is the first immunoglobulin produced during a primary immune response and is the largest immunoglobulin in molecular size.** It is mainly present in the **pentameric form** in blood and is highly effective in **agglutination and complement activation**.

### Serum IgM
- Usually exists as a **pentamer**.
- Contains **5 monomer units** joined together.
- Each monomer has:
    - **2 heavy chains (μ chains)**
    - **2 light chains (κ or λ)**
- The five monomers are connected by **disulfide bonds** and a **J (joining) chain**.
- Has **10 antigen-binding sites** → theoretically **valency = 10**.
![Pasted image 20260913182105.png\|203](/img/user/Attachments/Pasted%20image%2020260913182105.png)
Although IgM has **10 antigen-binding sites**, steric hindrance may prevent all 10 sites from binding simultaneously.

### Location

|Location|Importance|
|---|---|
|**Blood/serum**|Major location of secreted IgM|
|**Lymph**|Present|
|**Lymphoid tissues**|Produced by B cells/plasma cells|
|**B-cell surface**|Present as **monomeric membrane IgM** acting as a B-cell receptor|
|**Secretions**|Small amounts may be present, usually as secretory IgM|

**Most serum IgM is intravascular** because of its large molecular size.



### Functions of IgM

1. Complement activation ⭐
	IgM is the **most efficient immunoglobulin for activating the classical complement pathway**.
	- One IgM molecule bound to an antigen can efficiently activate complement.
	- Leads to formation of the **membrane attack complex (MAC)** and other complement-mediated effects.
2. Agglutination ⭐
	IgM is highly effective in **agglutinating particulate antigens**, such as bacteria and RBCs.
	**Reason:** Pentameric IgM has high valency.
3. Neutralization
	- Can bind to toxins and viruses.
	- Prevents their attachment or entry into host cells.
4. Opsonization
	IgM can promote phagocytosis **indirectly through complement activation**.
5. B-cell receptor
	Monomeric IgM is present on **naïve B lymphocytes** and functions as a **B-cell antigen receptor (BCR)**.

---

### 5. Clinical Significance
1. **Indicator of recent/acute infection**
	IgM is generally the **first antibody detected after a primary infection**.
	**Example:**
	- **Anti-HAV IgM** → recent/acute hepatitis A infection.
	- **Anti-HBc IgM** → recent/acute hepatitis B infection.
	- **Dengue IgM** → suggests recent dengue infection.
> **IgM positive generally suggests recent infection, but interpretation depends on the disease and test.**
2. **Congenital infection**
	IgM is important in diagnosing **intrauterine/congenital infections** because **maternal IgM does not normally cross the placenta**.

	Therefore, IgM detected in a newborn can indicate that the **baby has produced the antibody** in response to infection.

	Examples: Rubella, CMV, Toxoplasmosis, Syphilis
3. **Blood-group antibodies**
	Many naturally occurring **ABO blood-group antibodies** are predominantly IgM
	Example: Anti-A, Anti-B
	They can cause **agglutination of RBCs** and may activate complement.
4. **Macroglobulinemia**
	Excessive monoclonal IgM is characteristic of **Waldenström macroglobulinemia**.
	High IgM can increase blood viscosity → **hyperviscosity syndrome**.
5. **Selective IgM deficiency**
	Reduced IgM levels may be associated with:
	- Recurrent infections
	- Impaired immune response

|Property|IgM|
|---|---|

## IgA
**IgA is the major immunoglobulin present in body secretions and is the principal antibody responsible for protection of mucosal surfaces.** It prevents the attachment and entry of microorganisms into the body.

### Structure
IgA occurs mainly in **two forms**:
1. **Serum IgA** → Monomer
2. **Secretory IgA (sIgA)** → Dimer

#### Serum IgA
- Present mainly as a **monomer**.
- Contains:
    - **2 identical α (alpha) heavy chains**
    - **2 identical light chains** (κ or λ)
- Has **2 antigen-binding sites**.

#### Secretory IgA ⭐
- Present as a **dimer**.
- Contains:
	- **2 IgA monomers**
    - **1 J (joining) chain**
    - **1 secretory component**
- Has **4 antigen-binding sites**.
- The **secretory component protects IgA from proteolytic enzymes** present in secretions.

**Secretory IgA = 2 IgA monomers + J chain + secretory component**

### Location
IgA is found in:
- **Saliva**
- **Tears**
- **Nasal and respiratory secretions**
- **Bronchial secretions**
- **Gastrointestinal secretions**
- **Colostrum and breast milk** ⭐
- **Genitourinary secretions**
- **Blood/serum** — mainly monomeric IgA
**Major location → Mucosal secretions**

---

### Functions
1. Mucosal protection ⭐
	IgA provides the main **immune protection at mucosal surfaces**.
	It prevents microorganisms from attaching to epithelial cells.
2. Neutralization ⭐
	IgA neutralizes:
	- Bacterial toxins
	- Viruses
	- Microbial antigens
3. Prevention of microbial adhesion
	IgA binds microorganisms and prevents their **attachment to mucosal epithelial cells**.
4. Agglutination
	IgA can bind and agglutinate microorganisms, making their removal easier.
5. Protection of newborn
	**Secretory IgA in breast milk and colostrum** provides passive mucosal immunity to the infant.
6. Immune exclusion
	IgA traps microorganisms and antigens in mucus and helps remove them through **mucociliary clearance or intestinal peristalsis**.

### Clinical Significance
#### 1. Selective IgA deficiency ⭐
It is one of the **most common primary immunodeficiencies**.
May cause
- Recurrent respiratory infections
- Recurrent gastrointestinal infections
- Increased susceptibility to certain infections
- Allergic disorders
- Autoimmune diseases
#### 2. Celiac disease
**IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase (anti-tTG)** and **IgA anti-endomysial antibodies (EMA)** are commonly used in laboratory diagnosis of celiac disease.

**Important:** IgA deficiency can cause false-negative IgA-based tests, so **total IgA** may also be assessed.
#### 3. Mucosal infections
Reduced IgA may increase susceptibility to infections of:
- Respiratory tract
- Gastrointestinal tract
- Genitourinary tract
#### 4. Transfusion reactions
Patients with severe IgA deficiency may develop **anti-IgA antibodies** and can rarely develop severe allergic/anaphylactic reactions after exposure to IgA-containing blood products.
#### 5. Breast milk immunity
Secretory IgA in breast milk provides important **passive immunity to the infant**, especially at intestinal and respiratory mucosal surfaces.

## IgG
**IgG is the most abundant immunoglobulin in blood and extracellular fluid and is the major antibody involved in the secondary immune response.** It provides long-lasting systemic immunity and is the **only immunoglobulin that normally crosses the placenta**.

### Structure
- IgG is a **monomer**.
- Contains:
    - **2 identical γ (gamma) heavy chains**
    - **2 identical light chains** (κ or λ)
- The chains are connected by **disulfide bonds**.
- Has **2 antigen-binding sites**.
- Contains:
    - **Fab region** → antigen binding
    - **Fc region** → effector functions
    - **Hinge region** → provides flexibility

**IgG = 2 γ heavy chains + 2 light chains**

### Location
**Major location → Blood and extracellular fluid**

### Functions
1. Neutralization ⭐
	IgG binds to:
	- Toxins
	- Viruses
	- Microbial antigens
	and prevents them from interacting with host cells.
2. Opsonization ⭐
	IgG coats microorganisms and makes them easier for **phagocytes** to recognize and ingest through **Fc receptors**.
3. Complement activation
	Some IgG subclasses, especially **IgG1 and IgG3**, can activate the **classical complement pathway**.
4. ADCC
	IgG-coated target cells can be destroyed by **NK cells** through **antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC)**.
5. Secondary immune response ⭐
	IgG is the **predominant antibody produced during a secondary immune response**.
	It provides:
	- Faster response
	- Stronger response
	- Long-lasting protection
6. Placental transfer ⭐
	IgG is transported across the **placenta** from mother to fetus.
	It provides **passive immunity to the newborn**.
7. Agglutination and precipitation
	IgG can participate in:
	- Agglutination
	- Precipitation of soluble antigens

### Clinical Significance
1. Evidence of previous infection ⭐
	Specific IgG antibodies generally indicate **previous exposure or developing immunity**, although interpretation depends on the infection and test.
	Examples:
	- **Anti-HBs IgG** → immunity to hepatitis B
	- **Rubella IgG** → evidence of immunity/exposure
	- **Toxoplasma IgG** → previous exposure
2. Maternal-fetal immunity ⭐
	Maternal IgG crosses the placenta and protects the fetus/newborn against several infections during early life.
3. Hemolytic disease of the newborn
	Some maternal **IgG antibodies**, particularly antibodies against Rh(D) antigen, can cross the placenta and cause **hemolysis of fetal RBCs**.
4. Autoimmune diseases
	Abnormal IgG autoantibodies are involved in several autoimmune diseases.
	Examples:
	- Rheumatoid arthritis
	- Systemic lupus erythematosus
	- Myasthenia gravis
5. Multiple myeloma
	**Monoclonal IgG** may be produced in multiple myeloma.
	Laboratory findings may include:
	- Increased monoclonal IgG
	- M-protein on serum protein electrophoresis
	- Monoclonal band on immunofixation
6. IgG deficiency
	Low IgG levels can result in **increased susceptibility to recurrent infections**.

|---|---|

|**Antigen-binding sites**|2|
|**Molecular weight**|~150 kDa|
|**Serum concentration**|~7–16 mg/mL|
|**Percentage of total serum Ig**|~70–80%|
|**Half-life**|~21 days|
|**Placental transfer**|**Yes** ⭐|

## IgE
**IgE is an immunoglobulin mainly involved in type I hypersensitivity (allergic reactions) and protection against helminthic (parasitic worm) infections.** It is present in very low concentration in normal serum.

### Structure
- IgE is a **monomer**.
- Contains:
    - **2 identical ε (epsilon) heavy chains**
    - **2 identical light chains** (κ or λ)
- Has **2 antigen-binding sites**.
- Unlike IgG, IgE has **4 constant domains (Cε1–Cε4)** in its heavy chains.
- **IgE does not have a typical hinge region** like IgG.
- Its Fc region binds strongly to **Fcε receptors** on mast cells and basophils.

**IgE = 2 ε heavy chains + 2 light chains**

### Location
**Major location → Surface of mast cells and basophils**

Free IgE has a very low concentration in serum because most IgE is receptor-bound.


### Functions
which immunoglobin is heat labile :: IgE
#### 1. Type I hypersensitivity ⭐
IgE is the main antibody involved in **immediate hypersensitivity/allergic reactions**.
Examples:
- Bronchial asthma
- Allergic rhinitis
- Urticaria
- Anaphylaxis
- Food allergies
**Mechanism:**

```text
Allergen
   ↓
IgE production
   ↓
IgE binds mast cell
   ↓
Second exposure to allergen
   ↓
IgE cross-linking
   ↓
Mast cell degranulation
   ↓
Histamine + other mediators released
   ↓
Allergic symptoms
```

#### 2. Defense against helminths ⭐
IgE participates in defense against **helminthic parasites**, such as:
- Ascaris
- Hookworm
- Schistosoma

IgE helps activate **eosinophils** against parasites through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity.

#### 3. Mast cell activation
IgE binds to high-affinity **FcεRI receptors** on mast cells and basophils.
Cross-linking of IgE by an allergen causes **degranulation**.
#### 4. Eosinophil-mediated killing
IgE-coated parasites can be recognized by eosinophils, which release toxic granules and help destroy the parasite.


### Clinical Significance
#### 1. Allergy diagnosis ⭐
Increased **total IgE** or **allergen-specific IgE** may be associated with allergic disorders.
Examples:
- Allergic rhinitis
- Asthma
- Atopic dermatitis
- Food allergy

Laboratory tests include:
- **Total serum IgE**
- **Allergen-specific IgE**

#### 2. Parasitic infections ⭐
IgE may be increased in **helminthic infections**, especially infections involving tissue-invasive parasites.
However, increased IgE is **not specific** for parasitic infection.
#### 3. Anaphylaxis ⭐
IgE-mediated mast-cell activation can cause **anaphylaxis**, a severe and potentially life-threatening systemic allergic reaction.
#### 4. Hyper-IgE syndrome
**Hyper-IgE syndromes** are rare primary immunodeficiency disorders characterized by markedly elevated IgE along with recurrent infections and other clinical features.
#### 5. Atopic diseases
Elevated IgE is commonly associated with:
- Atopic dermatitis
- Alergic asthma
- Allergic rhinitis

## IgD
**IgD is an immunoglobulin present in very low concentration in serum and is mainly found on the surface of naïve B lymphocytes, where it functions as a B-cell antigen receptor (BCR).**

### Structure
- IgD is a **monomer**.
- Contains:
    - **2 identical δ (delta) heavy chains**
    - **2 identical light chains** (κ or λ)
- Has **2 antigen-binding sites**.
- Contains:
    - **Fab region** → antigen binding
    - **Fc region** → interaction with immune cells
    - **Hinge region** → provides flexibility

**IgD = 2 δ heavy chains + 2 light chains**

### Location
**Major location → Surface of naïve B cells**

IgD is usually expressed together with **IgM** on mature naïve B lymphocytes.

### Functions
#### 1. B-cell antigen receptor ⭐
The major function of IgD is to act as a **B-cell receptor (BCR)**.
It helps:
- Recognize specific antigens
- Initiate B-cell activation
- Participate in B-cell differentiation
#### 2. B-cell activatio
When antigen binds to membrane-bound IgD, it contributes to signaling that activates the B lymphocyte.
#### 3. Regulation of immune response
IgD may participate in the regulation of immune responses, particularly at **mucosal surfaces**.
#### 4. Mucosal immunity
IgD has been detected on B cells and in secretions of the **upper respiratory tract**, where it may interact with microbial antigens and participate in local immune defense.
### Clinical Significance
#### 1. IgD myeloma ⭐
**IgD multiple myeloma** is a rare type of multiple myeloma in which malignant plasma cells produce monoclonal IgD.
Laboratory findings may include:
- Monoclonal IgD
- M-protein
- Abnormal serum/urine immunofixation
#### 2. Hyper-IgD syndrome
**Hyper-IgD syndrome (HIDS)** is a rare autoinflammatory disorder associated with recurrent episodes of:
- Fever
- Lymphadenopathy
- Abdominal symptoms
- Inflammation

**Important:** Despite its name, the condition is not simply caused by excess IgD; it is associated with a genetic defect in the **MVK (mevalonate kinase)** pathway.

#### 3. IgD in respiratory immunity
IgD-poducing or IgD-expressing B cells may participate in immune responses at **upper respiratory mucosal surfaces**.
#### 4. Diagnostic importance
IgD measurement is **not routinely performed** like IgG, IgA, or IgM. It may be investigated in selected cases, particularly when **IgD myeloma** is suspected.
