## 1. Primary Screening & Basic Panels

These tests provide a bird’s-eye view of a patient’s overall cellular health, oxygen-carrying capacity, and immune status.

### 1.1 Complete Blood Count (CBC) with Differential

-   Specimen: EDTA (Lavender top)
-   Parameters Included:
    -   Hemoglobin (Hb / Hgb): Measures oxygen-carrying protein capacity.
    -   Hematocrit (Hct / PCV - Packed Cell Volume): Percentage of total blood volume occupied by red cells.
    -   Red Blood Cell Count (RBC): Absolute count of circulating erythrocytes.
    -   White Blood Cell Count (WBC): Total leukocyte count.
    -   Differential WBC Count: Relative and absolute percentages of Neutrophils, Lymphocytes, Monocytes, Eosinophils, and Basophils.
    -   Platelet Count (PLT): Crucial for initial hemostasis assessment.

### 1.2 Red Cell Indices (RBC Indices)

> -   Essential for classifying anemias (Microcytic, Normocytic, Macrocytic).
> -   MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume): Average size/volume of a single RBC (fL).
> -   MCH (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin): Average weight of Hb per RBC (pg).
> -   MCHC (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin Concentration): Average concentration of Hb in a given volume of packed RBCs (g/dL).
> -   RDW (Red Cell Distribution Width): Quantifies variation in RBC size (Anisocytosis).

## 2. Red Blood Cell & Anemia Diagnostics

When screening tests flag abnormal red cell indices or low hemoglobin, these targeted assays pinpoint the underlying etiology.

1.  Peripheral Blood Smear (PBS) / Manual Differential
    -   Stains Used: Leishman, Wright, or Giemsa stain.
    -   Purpose: Evaluating visual morphology—detecting abnormal RBC shapes (sickle cells, target cells, schistocytes), WBC maturity (blast cells, toxic granulation), and platelet clumps.
2.  Reticulocyte Count
    -   Stain Used: New Methylene Blue or Brilliant Cresyl Blue (Supravital stains).
    -   Purpose: Measures immature RBC production to evaluate bone marrow response (e.g., distinguishing hemolytic anemia from bone marrow failure).
3.  Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR)
    -   Methods: Westergren (Gold Standard) or Wintrobe.
    -   Purpose: Non-specific marker for systemic inflammation, infection, or tissue injury (driven by plasma fibrinogen levels).
4.  Hemoglobin Electrophoresis / HPLC
    -   Purpose: Identifies and quantifies normal ($\text{HbA}$, $\text{HbA}_2$, $\text{HbF}$) and variant hemoglobins ($\text{HbS}$, $\text{HbC}$, $\text{HbE}$) to diagnose hemoglobinopathies and thalassemias.
5.  Osmotic Fragility Test (OFT)
    -   Purpose: Measures RBC resistance to hemolysis in hypotonic saline solutions; diagnostic for Hereditary Spherocytosis.
6.  G6PD Screening / Assay
    -   Purpose: Detects deficiency of Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase, an enzyme protecting RBCs against oxidative destruction.

### 7\. Schilling Test

What is Schilling test
?
The **Schilling test** is a multi-stage medical laboratory procedure historically used to evaluate **Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) absorption** and determine the exact cause of B12 deficiency (e.g., pernicious anemia vs. intestinal malabsorption).


**physiology of stomach**
-   Parietal Cells: The stomach contains specific cells known as parietal cells, which are responsible for producing two vital substances: Hydrochloric Acid (HCl) and Intrinsic Factor.
-   Role of Intrinsic Factor: Intrinsic factor is absolutely crucial for the intestinal absorption of Vitamin B12. Without it, regardless of how much Vitamin B12 a person consumes in their diet, the body cannot absorb it into the bloodstream.

**Pathology: Pernicious Anemia**
-   In certain autoimmune diseases, the body's immune system abnormally produces autoantibodies that attack and destroy its own parietal cells in the stomach. When parietal cells are destroyed, the stomach stops producing intrinsic factor. The resulting inability to absorb Vitamin B12 severely hinders hemoglobin synthesis and red blood cell production (erythropoiesis), leading to a specific condition known as Pernicious Anemia.

**The Schilling Test Procedure**
The Schilling test is performed in stages to pinpoint exactly why a patient is not absorbing Vitamin B12.

Step 1: Baseline Testing (Without Intrinsic Factor)
-   Administration: The patient is given an oral dose of radio-labeled Vitamin B12. This Vitamin B12 is specifically tagged with radioactive Cobalt isotopes, most commonly Co-57 or Co-58.
-   Normal Expectation: If the patient is healthy and producing intrinsic factor normally, the radio-labeled B12 will be absorbed in the intestines, enter the bloodstream, and eventually be excreted into the urine. A normal individual should excretes 10% or more of the administered radio-labeled Vitamin B12 in a 24-hour urine collection.
-   Abnormal Finding: If the patient's 24-hour urine test shows less than 10% excretion, it indicates a severe malabsorption problem.

Step 2: Confirmatory Testing (With Intrinsic Factor)
If the first step yields an abnormally low excretion (less than 10%), the test is repeated to find the exact cause.

-   Administration: This time, the patient is given the oral radio-labeled Vitamin B12 along with an oral dose of Intrinsic Factor.
-   Interpretation: If the addition of the oral intrinsic factor causes the Vitamin B12 to successfully absorb—resulting in a urine excretion of greater than 10%—it definitively proves that the patient's intestines are functioning fine, but their stomach was failing to produce its own intrinsic factor.


### 8. sickle cell test

Introduction to Sickle Cell Disease and Hemoglobin
Under normal, healthy conditions, the body produces normal hemoglobin (governed by the 'A' gene), which plays a critical role in maintaining the resilient, biconcave disc-like shape of Red Blood Cells (RBCs). However, if an individual inherits two 'S' genes (one from the mother and one from the father), their body produces abnormal Sickle Hemoglobin (S Hemoglobin),. In this sickle hemoglobin, the alpha amino acid chain remains normal, but the beta chain becomes abnormal. This specific genetic abnormality causes the red blood cells to lose their normal biconcave shape and physically collapse into a crescent moon-like structure, resulting in Sickle Cell Disease.

The Sickle Cell Test Procedure
To detect the presence of these abnormally shaped cells in a clinical laboratory, technicians perform a specialized test utilizing a chemical reagent called Sodium Metabisulphite. The procedure is performed as follows:

-   Sample Mixing: The technician takes a test tube and adds exactly one pinch of Sodium Metabisulphite powder. To this powder, 100 microliters of the patient's EDTA blood sample is added and mixed thoroughly.
-   Initial Incubation: The mixture is left to sit at room temperature for an initial wait time of 15 minutes (or sometimes 30 minutes),. During this time, the sodium metabisulphite works to force any present sickle hemoglobin to take on its abnormal shape, making the sickle cells visibly appear.
-   Microscopic Examination: After the 15-minute wait, one drop of the mixture is transferred to a glass slide, covered with a coverslip, and examined under a microscope at 40x magnification,.

Strict Protocol for Reporting a Negative Result
If the crescent moon-shaped cells are visible under the microscope after 15 minutes, the test is confirmed positive. However, laboratory technicians must follow a highly strict protocol if no sickle cells are initially seen:

-   If no sickle cells are observed after the initial 15 to 30 minutes, the laboratory must not immediately release a negative report,. This precaution is necessary because sickle cells sometimes take much longer to physically appear in the mixture.
-   First Repeat: The test mixture must be left to sit and then re-examined after exactly 4 hours,.
-   Second Repeat: If the sample still shows no sickle cells after 4 hours, the mixture is incubated further and must be checked a final time after 24 hours,.
-   Only if the blood sample remains completely free of sickle cells after the entire 24-hour incubation period is the test officially declared and reported as negative,.

#### Principle / Immunology Concept

-   Many clinically important RBC antibodies are IgG (“incomplete antibodies”) that sensitize RBCs but do not cause visible agglutination on their own.
-   Anti‑human globulin reagent (Coombs serum) contains antibodies against human IgG (and often complement C3).
-   AHG binds to Fc portion of IgG (or to C3) already bound to RBC membranes; this bridges RBCs together → visible agglutination = positive Coombs test.

So, Coombs test is essentially a “bridge” test: AHG bridges sensitized RBCs to reveal hidden IgG/complement on them.

#### Key Viva / Short‑note Points

-   Invented by Coombs, Mourant and Race (1945).
-   Detects “incomplete” IgG antibodies and complement on RBCs or in serum.
-   Direct = patient RBCs; checks in vivo sensitization (AIHA, HDN).
-   Indirect = patient serum + reagent RBCs; used for antibody screening, crossmatch, antenatal testing.
-   Principle: AHG bridges sensitized RBCs → agglutination.
-   Essential quality control: Coombs control cells to confirm reagent activity and proper washing.

### 1. Important historical info to remember

-   The antiglobulin (Coombs) test principle was first described by Moreschi in 1908, using an antiglobulin reagent to agglutinate sensitized RBCs that did not agglutinate on their own.
-   In 1945, Robin Coombs with Arthur Mourant and Rob Race developed the modern Coombs test to detect weak, non‑agglutinating Rh (anti‑D) antibodies in serum and applied it to hemolytic disease of the newborn and transfusion compatibility.

These names and dates are very exam‑friendly: Moreschi (1908) – principle; Coombs, Mourant, Race (1945) – practical antiglobulin test for Rh antibodies and HDN.

---

### 2. Definition

-   Antiglobulin (Coombs) test: An immunohematology test that uses anti‑human globulin (AHG) to detect IgG antibodies and/or complement bound to red blood cells or present in serum, which are not capable of causing direct visible agglutination.
-   In transfusion medicine, cross matching using the antiglobulin phase is a compatibility test between donor red cells and recipient serum to detect clinically significant antibodies that may cause hemolysis after transfusion.

So, in simple words: Coombs test = a “bridging” test to show hidden IgG/complement on RBCs or in serum. Crossmatch = applying this to donor–recipient pairing.

---

### 3. Principle

-   Many clinically important RBC antibodies (especially IgG) are “incomplete”: they sensitize RBCs by binding to their antigens, but do not produce visible agglutination.
-   Anti‑human globulin reagent (AHG) contains antibodies against human IgG and often complement (C3). When added, AHG binds to the Fc region of IgG (or to C3) already attached to RBCs.
-   This creates bridges between sensitized RBCs, bringing them together and producing visible agglutination.
-   Presence of agglutination = positive antiglobulin reaction, indicating that RBCs have been coated with antibodies and/or complement (direct test), or that antibodies in serum can bind to RBCs (indirect test / crossmatch).

In short: sensitization first, then AHG “bridges” → agglutination → positive result.

---

### 4. What is tested

In the Coombs / antiglobulin context and cross matching, what is being tested is:

-   Antibodies against RBC antigens
    -   Mainly IgG alloantibodies in the recipient’s serum against donor RBC antigens (e.g. anti‑D, anti‑E, anti‑K).
    -   Sometimes autoantibodies in a patient’s serum against their own RBC antigens.
-   Complement coating of RBCs
    -   Complement component C3 bound to RBC surfaces in some immune hemolytic processes.
-   Compatibility between donor RBCs and recipient serum
    -   Whether the recipient has any clinically significant antibodies that could react with donor RBC antigens and cause hemolysis.

So, functionally: the test checks immune reactivity (antibody/complement) directed against RBC antigens.

---

### 5. Function of cross matching

Cross matching is a pre‑transfusion compatibility test performed between donor RBCs and recipient serum/plasma to prevent transfusion reactions.

Main functions:

-   Detect unexpected alloantibodies in recipient serum that are not covered by simple ABO/Rh typing (e.g. anti‑Kell, anti‑Duffy, anti‑Kidd, others).
-   Confirm ABO and Rh compatibility in practice, by mixing actual donor cells with recipient serum.
-   Predict in vivo safety: if agglutination or hemolysis occurs in vitro, the unit is considered incompatible and must not be transfused.
-   Reduce risk of hemolytic transfusion reactions, both immediate and delayed, and improve overall transfusion safety.

Clinically, cross matching is a crucial last “safety barrier” before issuing RBC units.

---

### 6. Key terms

#### 6.1 Alloantibody

-   An alloantibody is an antibody produced by an individual against antigens that are present on cells or tissues of another individual of the same species, but absent on the host’s own cells.
-   In transfusion medicine, these are antibodies against foreign RBC antigens (e.g. anti‑D, anti‑E, anti‑K) formed after prior exposure through pregnancy or transfusion.
-   These antibodies are usually clinically significant if they react at body temperature (37°C) and can cause hemolytic transfusion reactions or hemolytic disease of the newborn.

Example: A Rh‑negative person developing anti‑D after transfusion with Rh‑positive blood.

#### 6.2 Autoantibody

-   An autoantibody is an antibody produced by the immune system that is directed against the person’s own antigens on their own cells or tissues.
-   In hematology, autoantibodies may target RBC membrane antigens, leading to autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA).
-   Autoantibodies often cause a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), indicating in vivo coating of the patient’s own RBCs.

Example: Warm AIHA with IgG autoantibodies against self RBC antigens.

#### 6.3 Autoantigens

-   Autoantigens are the body’s own antigens (self antigens) that, under normal conditions, are tolerated by the immune system.
-   In autoimmune disease, these self antigens become the target of the immune response, with autoantibodies or autoreactive T cells attacking them.
-   For RBCs, autoantigens are normal membrane structures that become the target of autoantibodies in AIHA.

So: allo‑ = other person (same species); auto‑ = self.

---

### 7. Types of cross matching

#### 7.1 Major cross match

-   Major cross matching tests:
    -   Recipient serum/plasma (contains potential antibodies)
    -   Against donor red cells (carry donor antigens).
-   Purpose: Detect whether recipient has antibodies that will react with donor RBC antigens.
-   This is the clinically most important crossmatch, because recipient antibodies attacking donor RBCs can cause serious hemolytic transfusion reactions.

In practice: major crossmatch is mandatory before transfusing RBC units (except in certain emergency uncrossmatched situations).

#### 7.2 Minor cross match

-   Minor cross matching tests:
    -   Donor serum/plasma
    -   Against recipient red cells.
-   Historically relevant when transfusing whole blood, where donor plasma was significant.
-   Modern transfusion practice uses packed red cells with very little donor plasma, so minor cross matching is often considered less critical or omitted; emphasis is on ABO/Rh compatibility and major crossmatch.

Conceptually: minor crossmatch checks whether donor antibodies could attack recipient RBCs.

---

### 8. Validity window period of cross matching

In exam answers, this is usually asked as: “How long is a cross match valid?”

Key points you should remember:

-   Cross matching is valid only for a limited time, because the recipient’s antibody status can change after transfusions, pregnancy, or new immunizing events.
-   Commonly taught exam value:
    -   Cross match and antibody screen are valid for 72 hours (3 days) from the time the sample is drawn, for patients who have been recently transfused or pregnant.
-   After 72 hours, a new blood sample must be collected and cross matching repeated, before further units are issued.

Also remember:

-   Some centers may accept a longer interval if the patient has not been transfused or pregnant within the last 3 months, but for exam purposes “72 hours validity” is the safest answer.
-   This window is designed to ensure that newly formed alloantibodies (secondary immune responses) are detected before transfusion.
