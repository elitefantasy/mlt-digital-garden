### Anatomy of the Respiratory System

The respiratory pathway involves several organs that filter, condition, and transport air into the lungs for gas exchange:

* **Nose and Nasal Cavity:** Air enters through the nostrils and passes into the nasal cavity, which is divided into three regions. The **vestibular region** contains hair and mucus glands to trap and remove dust particles. The **respiratory region** conditions and humidifies the incoming air. Finally, the **olfactory epithelium** is responsible for the sense of smell.  
* **Pharynx:** This is a common pathway for both air and food. It is divided into the *nasopharynx* (behind the nose), *oropharynx* (behind the mouth), and *laryngopharynx*.  
* **Larynx (Voice Box):** Situated between the pharynx and trachea, it contains the **epiglottis**, a cartilaginous flap that closes over the glottis during swallowing to prevent food from entering the windpipe.  
* **Trachea (Windpipe):** A 12-centimeter long cylindrical tube that provides a clear pathway for air. It is supported by 14 to 16 incomplete, **C-shaped cartilaginous rings** that prevent the airway from collapsing. The trachea divides at the level of the T5 thoracic vertebra into the right and left primary bronchi.  
* **Bronchial Tree:** The primary bronchi further divide into secondary bronchi, tertiary bronchi, initial bronchioles, terminal bronchioles, and finally respiratory bronchioles. The portion up to the terminal bronchioles is the **conductive zone** (only passing air), while the respiratory zone begins at the respiratory bronchioles.

* **Alveoli:** These are the functional units of the lungs, resembling grape-like clusters. There are about 300 million alveoli in both lungs combined. They are made of **simple squamous epithelium** to facilitate rapid gas exchange.  
* **Lungs:** The principal organs of the respiratory system, located in the thoracic cavity above the diaphragm.  
  * The **right lung** has three lobes (superior, middle, and inferior) divided by horizontal and oblique fissures, and it sits slightly higher due to the liver below it.  
  * The **left lung** is slightly longer, has two lobes (superior and inferior) divided by an oblique fissure, and features a **cardiac notch** to accommodate the heart.  
  * The lungs are protected by double-layered membranes called pleura (inner visceral and outer parietal layers), with **pleural fluid** in between to act as a shock absorber and prevent friction during breathing.

### **Mechanism of Breathing**

Breathing is driven by pressure differences; air always moves from a high partial pressure to a low partial pressure.

* **Inspiration (Inhalation):** The diaphragm contracts and flattens, while the external intercostal muscles (located between the ribs) contract. This moves the ribcage upward and outward, increasing the volume of the thoracic cavity. Because pressure is inversely proportional to volume, this drops the pressure inside the lungs below atmospheric pressure, causing air to rush in.  
* **Expiration (Exhalation):** The muscles relax, and the diaphragm returns to its original dome shape, while the ribcage moves downward. This decreases the thoracic volume, increasing the internal pressure and forcing CO2-rich air out of the lungs.

### **Respiratory Volumes and Capacities**

* **Tidal Volume (TV):** The volume of air inhaled or exhaled during normal, resting breathing, which is about **500 mL**.
* **Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV):** The additional volume of air that can be forcefully inhaled after a normal inspiration, ranging from **2500 to 3000 mL**.
* **Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV):** The additional volume of air that can be forcefully exhaled after a normal exhalation, ranging from **1000 to 1100 mL**.
* **Residual Volume (RV):** The volume of air that remains in the lungs even after a forceful exhalation, about **1100 to 1200 mL**.
* **Vital Capacity (VC):** The total volume of air that can be exhaled after a maximum forceful inhalation (TV \+ ERV \+ IRV).
* **Total Lung Capacity (TLC):** The maximum volume of air the lungs can hold, which is the sum of the Vital Capacity and the Residual Volume.

### **Exchange of Gases**

Gas exchange occurs across a very thin **diffusion membrane** (less than 1 mm thick), which consists of the simple squamous epithelium of the alveoli, the endothelial cells of the pulmonary capillaries, and a shared basement membrane.

* **At the Alveoli:** The partial pressure of oxygen (PO2) in the alveoli is 104 mmHg, while the deoxygenated blood arriving via the pulmonary artery has a PO2 of 40 mmHg and a PCO2 of 45 mmHg. Due to this gradient, oxygen diffuses into the blood (raising its PO2 to 95 mmHg), and CO2 diffuses out into the alveoli to be exhaled.  
* **At the Tissues:** The newly oxygenated blood travels to the heart and is pumped via systemic arteries to the tissues. The tissues have a lower PO2 (40 mmHg) and a higher PCO2 (45 mmHg) due to constant metabolic activity. Oxygen rapidly diffuses into the cells, and CO2 diffuses into the blood to be carried back to the lungs.
