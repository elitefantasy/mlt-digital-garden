- A hereditary hemolytic anemia caused by abnormal hemoglobin (HbS) leading to sickling of RBCs, hemolysis, and vaso-occlusion (blood vessels blockage). 
- It primarily affects hemoglobin structure and leads to abnormal red blood cell function.
- It is a hereditary blood disorder characterized by RBCs that take on a crescent or sickle shape due to abnormal hemoglobin, known as hemoglobin S. 
- While the alpha (a) chains in hemoglobin S are normal, the *beta (ẞ)=chains* are defective, causing the hemoglobin molecules to polymerize into long chains. This structural abnormality leads to sickle-shaped cells, which are fragile and prone to destruction.
- In children, the abnormal cells can cluster together, blocking blood vessels and causing tissue damage, a condition known as *infarction*.
- Infarction often affects small bones in the hands and feet, leading to unequal digit lengths, referred to as *hand and foot syndrome*.
- Affected children may also develop *jaundice* due to increased breakdown of RBCs.

Sickle cell disorders depend on whether the hemoglobin-beta gene mutation is present on one or both chromosomes.

**Sickle Cell Trait (Heterozygous Carrier)**
- **Genetics:** Mutation on **only one** chromosome (either paternal or maternal).
- **Hemoglobin profile:** About **40% HbS** and **60% normal HbA**.
- **Clinical picture:** Usually **asymptomatic**, because normal HbA can still carry oxygen effectively.
- **Exceptions:** Under stress conditions (e.g., severe hypoxia, dehydration, extreme exertion, high altitude), HbS can sickle and cause symptoms.


**Sickle Cell Anemia (Homozygous Disease)**
- **Genetics:** Mutation on **both** chromosomes (paternal and maternal).
- **Hemoglobin profile:** **Almost all Hb is HbS**; little to no HbA.
- **Pathophysiology:** HbS molecules **polymerize** under low oxygen, distorting red cells into a sickle shape, reducing flexibility, and impairing oxygen delivery.
- **Clinical picture:** Patients develop **sickle cell anemia**, with chronic hemolytic anemia, vaso-occlusive crises, and organ damage.

> [!info] **Heterozygous** means having two different alleles of a gene (one normal, one mutated), while **homozygous** means having two identical alleles (both normal or both mutated).

## Pathogenesis
Sickle cell anemia results from a single amino acid substitution in the β-globin chain (glutamic acid → valine at position 6), producing abnormal hemoglobin S (HbS). The central pathogenic event is **polymerization of deoxygenated HbS**, which triggers a cascade of cellular and vascular abnormalities.

### Molecular Mechanism
1. **Deoxygenation of HbS**
    - When oxygen tension falls (e.g., in capillaries, during exercise, infection, or hypoxia), HbS releases oxygen and undergoes a conformational change.
    - The hydrophobic valine residue at β6 creates a "sticky patch" that interacts with complementary sites on adjacent HbS molecules.
2. **Polymerization and Fiber Formation**
    - Deoxygenated HbS molecules aggregate into long, rigid polymers (tactoids) inside the red blood cell (RBC).
    - These polymers distort the RBC membrane, producing the characteristic **sickle shape**.
3. **Reversible vs. Irreversible Sickling**
    - **Reversible sickling:** Early sickling is reversible; upon re-oxygenation, HbS depolymerizes and the RBC regains its normal biconcave shape.
    - **Irreversible sickling:** Repeated cycles of sickling and unsickling cause cumulative membrane damage, calcium influx, and dehydration of the RBC. The cell becomes permanently sickled even in the presence of oxygen.
        

### Cellular and Hemodynamic Consequences
1. Hemolysis (Chronic Hemolytic Anemia)
	- Sickled RBCs are rigid and fragile, leading to:
	    - **Intravascular hemolysis** (RBCs rupture in circulation).
	    - **Extravascular hemolysis** (RBCs trapped and destroyed by splenic macrophages).
	- Result: **Chronic anemia**, jaundice, gallstones (pigment stones), and reticulocytosis.
2. Vaso-Occlusion (Vaso-Occlusive Crises)
	- Rigid, sickled RBCs cannot deform to pass through small capillaries.
	- They adhere to activated endothelium (via adhesion molecules like VCAM-1, integrins) and trap leukocytes and platelets, forming microthrombi.
	- This causes **microvascular occlusion**, leading to tissue ischemia and infarction.
3. End-Organ Damage
	- Repeated vaso-occlusion and chronic hemolysis cause cumulative injury to multiple organs:
	- **Bone and bone marrow:** Painful crises, osteonecrosis (avascular necrosis), dactylitis (hand-foot syndrome in children).
	- **Spleen:** Repeated infarction → functional asplenia (autosplenectomy) → increased infection risk (encapsulated bacteria).
	- **Lungs:** Acute chest syndrome, pulmonary hypertension.
	- **Kidneys:** Papillary necrosis, hematuria, chronic kidney disease.
	- **Brain:** Stroke (especially in children).
	- **Heart:** Cardiomegaly, high-output heart failure due to chronic anemia.
	- **Eyes:** Retinopathy, vision loss.
	- **Skin and extremities:** Chronic ulcers, especially over malleoli.

## Factors Influencing HbS Polymerization and Sickling

The severity of sickle cell disease depends on several modulating factors that affect HbS polymerization and red cell sickling.

---

### 1. Amount of HbS and Interaction with Other Hemoglobin Chains

#### Heterozygous vs. Homozygous State
- **Heterozygous (HbA/HbS – sickle cell trait):**
    - Only ~40% HbS; remaining is normal HbA.
    - Sickling occurs **only under severe hypoxia** (e.g., extreme altitude, severe dehydration).
    - Generally asymptomatic.
- **Homozygous (HbS/HbS – sickle cell anemia):**
    - Nearly 100% HbS.
    - Polymerization occurs readily → **full-blown sickle cell anemia (SCA)**.

#### Role of HbF (Fetal Hemoglobin)
- **HbF (α₂γ₂) inhibits HbS polymerization** by interfering with HbS–HbS interactions.
- Newborns are protected until **5–6 months of age**, when HbF levels decline and HbS becomes predominant.
- Higher HbF levels → milder disease (basis for hydroxyurea therapy).

#### Interaction with HbC (HbSC Disease)
- **HbC:** Point mutation at β6 (glutamic acid → lysine).
- HbC has a **greater tendency to co-aggregate with deoxygenated HbS** than HbA does.
- **HbSC disease** (HbS/HbC):
    - Milder than homozygous SCA but still clinically significant.
    - Increased risk of retinopathy and splenomegaly.

---

### 2. Intracellular Hemoglobin Concentration (MCHC)

- **HbS polymerization rate is highly dependent on intracellular Hb concentration (MCHC).**
- **Increased MCHC → faster polymerization → more sickling.**

#### Examples:
- **Intracellular dehydration:**
    - Loss of water from RBCs → ↑ MCHC → ↑ HbS concentration → ↑ sickling.
- **Coexistent α-thalassemia:**
    - Reduces α-globin chain synthesis → less total hemoglobin per cell → ↓ MCHC.
    - Results in **milder hemolysis and less severe disease**.

---

### 3. Effect of pH (Acidosis)
- **Decrease in pH (acidosis) promotes sickling.**
- Mechanism:
    - Acidosis **decreases oxygen affinity of Hb** (right shift of oxygen dissociation curve).
    - Hb releases oxygen more readily → more deoxygenated HbS → ↑ polymerization → ↑ sickling.
- Clinical relevance: Acidotic states (e.g., infection, lactic acidosis, renal failure) can precipitate vaso-occlusive crises.

---

### 4. Duration of Exposure to Low Oxygen Tension
- **Sickling is time-dependent**; RBCs must remain deoxygenated long enough for HbS polymers to form.

#### Sites Prone to Sickling
- **Microvascular beds with sluggish blood flow:**
    - **Spleen** and **bone marrow** are classic sites where RBCs spend more time in low-oxygen environments.
    - This explains early splenic infarction and bone marrow necrosis in SCA.
#### Role of Inflammation and Adhesion
- **Inflammation** activates endothelium and upregulates adhesion molecules (e.g., VCAM-1, integrins).
- **Increased RBC adhesion** to endothelium → prolonged stasis → longer exposure to hypoxia → more sickling.
- Creates a vicious cycle: sickling → adhesion → stasis → more sickling → vaso-occlusion.

## Clinical Features
### A. Sickle Cell Anemia (HbSS) 
- Chronic hemolytic anemia : Fatigue, pallor, jaundice 
- Painful crises (vaso-occlusive crises) : Bone pain, abdominal pain 
- Acute chest syndrome 
- Splenomegaly -> autosplenectomy 
- Infections (especially in children) 
- Growth retardation

### B. Sickle Cell Trait (HbAS) 
- Usually asymptomatic 
- Mild symptoms only under extreme conditions: • Hypoxia • Dehydration High altitude

---
- Chronic hemolysis, Hyperbilirubinemia, microvascular occlusion, infarction & thrombosis. 
- Expansion of bone marrow - bone resorption & secondary new bone formation - prominent cheekbones& changes in skull (Crew-cut appearance in X-ray). 
- Spleen: Early phase: Splenomegaly ;;; Micro: Marked congestion with trapping of sickled red cells in splenic cords & sinuses. 
- Progressive shrinkage of spleen - autosplenectomy by adolescence or early childhood

#### Principle of cellulose acetate electrophoresis 
Cellulose acetate electrophoresis separates different types of hemoglobin according to their electrical charge and electrophoretic mobility when placed in an electric field. 

At alkaline pH (≈ 8.4-8.6), hemoglobin molecules carry a negative electrical charge. When the patient's hemolysate is applied to a cellulose acetate membrane and an electric current is passed: 
- Hemoglobin molecules migrate toward the anode (+). 
- Different hemoglobin variants have different net charges. 
- Therefore, they migrate at different rates and distances. 
- This produces distinct bands, which can be stained and compared with known controls.

At alkaline cellulose acetate electrophoresis: 
Cathode (-) → Origin ->  Hbs/HbD/HbG -> HbF → HbA → HbA2/НbC/НbЕ → Anode(+) 

HbA is the major normal adult hemoglobin and migrates faster than HbS toward the anode. 

Why Hb Variants Migrate Differently? 
Amino-acid substitutions in the globin chain alter the net charge of the hemoglobin molecule. For example: HbA: B-chain contains glutamic acid at position 6 HbS: ẞ-chain contains valine at position 6 This change alters the electrophoretic mobility of HbS.

**Sample requirement** 
Specimen: EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood. 
Hemolysate preparation:
1. Dilute 100-200 µL of whole blood in 10 mL of saline, centrifuge, and discard the supernatant. 
2. Take 15 µL packed cells and mix with 90 µL hemolyzing solution. 
3. Incubate 25 minutes. 
4. Hemolysate is stable for 3–4 days at 2–8 °C (protected from light). 
5. Hemolysate are prepared by addition of water and toluene to saline washed erythrocytes Tris-EDTA Boric Acid (TEB) buffer, pH 8.4.

**Procedure**
Collect blood in EDTA. 
1. Prepare hemolysates. 
2. Fill the electrophoresis chamber with working buffer. 
3. Place agarose gel (application side up) on the support plate. 
4. Blot the application zone for ~5 seconds using blotter strips. 
5. Position the sample template over the gel. 
6. Apply 5 µL hemolysate per well; allow absorption (90 seconds). 
7. Blot off excess sample and remove template. 
8. Place gel into the tank with the sample side toward the cathode. 
9. Run electrophoresis at 200 V for ~30 minutes. 
10. Dry gel, stain with Amido Black (5 min). 
11. Destain sequentially in three fresh baths of citric acid solution (5 min each). 
12. Air-dry the gel and compare the banding pattern with the controls.

Interpretation & Result Abnormal 
Hb fractions are identified based on their migration and relative percentages. 
- HbA (normal adult): Major band. 
- HbF: Increased in newborns, hereditary persistence of foetal haemoglobin (HPFH), and some thalassemia. 
- HbS: Indicates sickle cell trait (HbAS) or sickle cell disease (HbSS). 
- HbC: May indicate HbC disease or HbSC disease. 
- HbE: Common in Southeast Asia, usually mild anaemia. 
- HbD, HbO-Arab, others: Rare variants, clinically significant when combined with HbS or thalassemia.

#### Acid Agar Electrophoresis 
Principle 
Hemoglobin is separated on an agarose gel at acidic pH, approximately 5.8-6.2. 

At acidic pH, differences in the interaction of Hb molecules with the gel and buffer produce a different migration pattern from alkaline electrophoresis. 

Reagents . . . Agarose gel , Acid citrate buffer, approximately pH 5.8–6.2 , Hb hemolysate , Electrophoresis buffer , Staining reagent , Destaining solution , Hb controls

Procedure 
1. Prepare Hb hemolysate from EDTA blood. 
2. Prepare or use a precast agarose gel. 
3. Apply patient hemolysate and controls. 
4. Perform electrophoresis using acidic buffer. 
5. Hb fractions migrate through the agarose. 
6. Stain the gel. 
7. Destain. 
8. Compare the migration pattern with controls. 

Result 
Acid electrophoresis is particularly useful for differentiating variants that have similar mobility during alkaline electrophoresis.

Main use 
Confirmatory characterization of Hb variants.


#### Capillary Electrophoresis 
Principle 
Capillary electrophoresis separates Hb fractions inside a narrow capillary tube under a high-voltage electric field. 

At alkaline conditions, Hb fractions carry different charges and migrate at different velocities. 
The instrument detects the separated Hb fractions and produces an electropherogram. 

Reagents 
Depending on the analyzer: 
Hemolyzing solution , Alkaline buffer/electrolyte , Washing solution , Detection solution/reagents, Calibrators, Quality-control materials 

Most modern systems use automated reagent cartridges.

Procedure
1. EDTA blood is loaded. 
2. The analyzer prepares the Hb hemolysate. 
3. Hemolysate enters a capillary. 
4. High voltage is applied. 
5. Hb fractions migrate according to their electrophoretic mobility. 
6. Hb is detected photometrically. 
7. The analyzer generates an electropherogram. 
8. Individual fractions are quantified. 

Result- 
A typical report may provide: 
HbA% HbA2% HbF% HbS % HbC or other variant % Other unidentified fractions

#### High-Performance Liquid Chromatography
HPLC is not electrophoresis. It is a chromatographic method, but it is one of the most important modern methods for hemoglobin analysis. 

Principle 
HPLC separates hemoglobin fractions based on their different interactions with an ion-exchange column. 
Hb molecules bind differently to the column and are subsequently eluted using changing buffer conditions. 
The instrument detects Hb fractions and produces a chromatogram.

Procedure 
1. EDTA blood is introduced into the analyzer. 
2. RBCs are lysed. 
3. Hb enters the ion-exchange column. 
4. Hb fractions bind to the column with different affinities. 
5. Buffers elute the Hb fractions. 
6. Hb is detected photometrically, 
7. A chromatogram is generated. 
8. Hb fractions are quantified according to retention time.

Disorders detected 
Thalassemia: Reduced HbA, increased HbF and/or HЬA2 
Sickle cell trait (HbAS): HbA + HbS present • 
Sickle cell disease (HbSS): Predominantly HbS + HbF 
HbC disease: Predominantly НbС • 
HbSC disease: HbS and HbC, usually no HbA

## Disease Management
- General Management 
	- Adequate hydration 
	- Avoid hypoxia & stress
	- Folic acid supplementation 
- Specific Treatment 
	- Hydroxyurea : Increases HbF→ reduces sickling 
	- Pain management : NSAIDS / opioids during crisis 
	- Blood transfusion In severe anemia or complications 
- Advanced Treatment : Bone marrow transplantation (curative)
