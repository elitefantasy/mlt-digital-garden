# The Complete Blood Count (CBC): A Clinical Overview

#haematology

# 1\. Introduction and Overview

It serves as a quantitative and qualitative assessment of the three primary cellular components of peripheral blood: **Red Blood Cells (RBCs)**, **White Blood Cells (WBCs)**, and **Platelets (PLT)**.  
.

# 2\. Pre-Analytical Considerations: Specimen Integrity

* **Specimen Type:** Whole blood, collected via venipuncture in an **EDTA (Lavender top)** tube. EDTA is the preferred anticoagulant as it best preserves blood cell morphology for manual smears if needed.  
* **The "Pro" Rule:** Always inspect your sample for **micro-clots** before placing it on the automated analyzer. A clotted tube invalidates the platelet and WBC counts instantly, leading to erroneous results.  
* **Order of Draw:** Always adhere to the correct order of draw to prevent cross-contamination of anticoagulants.

# 3\. Core Components of the Hemogram

## A. Red Blood Cell (RBC) Indices

RBC indices are calculated parameters provided by modern automated analyzers. They are essential for the morphological classification of anemias.

| Index | Formula | Normal Range | Clinical Significance |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **MCV** | (Hct (%) / RBC (x10^12/L)) × 10 | 80–100 fL | Classifies RBC size (Microcytic: \<80 fL, Normocytic: 80–100 fL, Macrocytic: \>100 fL). |
| **MCH** | (Hb (g/dL) / RBC (x10^12/L)) × 10 | 27–31 pg | Average mass of hemoglobin per RBC. Indicates normochromic, hypochromic, or hyperchromic states. |
| **MCHC** | (Hb (g/dL) / Hct (%)) × 100 | 32–36 g/dL | Measures hemoglobin concentration per unit volume of RBCs. Used to identify conditions like spherocytosis or hypochromia. |
| **RDW** | (Standard Deviation of MCV / Mean MCV) × 100 | 11.5–14.5% | Quantifies variation in RBC size (anisocytosis); high values are early indicators of nutritional deficiencies. |

## B. White Blood Cell (WBC) & Differential

The WBC count represents the total number of leukocytes in the blood. The **differential count** (automated or manual) breaks this down into the five major types, each with distinct roles in immunity:

* **Neutrophils:** Primary responders to bacterial/fungal infections.  
* **Lymphocytes:** Key to viral defense and adaptive immunity.  
* **Monocytes:** "Cleanup" cells that process debris and alert the immune system.  
* **Eosinophils:** Active in parasitic infections and allergic reactions.  
* **Basophils:** Release histamine/heparin in allergic/inflammatory responses.

| Parameter | Normal Range | Clinical Significance |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Total WBC** | 4.5–11.0 x 10^9/L | Indicator of immune status; elevated in infection/inflammation, decreased in bone marrow suppression. |
| **Neutrophils** | 40–70% | Primary defense against bacterial/fungal infections. |
| **Lymphocytes** | 20–45% | Key for viral defense and adaptive immunity. |
| **Monocytes** | 2–10% | Involved in phagocytosis and immune response regulation. |
| **Eosinophils** | 1–6% | Active in parasitic infections and allergic reactions. |
| **Basophils** | 0–1% | Release histamine/heparin during allergic/inflammatory responses. |

## C. Platelets (PLT)

Essential for primary hemostasis. A low count (**thrombocytopenia**) increases bleeding risk, while a high count (**thrombocytosis**) can predispose patients to inappropriate clotting.

| Parameter | Normal Range | Clinical Significance |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Platelet Count** | 150–450 x 10^9/L | Essential for primary hemostasis; low (thrombocytopenia) causes bleeding risk, high (thrombocytosis) increases clotting risk. |

# 4\. Instrumentation: How it Works

Modern hematology analyzers have largely replaced manual chamber counting (like the Neubauer chamber, which is now primarily for specialized settings).

* **Electrical Impedance (Coulter Principle):** As cells pass through a small orifice, they create an impedance change in an electrical current, which is proportional to cell volume.  
* **Flow Cytometry:** Uses lasers to analyze cell granularity, diameter, and complexity (used to generate the WBC differential).  
* **Spectrophotometry:** Used specifically to quantify Hemoglobin concentration.

# 5\. Clinical Significance & Interpretation

When analyzing results, always consider that **physiological factors** (age, sex, altitude, pregnancy, exercise) can cause normal variations.

1. 

| Parameter | Normal Range | Clinical Significance |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Hemoglobin (Hb)** | Male: 13.5–17.5 g/dL; Female: 12.0–15.5 g/dL | Measures oxygen-carrying capacity; low indicates anemia, high indicates erythrocytosis. |
| **Hematocrit (Hct)** | Male: 41–53%; Female: 36–46% | Percentage of blood volume occupied by RBCs; reflects hydration status and oxygen transport capacity. |
| **WBC Count** | 4.5–11.0 x 10^9/L | Elevated in infection/inflammation (leukocytosis); low in marrow suppression (leukopenia). |
| **Platelet Count** | 150–450 x 10^9/L | Low indicates thrombocytopenia (bleeding risk); high indicates thrombocytosis (clotting risk). |
