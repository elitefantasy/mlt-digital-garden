# Anemia

Defination
**Anemia** is a condition in which the **hemoglobin (Hb) concentration**, **hematocrit (Hct)**, or **red blood cell (RBC) mass** is lower than normal for a person's age, sex, and physiological status, resulting in **reduced oxygen-carrying capacity of blood**.


Hemoglobin Reference Values

| Category | Hemoglobin Value / Cut-off |
|---|---:|
| Adult Male (Normal) | 13.0–17.0 g/dL |
| Adult Female (Normal) | 12.0–15.0 g/dL |
| Pregnant Woman (Normal) | ≥11.0 g/dL |
| Newborn (Normal) | 14–20 g/dL |
| Anemia Cut-off: Men | <13 g/dL |
| Anemia Cut-off: Non-pregnant Women | <12 g/dL |
| Anemia Cut-off: Pregnant Women / Children (6–59 months) | <11 g/dL |

### RBC Inclusion Bodies

- **Howell–Jolly bodies:** :: Composed of **DNA remnants** (nuclear fragments). They are often associated with post-splenectomy, hyposplenism, or megaloblastic anemia.
<!--SR:!2026-09-12,5,190-->

- **Heinz bodies:** :: Composed of denatured hemoglobin precipitates. They are typically associated with **G6PD deficiency** and other hemoglobinopathies or exposure to oxidative chemicals.
<!--SR:!2026-09-21,16,190-->

- **Pappenheimer bodies:** :: Composed of **iron-containing granules (hemosiderin)**. They are seen in sideroblastic anemia, hyposplenism, and iron-loading states.
<!--SR:!2026-09-13,2,150-->

- **Basophilic stippling:** :: Composed of aggregates of ribosomal RNA and ribosomes. Common associations include lead poisoning, thalassemia, and megaloblastic anemia.
<!--SR:!2026-11-10,64,310-->

- **Cabot rings:** Composed of remnants of mitotic spindle microtubules or nuclear membrane. They are associated with severe dyserythropoiesis, megaloblastic anemia, and myelodysplastic syndromes.

# Important RBC Indices

Calculated parameters used to define RBC size and hemoglobin content:

| Index | Formula & Calculation | Normal Range | Clinical Interpretation |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume)** | (PCV × 10) / RBC count (in millions/µL) | 76–96 fL | <76 fL: Microcytic (iron deficiency, thalassemia); >96 fL: Macrocytic (megaloblastic anemia – vitamin B12/folate deficiency) |
| **MCH (Mean Corpuscular Hemoglobin)** | (Hb in g/dL × 10) / RBC count | 27–32 pg | Measures average weight of Hb per single RBC. Decreased in hypochromic anemias. |
| **MCHC (Mean Corpuscular Hb Concentration)** | (Hb in g/dL × 100) / PCV | 31–36 g/dL (%) | Average Hb concentration per packed cell volume. Truly diagnostic for hypochromia. |
| **RDW (Red Cell Distribution Width)** | Standard deviation of MCV / Mean MCV × 100 | 11.5–14.5% | Quantifies **anisocytosis** (variation in RBC size). Elevated in iron deficiency anemia; normal in uncomplicated thalassemia trait. |

# 2. Hemorrhagic Anemia

Hemorrhagic anemia occurs due to significant blood loss.

**Term**
**Tissue Hypoperfusion**
Tissue hypoperfusion is a dangerous medical state where the body fails to deliver enough blood, oxygen, and nutrients to the cells. If it continues, cells switch to unsafe energy production, creating toxic lactic acid. This leads to severe organ failure and death if doctors do not treat it fast.

## Types

### 2.1 Acute Hemorrhagic Anemia
- Blood becomes diluted because plasma is replenished within 24 hours, whereas RBCs take approximately 4–6 weeks to replenish.
- Blood gets **diluted** because interstitial fluid enters the bloodstream.
- Therefore, in the initial stages, **Hb and HCT** may not fall immediately and may fall after fluid redistribution.
- RBC morphology is generally normal.
- Causes: Trauma, Surgery, GI hemorrhage
- Initially, hypovolemia leads to reduced venous return, which **decreases cardiac output.**
- The body attempts to maintain tissue perfusion through: tachycardia , vasoconstriction , sympathetic nervous system activation.
- **RAAS system activation:**
	- Causes the body to release aldosterone.
	- Aldosterone causes the kidneys to retain sodium and water and excrete potassium.
	- This helps maintain blood pressure and blood volume.
- **Bone marrow response:**
  - EPO increases.
  - EPO stimulates erythroid progenitor cells.
  - RBC production increases.
  - Reticulocytes are released and become evident after several days.
  - PBS may show **polychromasia**.

### 2.2 Chronic Hemorrhagic Anemia

- Causes: Peptic ulcer (Helicobacter pylori) , Purpura, GI bleeding, Menorrhagia, Iron depletion occurs.
- RBCs become: Microcytic, **Pale**, hypochromic
- Eventually leads to IDA.

# 4. Aplastic Anemia

- Aplastic anemia is a condition characterized by **bone marrow failure**, leading to a decrease in the production of blood cells.
- **Pancytopenia:** A reduction in platelet counts and other blood cell lines.
- **Bone Marrow Status:** Examination typically reveals a hypocellular marrow.
- **RBC Morphology:** It is often classified as a normocytic normochromic anemia, meaning the RBCs are normal in size and structure, generally within the 80–100 fL MCV range.
- **Etiology:** It is categorized as a bone marrow disorder resulting in decreased RBC production., radiation exposure, toxic substance, infections.

# 5. Anemia of Chronic Disorder

## Causes

- Chronic infections, such as tuberculosis.
- Chronic inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis.
- Chronic kidney disease.

## Pathophysiology

1. Due to inflammation, IL-6 (cytokine) is released.
2. IL-6 stimulates the liver to produce ==hepcidin==.
3. Hepcidin blocks iron release and reduces intestinal iron absorption.
4. Iron becomes trapped in storage form as ferritin.
5. EPO decreases.
6. RBC production in the bone marrow decreases.
7. Result: Iron is present in the body but is not available for Hb synthesis.
<!--SR:!2026-11-09,63,310-->

## Features

- Weakness
- Pallor

## Laboratory Findings

- Serum iron decreased.
- Serum ferritin normal or increased.
- CBC:
  - PCV decreased
  - RBC count decreased
  - Hb decreased
- RCI mostly normal.
- RDW mildly increased.
- PBS:
  - Normocytic
  - Normochromic
- Reticulocyte count decreased, indicating reduced bone marrow response.
- Bone marrow examination:
  - Increased hemosiderin
  - Increased iron stores

### A. Physiological Iron Loss

Natural daily iron loss occurs through:

- Bile
- Urine
- Shedding of skin cells
- Desquamation of the intestinal lining

### B. Chronic Blood Loss

- Gastrointestinal bleeding and conditions such as peptic ulcer disease.
- Heavy menstrual bleeding.
- Hookworm infestations.
- Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).
- Occult blood loss in stool.

### C. Reduced Iron Absorption and Poor Dietary Intake

- **Dietary inhibitors:**
  - Diets high in calcium
  - Phytates found in grains and cereals
  - Tannins found in tea and coffee

  These reduce iron absorption.

- **Gastrointestinal issues:**
  - Celiac disease
  - Chronic intestinal infections
  - Post-gastric bypass surgery

- **Inadequate nutrition**

### D. Increased Iron Demands

- Pregnancy and breastfeeding (lactation) due to low pre-pregnancy stores and increased maternal/fetal demands.
- Rapid growth phases during infancy and adolescence.

## Pathophysiology

Iron deficiency anemia develops in three successive stages:

### 1. Depletion of Iron Stores

Iron stored as ferritin and hemosiderin in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow gradually depletes.

Serum ferritin levels drop, serving as the earliest indicator of developing deficiency.

### 2. Iron-Deficient (Iron-Restricted) Erythropoiesis

As stores empty, the iron supply to the bone marrow becomes inadequate for red blood cell production.

When transferrin saturation falls below 15%, the marrow cannot meet the basal requirements for hemoglobin production.

- Erythrocyte protoporphyrin rises.
- Serum iron drops.
- Total Iron-Binding Capacity (TIBC) increases because transferrin production scales up to maximize the transport of available iron.

### 3. Established Iron Deficiency Anemia

Hb synthesis decreases.

RBCs undergo extra cell division, resulting in:

- Small RBCs — microcytic, low MCV.
- Pale RBCs — hypochromic, low MCH/increased central pallor.
- Increased RDW due to progressive variation in RBC size.

## Clinical Features

### General Anemic Symptoms — Tissue Hypoxia

- Fatigue and general weakness
- Pallor (pale skin/mucous membranes)
- Dizziness and shortness of breath
- Tachycardia due to increased cardiac output triggered to counter tissue hypoxia

1. Iron entererythroid cells
2. Iron transported into mitochondria 
3. Fe2+ is inserted into protoporphyrin 
4. Heme is produced 
5. Heme combine with globin 
6. Hemoglobin is formed 
Note : in sideroblastic anemia Iron reaches the mitochondria but is not efficiently incorporated into heme.

# 8\. Pernicious Anemia
- Caused by vitamin B12 deficiency, often due to autoimmune destruction of stomach parietal cells.
- Parietal cells produce intrinsic factors needed for vitamin B12 absorption.
- Yellowish skin tone (pale anemia + mild jaundice).
- Red, sore tongue.
- source of folic acid: milk , eggs, liver, fresh greenly leaf, 
- folic acid reabsorbed from bile into the bloodstream, similar to vitamin B12.
- role of folic acid: Functions as a coenzyme in DNA & RNA synthesis.
- folic acid daily requirements: 400 mu/g 

## Diagnosis

- PBS : Impaired RBC maturation results in macrocytic & normochromic or slightly hypochromic RBCS.
- Vit B12 Test
