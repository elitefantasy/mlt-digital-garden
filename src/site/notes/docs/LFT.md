# 1. Physiological Functions of the Liver

* **Metabolic Function:** carbohydrate metabolism (glycogen storage and gluconeogenesis), lipid metabolism (cholesterol and lipoprotein synthesis), and protein metabolism.  
* **Synthetic Function:** Synthesizes essential plasma proteins such as albumin and critical blood clotting factors (Fibrinogen, Prothrombin).  
* **Detoxification and Excretion:** Neutralizes toxins, drugs, and hormones; converts toxic ammonia into urea for renal excretion and processes bilirubin.  
* **Storage Function:** Acts as a reservoir for essential substances including glycogen, vitamins (A, D, B12), and minerals (Iron, Copper).  
* **Biliary Function:** Produces bile, which is essential for the emulsification and absorption of dietary fats and fat-soluble vitamins.

# 2. Bilirubin

Bilirubin is a yellow pigment produced from the breakdown of the heme portion of hemoglobin.

## 2.a Bilirubin Metabolism

* **Unconjugated (Indirect) Bilirubin:** Insoluble in water; transported to the liver bound to albumin.  
* **Conjugated (Direct) Bilirubin:** Formed in the liver when unconjugated bilirubin combines with glucuronic acid via the enzyme *glucuronyl transferase*. excreted into bile

## 2.b Clinical Classification of Jaundice

clinically significant when bilirubin is >2mg/dl

| Jaundice Type | Cause | Typical Lab Findings |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Pre-hepatic (Hemolytic)** | Excessive RBC breakdown | Elevated Unconjugated and Total Bilirubin. |
| **Hepatic (Hepatocellular)** | Liver cell damage (Hepatitis, Cirrhosis) | Elevated both Direct and Indirect Bilirubin. |
| **Post-hepatic (Obstructive)** | Bile duct blockage (Gallstones, Cancer) | Predominantly elevated Direct Bilirubin. |

## 2.c Laboratory Measurement (Diazo Method)
The diazo method for bilirubin is based on the **Van den Bergh reaction**, in which bilirubin reacts with a **diazotized aromatic amine** (usually diazotized sulfanilic acid) in acidic medium to form a colored **azobilirubin** compound, whose intensity is measured spectrophotometrically and is proportional to bilirubin concentration.

### Core chemical principle
- sulfanilic acid reacts sodium nitrate in acidic solution to form diazotized sulfanilic acid ( diazonium salt)
- bilirubin react with diazotized sulfanilic acid to produce azobilirubin (red-purple / pink)
- $\text{Bilirubin} + \text{Diazotized sulfanilic acid} \rightarrow \text{Azobilirubin (colored)}$
- The intensity of the azobilirubin color is measured in a spectrophotometer (commonly at **540–560 nm**, depending on the specific method), and is **directly proportional** to the bilirubin concentration in the sample
    

### Total vs direct (conjugated) bilirubin in diazo methods

- **Direct (conjugated) bilirubin:**
    - Conjugated bilirubin is water‑soluble and reacts **directly** with diazotized sulfanilic acid in acidic medium **without any accelerator**.
- **Total bilirubin:**
    - Unconjugated bilirubin is bound to albumin and not water‑soluble, so it needs an **accelerator** (e.g., **caffeine–sodium benzoate**, methanol, or surfactants) to release and solubilize it.
    - In the presence of an accelerator, **both conjugated and unconjugated bilirubin** react with the diazo reagent to form azobilirubin, giving the **total** value.
- **Indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin** is then calculated as: $\text{Indirect bilirubin} = \text{Total bilirubin} - \text{Direct bilirubin}$

### Common diazo-based methods

Two widely used diazo methods you’ll see in textbooks and lab manuals:

- **Evelyn–Malloy method**
    
    - Uses **methanol** as accelerator.
    - Measures red–purple azobilirubin at about **560 nm**
- **Jendrassik–Grof method** (very common in clinical labs)
    - Uses **caffeine–sodium benzoate** as accelerator for total bilirubin.
    - Often measures a blue/green azobilirubin complex in alkaline medium around **600 nm** (or sometimes 546–560 nm depending on the kit)

# 3. Proteins and Coagulation

The liver is the sole site of synthesis for major plasma proteins like albumin and clotting factors.

## 3.a Serum Proteins and A:G Ratio

* **Total Protein (Normal: 5.0–8.5 g/dL):** Measured via the ==**Biuret method** (cupric ions react with protein in alkaline medium to form a violet complex at 540 nm==).
* **Albumin (Normal: ==1;;3.5–5.0== g/dL):** Measured via the ==1;;Bromocresol Green (BCG) method== (turns from yellow-green to blue-green at 630 nm).
* **Albumin-Globulin (A:G) Ratio (Normal: 1.2–1.5):** In chronic liver disease, albumin synthesis drops, leading to a reversed ratio (<1.0).

## 3.b Prothrombin Time (PT) and INR

* **Function:** Measures the extrinsic pathway of coagulation, reflecting the synthesis of Factor II (Prothrombin).  
* **Standardization:** The **International Normalized Ratio (INR)** is used to standardize PT results across laboratories.  
* **Clinical Note:** Prolonged PT/High INR indicates severe liver synthetic failure.

# 4. Liver Enzymes

Enzymes are released into the bloodstream following hepatocyte injury or bile duct irritation.

| Enzyme         | Specificity & Significance                                                                             | Normal Range       |
| :------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------- |
| **ALT (SGPT)** | High specificity for liver cell damage; primary marker for viral hepatitis.                            | ==1;;7–40 IU/L==   |
| **AST (SGOT)** | Present in liver, heart, and muscle; less specific than ALT.                                           | ==1;;8–40 IU/L==   |
| **ALP**        | Marker for bile duct obstruction (cholestasis) or bone growth/disease.                                 | ==1;;30-120 IU/L== |
| **GGT**        | Highly sensitive marker for bile duct dysfunction and alcohol-induced injury. alcoholism , fatty liver | 0–42 IU/L          |
| **LDH**        | Non-specific marker for generalized tissue damage.                                                     | Variable           |

## 4.a Measurement Principles

### a. ALT (Alanine Aminotransferase): 
L-alanine and alpha-ketoglutarate are converted to pyruvate and L-glutamate by ALT.
   
$$
\text{L-Alanine} + \alpha\text{-Ketoglutarate} \xrightleftharpoons[\text{PLP}]{\text{ALT}} \text{Pyruvate} + \text{L-Glutamate}
$$
The resulting pyruvate is then reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) while oxidizing NADH to NAD+.
The rate of decrease in absorbance at 340 nm is directly proportional to ALT activity.

### b. AST (Aspartate Aminotransferase): 
 L-aspartate and alpha-ketoglutarate are converted to oxaloacetate and L-glutamate by AST.   
 $$
\text{L-Aspartate} + \alpha\text{-Ketoglutarate} \xrightleftharpoons[\text{PLP}]{\text{AST}} \text{Oxaloacetate} + \text{L-Glutamate}
$$
The resulting oxaloacetate is then reduced to malate by malate dehydrogenase (MDH) while oxidizing NADH to NAD+.
$$
\text{Oxaloacetate} + \text{NADH} + \text{H}^+ \xrightleftharpoons{\text{MDH}} \text{L-Malate} + \text{NAD}^+
$$
The rate of decrease in absorbance at 340 nm is directly proportional to AST activity. 


### c. Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP): 
Kinetic method using p-nitrophenylphosphate at 405 nm.

**ALP Isoenzymes**
Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) isoenzymes are tissue-specific forms of the ALP enzyme that help pinpoint the origin of elevated serum ALP—most commonly liver, bone, intestine, or placenta


Total ALP elevation is common but non-specific. Isoenzyme fractionation tells you **which tissue** is driving the rise, guiding differential diagnosis:

- **Liver (biliary) ALP** – typically elevated in **cholestasis**, biliary obstruction, infiltrative liver disease, and some hepatic tumors. 
- **Bone ALP** – elevated with high osteoblastic activity: **Paget disease**, healing fractures, osteomalacia/rickets, hyperparathyroidism, and osteoblastic metastases. 
- **Intestinal ALP** – can rise after fatty meals and in some GI/hepatic conditions; often a smaller contributor in fasting serum.
- **Placental ALP** – normal in **pregnancy** (especially 3rd trimester); can also appear as tumor-associated “Regan isoenzyme.”
- **Germ cell/Regan (Nagao) isoenzymes** – classically associated with certain **malignancies** (e.g., testicular germ cell tumors, some carcinomas).

 **Laboratory methods used**
- **Electrophoresis** (classic method to resolve liver, bone, intestinal, placental bands).
- **Isoenzyme-selective inhibition/chemical differentiation** (e.g., heat stability, inhibitor sensitivity such as L-phenylalanine for placental-like forms).
- **Immunoassays** specific for bone ALP or placental ALP when targeted questions are needed.

# V. Clinical Summary for Examinations

## Differential Diagnosis via AST/ALT Ratio

* **Viral Hepatitis:** ALT \> AST.  
* **Alcoholic Hepatitis:** AST \> ALT.

## **Key Specimen Collection Rules**

* **Tube Selection:** Serum (Red/Yellow top) is generally preferred. **Never use EDTA** (Lavender top) for LFT as it chelates ions required for enzyme activity.  
* **Patient Status:** Fasting may be required for associated lipid profiles, but not strictly for basic transaminases.
