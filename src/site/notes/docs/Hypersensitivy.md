## 1. Introduction and Classification Overview
Hypersensitivity is defined as an exaggerated or inappropriate immune response that causes tissue damage, disease, or inflammation. 
- Foreign antigens
- Environmental allergens
- Drugs
- Sometimes self-antigens

 **Gell and Coombs Classification system**

| Type         | Name                     | Primary Immune Components                 |
| ------------ | ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- |
| **Type I**   | Immediate / Anaphylactic | ==1;;IgE antibodies, Mast cells==         |

## 2. Type I Hypersensitivity: Immediate / Anaphylactic

 rapid allergic responses,  within minutes of exposure.


- **Primary Mediators:** IgE antibodies, mast cells, and basophils.
- **Chemical Mediators:** Histamine, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins.


### Biphasic Mechanism
1. **Sensitization Phase:** Initial exposure triggers B cells to produce allergen-specific IgE. These antibodies bind to **high-affinity Fc receptors** on the surface of mast cells and basophils, sensitizing the individual.
2. **Effector Phase:** Upon re-exposure, the allergen cross-links the IgE already bound to the mast cells, triggering explosive degranulation and the release of inflammatory mediators.

### Physiological Effects of Histamine

- Vasodilation (widening of blood vessels).
- Increased vascular permeability (edema).
- Bronchoconstriction (narrowing of airways).
- Increased mucus secretion.
- Pruritus (itching).

### Clinical Manifestations & Anaphylaxis

Common conditions include allergic rhinitis, bronchial asthma, urticaria (hives), and food allergies.

**Systemic Anaphylaxis:** A life-threatening emergency characterized by hypotension, shock, laryngeal edema, and bronchospasm.

- **Urgent Protocol:** Immediate administration of **Adrenaline (Epinephrine)**, followed by antihistamines and corticosteroids to manage the late-phase response.

**what is schultz dale phenomenon** :: The Schultz-Dale phenomenon is a laboratory technique that demonstrates Type I (immediate) hypersensitivity reactions by showing that sensitized smooth muscle contracts when exposed to a specific antigen.


## 3. Type II Hypersensitivity: Cytotoxic / Antibody-Mediated

Type II reactions involve IgG or IgM antibodies reacting against antigens present on the host's cell surfaces or fixed tissue components.

### Mechanisms of Cell Damage

1. **Complement-mediated lysis:** Activation of the classical pathway leads to the assembly of the Membrane Attack Complex (MAC), causing osmotic lysis.
2. **Opsonization and phagocytosis:** C3b and IgG coat the target cell, marking it for destruction by phagocytes.
3. **Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC):** NK cells, macrophages, and neutrophils bind to the antibody-coated cell and release perforins/granzymes to induce apoptosis.

### Clinical Examples
?
- Mismatched blood transfusion reactions.
- Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn (HDN) / Rhesus incompatibility.
- Goodpasture syndrome (antibodies attacking the glomerular basement membrane).
- Autoimmune hemolytic anemia and drug-induced hemolytic anemia.
- **grave's disease**

**Laboratory Alert:**  **Direct Antiglobulin Test (DAT)** 

## 4. Type III Hypersensitivity: Immune Complex-Mediated

Type III reactions are caused by the deposition of soluble, free-floating antigen-antibody complexes.

### Pathogenesis and Inflammatory Cascade

Soluble complexes form in the circulation and, rather than being cleared, they settle into tissues. This deposition often occurs in areas of high-pressure filtration, such as the **glomeruli of the kidneys (basement membrane filtration)**, joint spaces, and blood vessel walls.

1. **Complex Deposition:** Complexes settle into tissue structures.
2. **Complement Activation:** This triggers the release of anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a.
3. **Neutrophil Recruitment:** C5a attracts neutrophils which attempt to phagocytize the deposited complexes.
4. **Frustrated Phagocytosis:** Neutrophils release lysosomal enzymes and reactive oxygen species, causing significant local tissue damage.

?
- ==Serum Sickness and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE).(chronic autoimmune, widespread inflammation), Arthus reaction(buildup of immune complexes under the skin)==

## 5. Type IV Hypersensitivity: Delayed-Type / Cell-Mediated

Type IV is the only classification mediated entirely by T-lymphocytes (CD4+ and CD8+ cells) rather than antibodies.

### Mechanism & Kinetics

- **Mechanism:** Sensitized T cells release cytokines that activate macrophages and recruit cytotoxic T cells, leading to cellular injury and granuloma formation.
- **Onset:** Delayed reaction, appearing **24–72 hours** after exposure.
- **Laboratory Distinction:** This reaction **cannot be transferred via serum**; passive transfer is only possible through the transfer of T-lymphocytes.

### Clinical Examples
?
- Tuberculin (Mantoux) skin test.
- Allergic contact dermatitis (e.g., nickel or poison ivy).
- Granulomatous inflammation (leprosy([[docs/Lepromin Test\|Lepromin Test]]), tuberculosis).
- **Transplant rejection** and Type 1 diabetes.

**Laboratory Alert:** Because of the 24–72 hour delay, patients must return to the clinic for a manual reading of the Mantoux test. A negative result at 24 hours does not rule out a reaction.

## 6. Type V Hypersensitivity: Antibody-Mediated Stimulatory

While many authors classify these under Type II, Type V is frequently segregated into its own subcategory as the mechanism does not destroy the target cell, but rather alters its function.

### Mechanism

Antibodies bind to cell-surface receptors and either **mimic the effects of the natural ligand** (stimulating the cell) or **prevent the intended ligand from binding** (inhibiting/blocking signaling).

### Clinical Examples

- **Graves’ Disease (Stimulatory):** Antibodies bind to and stimulate the TSH receptor, inducing hyperthyroidism.
- **Myasthenia Gravis (Inhibitory/Blocking):** Antibodies bind to Acetylcholine receptors (AChR), preventing nerve impulses from reaching the muscle, leading to muscle weakness.

# Shwartzman rexn
### What is the Shwartzman Reaction?
- **Pathogenesis:** It is an **endotoxin-induced phenomenon** rather than an adaptive antigen-antibody or T-cell-mediated immune response.
- **Mechanism:** It occurs when an initial (sensitizing) dose of bacterial endotoxin (such as Gram-negative lipopolysaccharide) is followed 24 hours later by a second (provoking) intravenous or intradermal dose.
- **Clinical Effect:** This causes localized microvascular occlusion, intravascular coagulation, neutrophil infiltration, and localized or systemic tissue necrosis.
