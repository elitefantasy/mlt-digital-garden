# Chromatography

Introduction and Definition
* **Definition:** Chromatography is a **chemical analysis laboratory technique** used to separate a mixture into its individual components. 

parts of chromatography
- sample or analyte
- stationary phase
- mobile phase
- chromatography column
- detector

#### Principle and Purpose
* **Basic Principle:** different compounds within a mixture move at different rates or speeds depending on their specific nature.  
* **Purpose:** This technique is primarily used to **separate, identify, and quantify** the individual components of a mixture.

#### The Two Phases of Chromatography
Every chromatographic system relies on the interaction between two distinct phases:

1. **Stationary Phase:**  
	1. This phase remains fixed in one place and **does not move** during the process.  
	2. It acts as a support for the sample.  
	3. It can be either a **solid or a liquid** (e.g., gels).  
	4. *Common examples:* Silica gel, alumina, filter paper, columns, and thin-layer plates.  
2. **Mobile Phase:**  
	1. This phase **moves through the stationary phase**, conveying the sample or mixture along with it to help separate the components.  
	2. It is **always a fluid**, meaning it can be either a **liquid or a gas**.  
	3. *Common examples:* Water, liquid solvents, and gases like helium or nitrogen. The choice of mobile phase depends entirely on the type of chromatography being used.

#### Factors Influencing Separation
* The type of **stationary phase**  
* The type of **mobile phase**  
* The **flow rate** of the mobile phase  
* The **temperature**

## Types of Chromatography

### Paper Chromatography
- **Paper chromatography** is a type of **partition chromatography** in which the **stationary phase** is water held in the cellulose fibers of special filter paper, and the **mobile phase** is a solvent (or solvent mixture) that moves through the paper by **capillary action**.

#### Principle
- Separation is based mainly on **differential partition** of components between:
    - **Stationary phase:** water adsorbed on cellulose (polar).
    - **Mobile phase:** organic/aqueous solvent moving up/down the paper.
- Components with **higher affinity for the mobile phase** (more soluble in solvent, less polar) move **faster** and travel farther.
- Components with **higher affinity for the stationary phase** (more polar, more hydrogen bonding with water/cellulose) move **slower** and stay closer to the origin.

#### Basic setup and materials
- **Stationary phase:**
    - Special **chromatography paper** (Whatman filter paper, etc.) made of pure cellulose
- **Mobile phase:**
    - Suitable **solvent or solvent mixture** (e.g., butanol–acetic acid–water for amino acids; various organic–aqueous systems for sugars, pigments, drugs).
- **Chamber (tank):**
    - Closed container saturated with solvent vapour to prevent evaporation and ensure uniform movement.
- **Sample application:**
    - Small spot or narrow line applied near one end of the paper (1–2 cm from edge) using a **capillary tube** or micropipette.

#### General procedure (step‑wise)
1. **Select paper and solvent system** according to the sample (amino acids, sugars, pigments, etc.).
2. **Cut paper** to suitable size; draw a light **origin line** with pencil (1–2 cm from bottom edge).
3. **Apply sample** as a small, concentrated spot/line on the origin using a capillary tube; allow to dry.
4. **Prepare chamber:** pour solvent into the bottom; line the chamber with filter paper if needed; close and allow vapour saturation
5. **Place paper** in the chamber so that the lower edge dips into the solvent, but the **sample spot is above the solvent level**
6. **Development:** solvent moves through the paper by capillary action, carrying sample components at different rates → separation into distinct spots/bands.
7. When the **solvent front** reaches near the top (or desired distance), remove the paper and **mark the solvent front** immediately.
8. **Dry the chromatogram.**
9. **Visualize** spots:
    - Naturally colored compounds: visible directly.
    - Colorless compounds: use **UV light**, **ninhydrin** (amino acids → purple), **iodine vapour**, **anisaldehyde**, **sulfuric acid charring**, etc.
10. **Measure distances** and calculate **Rf values**.

---

#### Rf value (retention factor)
- **Definition:**

    R_f = $\frac{\text{Distance travelled by the substance (from origin)}}{\text{Distance travelled by the solvent front (from origin)}}$
- **Key points:**
    - Rf is **unitless** and always between **0 and 1**.
    - Depends on:
        - **Solvent system** used
        - **Temperature**
        - Type of **paper**
        - Nature of the **compound** (polarity, size, functional groups)
    - For good separation:
        - Ideal Rf of sample: **0.05–0.85**.
        - Minimum difference between Rf values of two components: **≥ 0.05**.
    - Used for **identification** by comparing with standards run on the same plate under identical conditions.

---

#### Types of paper chromatography (by direction of solvent flow)

1) Ascending paper chromatography
	- Solvent moves **upwards** from the bottom of the paper by capillary action.
	- Paper is suspended vertically; lower edge dips in solvent; sample spot above solvent level.
	- **Advantages:** simple setup, commonly used in teaching and routine analysis.
	- **Disadvantages:** slower; limited length of travel.
2) Descending paper chromatography
	- Solvent moves **downwards** from a trough at the top of the chamber; gravity assists capillary action.
	- Paper hangs from a solvent reservoir at the top; solvent flows down over the sample.
	- **Advantages:** faster development; longer run possible.
	- **Disadvantages:** more complex setup; risk of uneven flow if not properly arranged.
3) Radial (circular) paper chromatography
	- A circular filter paper is used; sample is spotted at the **center**.
	- Solvent is introduced at the center (via wick) and moves **outwards radially**, forming concentric rings of separated components.
	- **Uses:** quick qualitative analysis; demonstration of separation of dyes/pigments.
4) Two‑dimensional (2D) paper chromatography
	- Rectangular paper is used.
	- First run: develop in **one solvent** in one direction (e.g., ascending).
	- Dry the paper, rotate it by **90°**, then develop in a **second solvent** in the perpendicular direction.
	- **Use:** separates complex mixtures where components have **similar Rf values** in a single solvent system (e.g., amino acid mixtures, metabolic extracts).


#### 7) Factors affecting separation (high‑yield for MCQs/short notes)
- **Nature of solvent system:** polarity, composition, ratio of solvents.
- **Nature of stationary phase:** type of paper, thickness, water content, fiber structure.
- **Amount of sample applied:** too much → tailing and poor resolution.
- **Temperature:** affects solubility, evaporation, and Rf values.
- **Saturation of chamber:** insufficient saturation → uneven solvent front, poor reproducibility.
- **Distance of origin from solvent:** spot must not touch solvent directly.

#### Applications (especially for medical/biochemistry exams)
- **Amino acid analysis:**

    - Separation and identification of amino acids in urine, blood, hydrolyzed proteins (e.g., in metabolic disorders like **phenylketonuria**, **maple syrup urine disease**)
- **Carbohydrate analysis:**
    - Separation of mono‑, di‑, and oligosaccharides in biological fluids and food samples
- **Pigment separation:**
    - Chlorophylls, carotenoids, xanthophylls in plant extracts (classic lab example)
- **Drug and metabolite screening:**
    - Qualitative detection of drugs, metabolites, and toxins in research and forensic contexts (now often replaced by TLC/HPLC but conceptually important)
- **Purity check:**
    - Detecting impurities in pharmaceuticals and biochemicals.


### Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC)
- **Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC)** is a **solid–liquid adsorption (or partition) chromatographic technique** in which a **thin layer of adsorbent** (e.g., silica gel or alumina) coated on a **glass, plastic, or aluminum plate** acts as the **stationary phase**, and a **liquid solvent (mobile phase)** moves through the layer by **capillary action** to separate mixture components.

#### Principle
- TLC works mainly on the principle of **adsorption** (sometimes combined with partition).
- **Stationary phase:** polar solid adsorbent (commonly **silica gel, SiO₂**, or **alumina, Al₂O₃**) with surface –OH groups that form **hydrogen bonds**, **dipole–dipole**, and **van der Waals** interactions with analytes.
- **Mobile phase:** organic solvent or solvent mixture (the **eluent**) that moves up the plate.
- Components with:
    - **Stronger affinity for stationary phase** → more adsorbed → move **slower** → lower Rf.
    - **Stronger affinity for mobile phase** (more soluble, less polar) → move **faster** → higher Rf.

#### Materials and setup
- **TLC plates:**
    - Glass/plastic/aluminum sheets coated with a thin, uniform layer of **silica gel G/GF₂₅₄**, **alumina**, or other adsorbents.
    - “GF₂₅₄” indicates a **fluorescent indicator** for UV visualization.
- **Mobile phase (eluent):**
    - Chosen based on polarity of analytes (e.g., hexane–ethyl acetate, chloroform–methanol, toluene–acetone).
- **Developing chamber:**
    - Closed jar/tank lined with filter paper and saturated with solvent vapour to ensure uniform movement.

#### Procedure (step‑wise)
1. **Select plate and solvent system** according to sample polarity.
2. **Draw origin line** lightly with pencil ~1 cm from the bottom of the plate.
3. **Apply sample** as a small spot (or narrow band) on the origin using a **capillary tube** or micropipette; allow to dry.
4. **Prepare chamber:** pour solvent to a shallow depth; saturate chamber with solvent vapour.
5. **Place plate** in chamber so that the lower edge dips in solvent, but the **spot is above solvent level**.
6. **Development:** solvent rises by capillary action, carrying components at different rates → separation into distinct spots.
7. When solvent front nears the top, **remove plate** and immediately **mark the solvent front**.
8. **Dry the plate.**
9. **Visualize spots:**
    - **UV light (254 nm / 365 nm)** for UV‑active or fluorescent compounds.
    - **Iodine vapour** → brown spots (reversible).
    - Chemical reagents: **ninhydrin** (amino acids → purple), **anisaldehyde**, **vanillin–sulfuric acid**, **KMnO₄**, etc.
10. **Measure distances** and calculate **Rf values**.

#### Rf value (retention factor)
- **Formula:**
$$R_f = \frac{\text{Distance travelled by substance (from origin)}}{\text{Distance travelled by solvent front (from origin)}}$$


#### Types of TLC (by development mode)
- **Ascending TLC:** solvent moves **upwards** from bottom (most common).
- **Descending TLC:** solvent flows **downwards** from a top reservoir (less common).
- **Two‑dimensional (2D) TLC:**
    - First run in one solvent in one direction; dry; rotate plate 90°; run in a second solvent.
    - Used for **complex mixtures** with overlapping Rf values.

#### Factors affecting separation
- **Polarity of solvent system** (major factor).
- **Nature of adsorbent** (silica vs alumina; particle size; layer thickness).
- **Amount of sample applied** (overloading → tailing, poor resolution).
- **Temperature and chamber saturation** (affect solvent front and Rf reproducibility).
- **Distance of origin from solvent** (spot must not dip into solvent).[

- drug detection in urine, blood

## Detection and Output (Chromatogram)

* Chromatography systems utilize **detectors** that record the rate of movement, often referred to as the elution time or difference time.  
* These detectors generate a **chromatogram**, which is a graphical representation that correlates signal intensity with time.  
* This graph provides highly valuable data regarding the **composition and concentration** of the sample.
