A vaccine is a biological preparation that helps the body's immune system build protection against a specific disease.

## Vaccine contains
A vaccine generally contains:
- **Antigen** → produces an immune response ⭐
- **Adjuvant** → enhances the immune response
- **Stabilizer** → maintains vaccine stability
- **Preservative** → prevents contamination in some multidose vaccines
- **Diluent** → used to reconstitute or dilute the vaccine
- **Residual manufacturing components** → may be present in very small amounts

> The exact components vary according to the type of vaccine.

---



## How Vaccine Works

The basic mechanism can be summarized as:

```text
Vaccine administration
        ↓
Introduction of vaccine antigen
        ↓
Antigen uptake by antigen-presenting cells
        ↓
Antigen presentation to T lymphocytes
        ↓
Activation of B cells + T cells
        ↓
Plasma cells produce antibodies
        ↓
Memory B cells + Memory T cells formed
        ↓
Later exposure to actual pathogen
        ↓
Rapid and strong secondary immune response
        ↓
Protection from disease
```

### ⭐ Important point

A vaccine **does not usually provide immediate immunity**. It stimulates the adaptive immune system to develop **immunological memory**.

## Types of Vaccines

### 1. Live Attenuated Vaccine
#### Mechanism
Contains a **live microorganism that has been weakened (attenuated)** so that it usually does not cause disease in healthy individuals.

The attenuated organism can replicate to a limited extent and produces a strong, long-lasting immune response.

It stimulates:
- Antibody response
- T-cell response
- Memory cells

#### Examples
?
- **BCG**
- **MMR**
- **Varicella**
- **Yellow fever**
- **Rotavirus**
- **Oral polio vaccine (OPV)**
- Live attenuated influenza vaccine

#### Advantages
- Produces **strong immune response** ⭐
- Produces both **humoral and cellular immunity**
- Often provides **long-lasting immunity**
- Fewer doses/boosters may be required for some vaccines

#### Disadvantages
- Generally unsuitable for many **severely immunocompromised** individuals.
- Requires careful storage/cold chain.
- Rarely, attenuation can be associated with reversion or vaccine-associated disease for particular vaccines.
- Generally avoided during pregnancy for several live vaccines.

### 2. Inactivated Vaccine

#### Mechanism
Contains a microorganism that has been **killed/inactivated** so that it cannot replicate or cause infection.

The antigen is recognized by the immune system and stimulates mainly a **humoral antibody response**.

#### Examples
?
- **Inactivated polio vaccine (IPV)**
- **Hepatitis A vaccine**
- **Rabies vaccine**
- **Inactivated influenza vaccine**
- Whole-cell inactivated **pertussis** vaccine

#### Advantages
- Cannot replicate in the host.
- **Cannot cause infection from replication of the vaccine organism.**
- Generally safer than live vaccines for immunocompromised people.
- Relatively stable.

#### Disadvantages
- Produces a weaker immune response than many live vaccines.
- Mainly produces **humoral immunity**.
- Usually requires **multiple doses and booster doses**.
- Adjuvants may be required.

---

### 3. Toxoid Vaccine
#### Mechanism
Contains a **toxin that has been chemically or physically inactivated** but retains its antigenic properties.

The immune system produces **antitoxin antibodies**.

```text
Toxin → Inactivation → Toxoid
                     ↓
              Antibody production
                     ↓
              Toxin neutralization
```

#### Examples
?
- **Diphtheria toxoid**
- **Tetanus toxoid**

#### Advantages
- Very effective against **toxin-mediated diseases**.
- Canot cause toxicity from the original active toxin.
- Produces protective antitoxin antibodies.
- Well-established vaccine technology.

#### Disadvantages
- Does **not provide immunity against the organism itself**; mainly protects against its toxin.
- Booster doses are generally required.
- Adjuvants are often used.
- Local reactions may occur.

---

### 4. Subunit Vaccine
#### Mechanism
Contains only **specific antigenic components** of a microorganism rather than the whole organism.

The selected antigen stimulates B and T cell responses and produces protective antibodies.

#### Examples
- **Acellular pertussis vaccine**
- Some **hepatitis B** vaccines
- Some influenza vaccines
- Recombinant zoster vaccine contains a subunit antigen

#### Advantages
- No whole live organism.
- Generally **safer and well tolerated**.
- Cannot cause infection by replication.
- Contains only selected antigenic components.

#### Disadvantages
- May produce a weaker immune response than live vaccines.
- **Adjuvants and booster doses** may be needed.
- Requires identification of suitable protective antigens.
- Antigenic variation can reduce effectiveness.

### 5. Recombinant Vaccine
#### Mechanism
A gene coding for a desired antigen is inserted into a suitable **expression system** such as yeast or cultured cells.

The antigen is produced, purified and used as the vaccine.

```text
Antigen gene
     ↓
Inserted into expression system
     ↓
Antigen production
     ↓
Purification
     ↓
Vaccination
     ↓
Immune response
```

#### Examples
?
- **Recombinant hepatitis B vaccine** ⭐
- **Recombinant HPV vaccines**
- Recombinant zoster vaccine

#### Advantages
- Does not require the whole pathogen.
- **Good safety profile**.
- Prduces a highly purified, specific antigen.
- Manufacturing can be standardized.

#### Disadvantage
- Production can be technically complex.
- Often requires **adjuvants**.
- Booster doses may be required.
- Some recombinant antigens may be less immunogenic than whole organisms.
    

> **Important:** Recombinant vaccines are often also **subunit vaccines**. "Recombinant" describes **how the antigen is produced**, whereas "subunit" describes the fact that only a component of the pathogen is used.

---

### 6. Conjugate Vaccine
#### Mechanism
A **polysaccharide antigen** from a bacterium is chemically linked (**conjugated**) to a **protein carrier**.

The protein carrier converts a poorly immunogenic polysaccharide response into a more effective **T-cell-dependent immune response**.

```text
Polysaccharide antigen
        +
Protein carrier
        ↓
Conjugate vaccine
        ↓
T-cell help
        ↓
Strong B-cell response
        ↓
IgG + Memory B cells
```

#### Examples
- **Hib vaccine** ⭐
- **Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV)**
- **Meningococcal conjugate vaccines**

#### Advantage
- Produces strong **IgG antibody response**.
- Produces **immunological memory**.
- Effective in **young children**, unlike plain polysaccharide vaccines.
- Can reduce nasopharyngeal carriage for some vaccines, contributing to herd protection.

#### Disadvantage
- More expensive/complex to manufacture than simple polysaccharide vaccines.
- Requires conjugation technology.
- Protection is directed against the specific included serotypes/antigens.
    

---

### 7. mRNA Vaccine

#### Mechanism
Contains **messenger RNA (mRNA)** encoding a pathogen antigen.
After administration:

```text
mRNA vaccine
     ↓
mRNA enters host cells
     ↓
Cells produce antigen
     ↓
Antigen presented to immune system
     ↓
B-cell + T-cell activation
     ↓
Antibodies + Memory cells
     ↓
Protection
```

The mRNA **does not need to enter the nucleus** to produce the antigen and does not alter the host's DNA.

#### Examples
?
- **Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine**
- **Moderna COVID-19 vaccine**

#### Advantages
- Rapid vaccine development and redesign.
- Does not require production of whole infectious organisms.
- Produces both **antibody and cellular immune responses**.
- No need for viral-vector replication.
#### Disadvantages
- Some formulations require stringent **cold-chain/storage conditions**.
- Reactogenicity such as fever, fatigue and injection-site pain can occur.
- Protection may decrease over time, so booster doses may be needed.
- Long-term platform experience is more limited than for traditional vaccine technologies.

---

### 8. Viral Vector Vaccine

#### Mechanism
Uses a **modified, non-disease-causing viral vector** to deliver the gene encoding an antigen into host cells.

The host cells produce the antigen, which stimulates the immune system.

```text
Viral vector containing antigen gene
              ↓
        Enters host cell
              ↓
       Antigen production
              ↓
    B-cell + T-cell activation
              ↓
       Immune memory
```

#### Examples
- **Oxford–AstraZeneca COVID-19 vaccine**
- **Janssen COVID-19 vaccine**
- **Ebola vaccines** using viral-vector platforms


### 9. DNA Vaccine

#### Mechanism
Contains a **DNA plasmid** encoding the antigen.

```text
DNA plasmid
    ↓
Enters host cells
    ↓
DNA → mRNA
    ↓
mRNA → Antigen
    ↓
Antigen presentation
    ↓
B-cell + T-cell response
    ↓
Memory cells
```

Unlike mRNA vaccines, the DNA must reach the **nucleus** for transcription.

#### Examples
- **ZyCoV-D** — DNA COVID-19 vaccine developed in India.
- Several DNA vaccines have been developed for veterinary use and are under investigation for various human diseases.
