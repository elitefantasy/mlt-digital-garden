### Reproduction
**Asexual Reproduction**
- Binary fission — splitting into two.

### life cycle
- **Trophozoite:** Vegetative stage; motile, feeding, and sensitive to environmental changes.
- **Cyst:** Protective stage; has a thick wall, is non-motile, and is the infective stage for humans.
- _Encystation:_ Transformation of a trophozoite into a cyst
- _Excystation:_ Transformation of a cyst back into an active trophozoite

### infective stages 

#### **1. Amoebae**
*   ***Entamoeba histolytica***: **Mature quadrinucleate cyst** (ingested via contaminated food or water) 
*   ***Entamoeba coli*** *(non-pathogenic)*: **Mature 8-nucleated cyst** (ingested via the fecal-oral route) 
*   ***Naegleria fowleri*** *(free-living amoeba)*: **Trophozoite** (enters the central nervous system via the nasal mucosa during swimming/diving in warm fresh water).
*   ***Acanthamoeba* species**: **Both Trophozoites and Cysts** can cause infection (enters via damaged cornea/contact lens use, skin, or respiratory tract).
*   ***Entamoeba gingivalis***: **Trophozoite** (lacks a cyst stage; transmitted directly via oral contact/saliva).

---

### **2. Flagellates**
*   ***Giardia lamblia***: **Mature quadrinucleate cyst** (ingested via contaminated water or food; as few as 10 cysts can establish infection) 
*   ***Trichomonas vaginalis***: **Trophozoite** (lacks a cyst stage entirely; transmitted via direct sexual contact) 
*   ***Leishmania donovani*** *(and other Leishmania spp.)*: **Promastigote** (flagellated form injected into human skin by the female sandfly vector, *Phlebotomus*)
*   ***Trypanosoma brucei*** *(African Sleeping Sickness)*: **Metacyclic trypomastigote** (injected into humans by the bite of the Tsetse fly, *Glossina*) 
*   ***Trypanosoma cruzi*** *(Chagas Disease)*: **Metacyclic trypomastigote** (passed in feces of the Reduviid / Triatomine / Kissing bug and rubbed into the bite wound or mucous membranes) 

---

### **3. Ciliates**
*   ***Balantidium coli***: **Cyst** (ingested via food or water contaminated with pig or human feces) 

---

### **4. Sporozoans / Apicomplexa**
*   ***Plasmodium* species** (*P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. malariae, P. ovale*):
    *   **Infective stage for humans**: **Sporozoite** (injected by the female *Anopheles* mosquito during a blood meal) 
    *   **Infective stage for mosquito**: **Gametocyte** (ingested from human blood during a bite) 
*   ***Toxoplasma gondii***: All three morphological stages can be infectious to humans depending on the transmission route 
    1. **Mature Sporulated Oocyst** (ingested from cat feces/soil/water)
    2. **Tissue Cyst containing Bradyzoites** (ingested in raw or undercooked infected meat)
    3. **Tachyzoite** (transplacental/congenital transmission from mother to fetus) 
*   ***Cryptosporidium parvum* / *C. hominis***: **Mature sporulated Oocyst** containing 4 sporozoites (ingested via contaminated food/water) . *(Note: Thin-walled oocysts cause autoinfection within the same host) .*

#### Life Cycle & Transmission
 * ingestion of quadrinucleate cyst
 * travel to stomach and then to
 * excystation: occurs in small intestine , cyst wall breakdown , amoeboid form is released
 * the released form develop into trophozoite -> migrate to large intestine
 * Trophozoites multiply by **binary fission**. They remain in the intestinal lumen in many individuals (dormant)
 * **Invasive stage**
	- Some trophozoites invade the **intestinal mucosa**.
	- They produce ==flask-shaped== ulcers
	- This can cause **amoebic dysentery/amoebic colitis**.
- **Extraintestinal spread**
	- In some cases, trophozoites enter the **portal circulation**.
	- They can reach the **liver** and cause an **amoebic liver abscess**.
	- Rarely, other organs such as the lungs or brain may be involved.
 * **Course of Colonization**:
	* Asymptomatic (90%): no invasion ,  encyst under unfavorable conditions, and pass into feces as cysts (carriers).
	* Symptomatic/Invasive (10%): Trophozoites invade the mucosa, producing ulcerations, dysentery, and potential hematogenous spread.
-  **Encystment**
    - In the colon, some trophozoites stop feeding and become **precystic forms**.
    - They become rounded and form a cyst wall.
-  **Cyst maturation**
    - The nucleus divides:
    - **1 nucleus → 2 nuclei → 4 nuclei**
    - The **mature quadrinucleate cyst** is formed.
-  **Cysts passed in stool**
	- Mature cysts are passed in **formed stool**.
	- They survive outside the body better than trophozoites.
	- Another person becomes infected by ingesting these cysts.

- **Microscopy & Staining**
	* **Direct Stool Examination**: 
		* saline mount: motile trophozoite
		* iodine mount: cysts nuclei and glycogen mass
	* rbcs, pus cells, charcot leyden crystals
	* formal ether concentration for low parasite loads
 * **Staining Properties:**
	* Trichrome stain:  cyst and trophozoite morphology.
	* Iron hematoxylin: Nuclei and chromatoid bodies stain dark blue/black; glycogen remains clear.
	* Iodine wet mount: Glycogen turns golden brown, nuclei/karyosome bright yellow, chromatoid bodies remain unstained.

### Flagellate | Giardia Lambila
[[docs/protozoa#Classification\|protozoa#Classification]]
- First identified by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek
- Only Parasite which Affects Small Intestine
- Leading cause of traveler's' diarrhea
- Primary Habitat: Human small intestine
- Specific Location: Duodenum and upper jejunum
- giardia does not spread via bloodstream and does not spread to other parts of GIT
- giardia trophozoite are anaerobe 
- giardia cyst are resistant to chlorination and ozonolysis 
![Pasted image 20260831194155.png\|423](/img/user/Attachments/Pasted%20image%2020260831194155.png)

Exist in Two Forms
- **Trophozoite:** Pear-shaped or **tennis-racket-shaped** ,  Contains **2 nuclei** (giving it a face-like appearance), 2 axostyles, and **4 pairs of flagella**. It moves forward in rapid, falling-leaf jerks 
- **Cyst:** Oval (8-12 um), containing **2 to 4 nuclei clustered at one end** and refractile, S-shaped fibrils (flagellar remnants) placed lengthwise

#### Life Cycle
has a **simple, direct life cycle** with **two main stages**: the **infective cyst** and the **multiplying trophozoite**. No intermediate host is required. 

1. **Infective stage and transmission**
	- **Mature cysts** are passed in the **feces** of an infected person or animal. 
	- Cysts are **environmentally resistant** and can survive for **weeks to months** in moist conditions (water, soil, surfaces).
	- Infection occurs by **ingestion of cysts**
	- As few as **10 cysts** can cause infection. 
2. **Excystation (in the host)**
	- After ingestion, cysts pass through the **stomach** and reach the **duodenum/proximal small intestine**. 
	- cyst -> excystation(cyst to trophozoite) releasing two trophozoite 
3. **Trophozoite stage (pathogenic, replicative)**
	- pear shaped, motile, flagellated, two nuclei, ventral sucking disck
	- attach to small intestin via ventral disc
	- binary fission multiplication 
	- cause dx like melabsorption , diarhea , bloating
	- trophozoite do not survive outside host cell
4. **Encystation (formation of cysts)**
	- secrete tough hyaline cyst wall
	- nucleus divide -> mature quadrinucleate cyst
	- cyst passed into stool

##### Clinical Features
- Asymptomatic Cases: Common in many infections. 
- Symptomatic Cases: Mucus-heavy diarrhea, fat malabsorption, epigastric discomfort, and flatulence. 
- Effects in Children: Chronic diarrhea, nutrient malabsorption, weight loss, and sprue-like syndrome. 
- Complications: Gallbladder colonization leading to biliary colic and jaundice. 
- Incubation Period: Typically, around two weeks, but variable.

##### ⭐ Most characteristic features
- **Pear/tear-drop shaped trophozoite** — very characteristic.
- **Bilateral (bilaterally) symmetrical** body.
- **Two nuclei** → gives a characteristic **“face-like” appearance**
- **Ventral sucking/adhesive disc** → attaches to the **duodenal mucosa**.
- **4 pairs of flagella = 8 flagella** → helps in motility.
- **No mitochondria** → anaerobic/microaerophilic organism with reduced mitochondria (mitosomes).
- **Cyst has 4 nuclei** when mature.
- Cyst is the **infective stage**.
- Transmission is mainly **fecal–oral**, especially through contaminated water.
- Causes **giardiasis**, commonly associated with **foul-smelling, greasy diarrhea and malabsorption**.

##### Laboratory Diagnosis of Giardiasis 
- Stool Examination: Identifies Giardia lamblia in stool samples. 
- Forms Detected: Cysts in formed stools; cysts & trophozoites in diarrheal stools. 
- Macroscopic Examination: Stool may have an offensive odor, a pale fatty appearance, and float in water. 
- Microscopic Examination: Uses wet preparations and concentration techniques for detection. 
- Asymptomatic Carriers: Typically show only cysts in stool samples.
- Concentration Technique : 
	- Flotation Preparation Zinc Sulphate Concentration Technique 
	- Sedimentation Technique Formalin Ethyl Acetate

#### A. Trophozoite
- large intestine
- motile, invasive, found in dysentric stool
- covered with cilia

![Pasted image 20260901191243.png\|356](/img/user/Attachments/Pasted%20image%2020260901191243.png)
- **Structure:** 
    - Anterior end is narrow; posterior end is broad.
    - **Anterior End:** Features a groove (_peristome_) leading to the mouth (_cytostome_) and a short funnel-shaped gullet (_cytopharynx_).
    - **Posterior End:** Has a small anal pore (_cytopyge_).
    - **Adoral Cilia:** Cilia surrounding the mouth are larger.
- **Nuclear Apparatus (Two Nuclei):**
    1. A large kidney-shaped **macronucleus**.
    2. A small **micronucleus** lying in the concavity of the macronucleus.
- **Cytoplasmic Contents:** Contains 1–2 contractile vacuoles and several food vacuoles.

#### B. Cyst
- cytoplasm of cyst is granular , and contains kidney shaped macronucleus and vacuole 
- **Shape & Size:** Spherical in shape, measuring 40–60 µm in diameter.
- **Cyst Wall:** Thick and transparent double-layered wall.
- **Contents:** Granular cytoplasm containing a macronucleus, micronucleus, and vacuoles.
- **Significance:** The **infective stage** of _B. coli_; found in chronic cases and healthy carriers.

#### 3. Life Cycle
- **Host Requirement:** Completes its life cycle in **one host only** (single-host life cycle).
    - **Natural Host:** Pig
    - **Accidental Host:** Man
    - **Reservoirs:** Pig, monkey, and rat
    - **Infective Form:** Cyst
- **Transmission:** Balantidiasis is a zoonosis. Humans acquire infection by ingesting food or water contaminated with feces containing cysts from pigs, animal reservoirs, or human carriers.
- **Excystation:** Occurs in the small intestine following cyst ingestion.
- **Development & Colonization:**
    - From each cyst, a **single trophozoite** is liberated, which migrates to the large intestine.
    - Trophozoites multiply in the large intestine by **transverse binary fission**.
    - Sexual reproduction occurs infrequently by **conjugation**, during which reciprocal exchange of nuclear material happens between two trophozoites enclosed within a single cyst wall.
- **Encystation:**
    - Occurs as trophozoites pass down the colon or in evacuated stool.
    - The cell rounds up and secretes a tough cyst wall.
    - Cysts remain viable in feces for **1 to 2 days** to contaminate food/water and spread to new hosts.
#### 4. Pathogenesis & Clinical Feature
- **Commensal Phase:** In healthy individuals, _B. coli_ lives as a lumen commensal and remains asymptomatic.
- **Predisposing Factors:** Disease develops when host resistance is lowered by:
    - Malnourishment
    - Alcoholism
    - Achlorhydria (absence of stomach acid)
    - Concurrent infection by _Trichuris trichiura_ or bacterial infections
- **Pathology:**
    - Trophozoites burrow into the intestinal mucosa, establish colonies, and cause an inflammatory reaction.
    - Forms **mucosal ulcers** and **submucosal abscesses** (resembling lesions in amebiasis).
    - **Key Distinction:** Unlike _Entamoeba histolytica_, _B. coli_ **does not** invade the liver or any other extraintestinal sites.
- **Clinical Features:**
    - Most cases are asymptomatic.
    - Symptomatic disease (balantidiasis) resembles amebiasis.
    - Presents with diarrhea or frank dysentery, abdominal colic, tenesmus, nausea, and vomiting.

#### 5. Laboratory Diagnosis
- **Stool Examination:**
    - Demonstration of trophozoites and cysts in feces.
    - **Diarrheic Feces:** Motile trophozoites (recognized by large size, kidney-shaped macronucleus, and rapid-revolving motility).
    - **Formed Stool:** Cysts (recognized by round shape and large macronucleus).
- **Biopsy:** Biopsy specimens and scrapings from intestinal ulcers can be examined if stool tests are negative.
- **Culture:** Can be cultured in vitro in Locke's egg albumin medium or IH polygenic medium (similar to _E. histolytica_), though rarely necessary.
#### 6. Treatment & Prophylaxis
- **Treatment:** **Tetracycline** is the drug of choice.
- **Prophylaxis:** Avoid food/water contamination; treat infected pigs and humans.

# leishmania

## Defination and agent
- vector-borne protozoal disease caused by _Leishmania_ species and transmitted by the bite of infected **female sandflies**.
- three major clinical forms are: visceral, cutaneous, and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis
- In India, **visceral leishmaniasis (VL)** is commonly called **kala-azar**, meaning “black disease,” referring to the darkening of skin that may occur in chronic illness

_Leishmania_ occurs in two important forms:

|Feature|Amastigote form|Promastigote form|
|---|---|---|

## Life Cycle
1. An infected female sandfly bites a human and injects **metacyclic promastigotes** into the skin.
2. Promastigotes are phagocytosed by macrophages.
3. Inside macrophages, promastigotes transform into **amastigotes**.
4. Amastigotes multiply by binary fission within macrophages.
5. In visceral disease, infected macrophages carry parasites to the spleen, liver, bone marrow, and lymph nodes.
6. Another sandfly takes a blood meal and ingests macrophages containing amastigotes.
7. In the sandfly gut, amastigotes change into promastigotes, multiply, and migrate to the proboscis.
8. The next bite transmits infective promastigotes to another human.

## Clinical Forms

### Visceral Leishmaniasis

Visceral leishmaniasis, also called kala-azar, is the most severe form. Without treatment, it is fatal in more than 95% of cases.

**Incubation Period**
Usually weeks to months; it may occasionally be longer.

**Clinical Features**
- Prolonged, irregular fever.
- Progressive weight loss and weakness.
- Massive splenomegaly.
- Hepatomegaly.
- Pallor due to anemia.
- Pancytopenia.
- Hyperpigmentation of skin in some patients.
- Lymphadenopathy, more frequent in African cases.
- Recurrent infections and bleeding tendency in advanced disease.

**Laboratory Findings**

|Investigation|Finding|
|---|---|
|Hemoglobin|Decreased|
|Total leukocyte count|Leukopenia|
|Platelet count|Thrombocytopenia|
|Peripheral smear|May show pancytopenia; parasites usually not seen|
|ESR|Often raised|
|Serum albumin|Decreased|
|Serum globulins|Increased|
|Albumin:globulin ratio|Reversed|
|Bone marrow/splenic aspirate|LD bodies may be demonstrated|

**Why Pancytopenia Occurs**
- Hypersplenism causes increased removal of blood cells.
- Bone marrow may be infiltrated by infected macrophages.
- Chronic infection suppresses hematopoiesis.
- Nutritional deficiency may contribute.



### Cutaneous Leishmaniasis
Cutaneous leishmaniasis is the most common form and affects the skin at the site of sandfly bite.

#### Clinical Features
- Begins as a papule at the bite site.
- Gradually develops into a nodule or plaque.
- May ulcerate, producing a painless ulcer.
- Ulcer classically has raised indurated borders and a depressed central area.
- Usually occurs on exposed body parts: face, arms, hands, or legs.
- Lesions often heal spontaneously but can leave permanent scars.

Common names include **Oriental sore**, **Delhi boil**, **Baghdad boil**, and **Aleppo boil**.

A typical lesion may be a round ulcer with an inflamed raised border and central tissue loss.

---
## Mucocutaneous Leishmaniasis
Mucocutaneous disease occurs mainly in the Americas and is commonly associated with the _L. braziliensis_ complex.

### Clinical Features
- May occur months or years after a skin lesion.
- Involves nasal, oral, pharyngeal, or laryngeal mucosa.
- Nasal stuffiness, epistaxis, ulceration, and tissue destruction may occur.
- Severe cases can cause destruction of the nasal septum and facial disfigurement.

---

## Post-Kala-Azar Dermal Leishmaniasis
**Post-kala-azar dermal leishmaniasis (PKDL)** is a dermal condition occurring after apparent cure from visceral leishmaniasis, particularly in India and East Africa.

### Clinical Features
- Hypopigmented macules.
- Erythematous papules.
- Nodules or plaques.
- Lesions commonly appear on the face, trunk, and upper limbs.
- Patient may appear otherwise well.
- Skin lesions contain parasites and can serve as a reservoir for transmission.

---

## Laboratory Diagnosis

### Specimens

|Clinical form|Preferred specimens|
|---|---|
|Visceral leishmaniasis|Splenic aspirate, bone marrow aspirate, lymph node aspirate, peripheral blood in selected cases|
|Cutaneous leishmaniasis|Scraping from active ulcer edge, slit-skin smear, lesion aspirate, skin biopsy|
|Mucocutaneous leishmaniasis|Scraping, aspirate, or biopsy from mucosal lesion|
|PKDL|Slit-skin smear, skin biopsy, lesion aspirate|

---

### Direct Microscopy

#### Principle

Demonstration of **amastigotes (LD bodies)** in macrophages or tissue macrophages confirms leishmaniasis.

#### Staining

- Giemsa stain.
- Leishman stain.
- Wright stain.
- Hematoxylin and eosin stain in tissue sections.

#### Microscopic Appearance of LD Bodies

- Round or oval bodies measuring about 1–5 µm.
- A large round nucleus.
- A small rod-shaped kinetoplast.
- Located inside macrophages or occasionally extracellularly.

Microscopy rapidly detects amastigotes but cannot reliably identify the parasite species. In cutaneous lesions, Giemsa-stained scrapings or biopsy impression smears are commonly used.

#### Diagnostic Value of Aspirates in VL

|Specimen|Relative sensitivity|Major concern|
|---|---|---|
|Splenic aspirate|Highest|Risk of serious hemorrhage; only trained personnel should perform it|
|Bone marrow aspirate|Moderate|Safer than splenic aspiration|
|Lymph node aspirate|Variable|Useful when nodes are enlarged, especially in some regions|

A bone marrow smear may show clusters of amastigotes in macrophages.

---

## Culture

Clinical material can be inoculated into culture media such as:

- Novy–MacNeal–Nicolle (NNN) medium.
- Schneider’s insect medium.
- RPMI-based media.

In culture, amastigotes transform into **promastigotes**.

### Advantages

- Helps in isolation of parasite.
- May allow further identification or drug-susceptibility work.

### Limitations

- Slow.
- Risk of contamination.
- Requires specialized facilities.
- Less useful for urgent diagnosis.

---

## Serological Tests

Serology is particularly helpful in **visceral leishmaniasis** but has limited value in cutaneous and mucocutaneous disease.

|Test|Use / principle|
|---|---|
|rK39 rapid diagnostic test|Detects antibodies against recombinant K39 antigen; widely used for VL|
|Direct agglutination test|Detects anti-_Leishmania_ antibodies|
|ELISA|Detects antibodies in serum|
|Indirect fluorescent antibody test|Antibody detection|
|Western blot|More specialized antibody testing|

### Limitations of Antibody Tests

- Antibodies may remain positive after successful treatment.
- Cannot always distinguish past infection from active disease.
- May be less sensitive in immunocompromised patients, especially HIV infection.
- Not reliable for diagnosing cutaneous leishmaniasis.

---

## Molecular Tests

- Conventional PCR.
- Real-time PCR.
- Species-specific PCR.
- DNA sequencing in reference laboratories.

Molecular testing improves sensitivity and can identify species, which is important because species influences the clinical form and treatment choice

---

## Montenegro Skin Test

Also called the **leishmanin skin test**.

### Principle

It detects delayed-type hypersensitivity to _Leishmania_ antigen.

### Interpretation

- Usually positive in cutaneous and mucocutaneous leishmaniasis.
- Often negative during active visceral leishmaniasis due to depressed cell-mediated immunity.
- May become positive after cure of VL.
- Indicates exposure or cellular immune response, not necessarily active disease.

---

## Differential Diagnosis

### Visceral Leishmaniasis

|Condition|Distinguishing feature|
|---|---|
|Malaria|Parasites detectable in peripheral blood smear|
|Typhoid fever|Blood culture may be positive; splenomegaly usually less marked|
|Tuberculosis|Chronic fever with suggestive clinical and radiological features|
|Leukemia/lymphoma|Abnormal cells in peripheral smear or marrow examination|
|Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis|Fever, splenomegaly, cytopenias, high ferritin; requires specialized work-up|
|Cirrhosis with portal hypertension|Splenomegaly but different liver-related findings|

### Cutaneous Leishmaniasis

- Pyoderma.
- Tuberculosis verrucosa cutis.
- Lupus vulgaris.
- Fungal skin infection.
- Syphilitic ulcer.
- Skin malignancy.
- Traumatic ulcer.

## Treatment

Treatment depends on the form of disease, species, geographical region, severity, pregnancy status, immune status, and drug availability. Confirmed VL requires complete treatment promptly.

|Drug|Important use / point|
|---|---|
|Liposomal amphotericin B|Preferred option for many VL settings; effective and less toxic than conventional amphotericin B|
|Amphotericin B deoxycholate|Effective but more nephrotoxic and infusion-related adverse effects are common|
|Miltefosine|Oral drug; used in selected VL and cutaneous cases; teratogenic, so avoid in pregnancy|
|Paromomycin|Injectable aminoglycoside; used in some VL treatment regimens|
|Pentavalent antimonials|Sodium stibogluconate and meglumine antimoniate; resistance and toxicity limit use in some areas|
|Local therapy|May be used for selected simple cutaneous lesions; includes local heat, cryotherapy, or intralesional therapy|

### Important Adverse Effects

|Drug|Important adverse effects|
|---|---|
|Amphotericin B|Fever, chills, nephrotoxicity, hypokalemia, anemia|
|Miltefosine|Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, teratogenicity|
|Paromomycin|Ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity risk|
|Pentavalent antimonials|Pancreatitis, cardiotoxicity, hepatotoxicity|

**Exam caution:** Drug regimen and dose vary by national guidelines. Do not write one fixed dose for all forms of leishmaniasis.

---

## Prevention and Control

- Early detection and complete treatment of cases.
- Vector control through residual insecticide spraying in endemic areas.
- Use of insecticide-treated bed nets.
- Wearing full-sleeved clothing, especially from dusk to dawn.
- Use of repellents and screens.
- Improving housing by plastering cracks and reducing sandfly resting places.
- Environmental sanitation and removal of organic waste.
- Control of reservoir animals where zoonotic transmission occurs.
- Surveillance for relapse, HIV co-infection, and PKDL.

Leishmaniasis is linked with poverty, poor housing, malnutrition, population movement, and weakened immunity; therefore, control requires both medical and public-health measures.

---

## Epidemiology in India

Kala-azar has historically been endemic mainly in **Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal, and Uttar Pradesh**, while cutaneous disease has been reported in north-western parts of India.

India reached the WHO-defined public-health elimination threshold for kala-azar in 2023: fewer than 1 case per 10,000 population at the subdistrict level. Continued surveillance remains essential to prevent resurgence.

# Trypanosoma
[[docs/protozoa#Classification\|#Classification]]
## Introduction
- **Trypanosoma** is a **blood and tissue flagellate**.
- possess a special mitochondrial DNA-containing structure called the **kinetoplast**.
![Pasted image 20261015174444.png\|169](/img/user/Attachments/Pasted%20image%2020261015174444.png)

Two medically important groups are:
- **Salivaria:** Parasites transmitted in the saliva of a biting insect, e.g., _T. brucei_.
- **Stercoraria:** Parasites transmitted through insect feces contaminating the bite wound or mucosa, e.g., _T. cruzi_.

The major human pathogens are _Trypanosoma brucei gambiense_, _T. brucei rhodesiense_, and _Trypanosoma cruzi_.

### Morphology
- shape: Elongated, spindle-shaped, curved organism
- flagellum: Single flagellum arising near the kinetoplast
- undulating membrane: Wavy membrane formed by the flagellum attached to the body
- kinetoplast: Dark-staining mitochondrial DNA mass, usually posterior to the nucleus
- movement: Active, twisting and lashing movement
![Pasted image 20261001102234.png\|520](/img/user/Attachments/Pasted%20image%2020261001102234.png)


### Important forms:
- **Trypomastigote:** bloodstream form ; <span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)"><b>infective</b></span> or diagnostic form depending on species.
- **Epimastigote:** Multiplying form in insect vector.
- **Amastigote:** without flagellum ; <span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)"><b>characteristic</b></span> of _T. cruzi_ in human tissues.
- **Metacyclic trypomastigote:** <span style="color:rgb(255, 0, 0)">Infective stage</span> transmitted to humans.

## American Trypanosomiasis: Chagas Disease : cruzi

### life cycle of T.cruzi
- bug bite -> deposit feces -> **metacyclic trypomastigote**
- invade host cell especially macrophages

## Antigenic Variation
Very important mechanism of _Trypanosoma_.
- Trypanosomes can **change their surface glycoproteins**.
- These are called **Variant Surface Glycoproteins (VSGs)**.
- The immune system produces antibodies against one VSG.
- The parasite changes its VSG → **escapes immune response**.
- New antigenic variants appear approximately every **7–10 days**.
- A single parasite may possess **~1,000 or more VSG genes**.
