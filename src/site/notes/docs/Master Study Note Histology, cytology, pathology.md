 **Decalcification Methodologies**
* **Purpose**: Calcified tissues (like bones and teeth) or pathologically calcified areas (e.g., tubercular necrosis) are too hard to slice with a microtome knife . Decalcification **removes calcium and mineral salts** from the tissue before processing to ensure smooth cutting without ruining microtome blades .  
* **Tissue Preparation**: The specimen must be cut into thin slices (**2 to 6 mm** thickness; or **4 to 5 mm** for bones) . The tissue **must be completely fixed** in **buffered formalin or Zenker fluid** before decalcifying . **Washing is mandatory**: the tissue must be thoroughly washed in running tap water to remove all excess fixative before placing it in the decalcifying solution . 

* **Decalcifying Chemical Agents**:  
	1. *Aqueous Formic Acid*: Formic acid (5 mL), Distilled water (90 mL), 40% Formaldehyde (5 mL) . Very slow (takes **2 to 7 days**); ideal for autopsy and research but unsuitable for urgent biopsies .  
	2. *Aqueous Nitric Acid*: Nitric acid (5 mL), Distilled water (95 mL) . Extremely rapid but can cause tissue hydrolysis, cell swelling, and color changes due to nitrous oxide formation .  
	3. *Perenyi’s Fluid*: Nitric acid (10 mL), Methyl alcohol (40 mL), 0.5% Chromic acid (30 mL) . Specific for bone tissues; decalcifies in **2 to 10 days** .  
	4. *Ebner’s Fluid*: Sodium chloride (50 mL), Distilled water (50 mL), Hydrochloric acid (8 mL) . Rapid action due to HCl; ideal for teeth and dense hard tissues .  
	5. *Bouin’s Fluid*: Picric acid (500 mL), Formaldehyde/Formalin (167 mL), Formic acid (33 mL) . Used for research; slow action, causes minimal tissue damage .  
	6. *Chelating Agents (EDTA)*: Slow acting but preserves delicate bone morphology, cellular antigens, and DNA, making it highly ideal for **Immunohistochemistry (IHC)** . 

* **Endpoint Determination (Ammonium Oxalate Test)**:  
	1. Pipette **5 mL of used decalcifying fluid** into a clean test tube .  
	2. Add **concentrated ammonia solution drop-by-drop** and test with litmus paper until the solution becomes alkaline .  
	3. If the solution becomes turbid (cloudy), continue adding ammonia. If it remains clear, add **0.5 mL of Ammonium Oxalate solution** .  
	4. *Interpretation*: If calcium ions are still leaching out of the bone, a cloudy white calcium oxalate precipitate will form . If the solution remains **completely clear for 15–20 minutes**, decalcification is complete .  

* **Neutralization and Post-Washing**: Once decalcification is complete, the acid must be neutralized by immersing the tissue in an alkali solution of **5 g/dL (5%) Lithium Carbonate or Sodium Sulfate** . The tissue is then washed in running tap water for **24 to 48 hours (1 to 2 days)** to wash out all remaining acid .

#### **2\. Specialized Optical Systems**

* **Dark Field Microscopy**:  
	* *Principle*: Works on **oblique (indirect) illumination** .uses special **black-centered condenser** that blocks all direct light rays from entering the objective lens . Light only reaches the specimen from highly oblique angles . The light rays are diffracted, refracted, and reflected by the specimen structures into the objective .  
	* *Visualization*: The specimen appears brightly illuminated and glowing against a completely black background .  
	* *Key Application*: Specifically used to identify extremely thin, living spirochetes that are invisible under bright-field microscopy, such as ***Treponema pallidum*** **(the causative agent of syphilis)** and *Leptospira* . It is also used to observe bacterial motility and protozoa .

* **Electron Microscopy (TEM vs. SEM)**:  
	* *Emission and Focusing*: Uses a high-voltage **electron gun** that emits a beam of electrons under a high vacuum . Lenses are not glass, but **electromagnetic coils** that focus the electron beam onto the specimen based on its electrical charge .  
	* *Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)*: The electron beam passes directly **through** the tissue specimen . This produces a flat, X-ray-like 2D image showing the internal ultra-structures and organelles of the cell .  
	* *Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM)*: The tissue specimen is dried and coated with a thin layer of heavy metal (such as gold) . The electron beam **scans the surface** and bounces off the metal coating, creating a highly detailed **3D surface topography image** .  
	* *The Vacuum Rule*: The entire column must operate under a high vacuum . Specimen specimens **must be completely dead and dry** . Any moisture inside the tissue would boil at room temperature under vacuum, instantly destroying the cells . Air molecules would also scatter the electron beam, ruining resolution .



#### **2\. Museum Techniques and Kaiserling Solutions**

* **Purpose**: Preserves diseased human organs for teaching, display, and permanent archiving .  
* **The Three Kaiserling Solutions**:  
  1. *Kaiserling I (Fixative)*: Formalin (400 mL), Potassium acetate (30 g), and Potassium nitrate (2000 mL) (dissolved in water) . Keeps the organ shape intact . The organ remains in Kaiserling I for **6 months** .  
  2. *Kaiserling II (Color Restorer)*: **80% Ethyl Alcohol** . During fixation in Kaiserling I, formalin causes the blood and organ color to turn grey-brown . Immersing the organ in Kaiserling II for a short time restores its natural color .  
  3. *Kaiserling III (Mounting Fluid)*: Glycerin (300 mL), Sodium acetate (100 mL), Formalin (5 mL), and Distilled water (1000 mL) . This is the final fluid filled in the display box .  
* **Mounting and Perspex (Papees) Boxes**:  
  * Organs are trimmed cleanly using a **Butcher’s knife** .  
  * To prevent hollow organs or cysts from collapsing, cavities are filled with **Arsenious acid gelatin fluid** to maintain their original shape .  
  * The organ is positioned on a center **Perspex plate** and secured using **nylon threads** . Nylon is used because it is rot-resistant, highly durable, and chemically inert, remaining completely invisible in the fluid .  
  * The plate is slid into a clear **Perspex box (glass jar)**, filled with Kaiserling III, and permanently heat-sealed .
