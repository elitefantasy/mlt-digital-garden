# 1. Intro
## Definition
- production of globin chain is defective alpha or beta
- due to which Hb formation is defective 
- causes microcytic hypochromic anemia, ineffective erythropoiesis
- caused by mutations
- accumulation of insoluble unpaired chains(excess chain) that damage red blood cells, resulting in ineffective erythropoiesis and hemolytic anemia


## Normal Hemoglobin
Adult hemoglobin is mainly composed of:

- **HbA:** $\alpha_2\beta_2$, approximately 96–98%.
- **HbA2:** $\alpha_2\delta_2$, approximately 1.5–3.5%.
- **HbF:** $\alpha_2\gamma_2$, normally less than 1% in adults.

The main globin genes are:

|Globin chain|Gene location|Main hemoglobin|
|---|---|---|
|Alpha chain|Chromosome 16|HbA, HbA2, HbF|
|Beta chain|Chromosome 11|HbA|
|Gamma chain|Chromosome 11|HbF|
|Delta chain|Chromosome 11|HbA2|



# 2. Pathophysiology

The key problem is an **imbalance of globin-chain production**.

- In **beta-thalassemia**, beta-chain synthesis decreases, leaving excess alpha chains.
- In **alpha-thalassemia**, alpha-chain synthesis decreases, leaving excess beta or gamma chains.
- The unmatched excess chains precipitate inside erythroid precursors and red cells.
- This damages red-cell membranes, causing **ineffective erythropoiesis** in bone marrow and **hemolysis** in circulation and spleen.
- Chronic anemia increases erythropoietin production, causing marrow expansion, skeletal changes, and extramedullary hematopoiesis

A useful memory link is:

- **Reduced globin production -> unbalanced chains -> damaged RBCs -> anemia + hemolysis + marrow expansion.**

## 3.1 Alpha-Thalassemia

Alpha-thalassemia results from mutations or deletions in the genes encoding alpha-globin chains, primarily HBA1 and HBA2, located on chromosome 16
- inherited blood disorder

Loss of one or two alpha-globin genes leads to the **alpha-thalassemia trait or alpha-thalassemia minor**
loss of three or four genes results in more severe forms, including **Hb H disease and hydrops fetalis**

**Genetic Defect:**
- Deficiency or absence of alpha (α) chains
- In adults, leads to excess beta (β) chains
- In infants, causes overproduction of gamma (γ) chains


**Laboratory Findings (MCV and Hemoglobin):**
- MCV: decr
- Hemoglobin levels: 9-8 g/dL
- HbH pattern on electrophoresis
- Hemolysis and splenomegaly are common


## 3.2 Beta-Thalassemia

Beta-thalassemia results from mutations affecting the beta-globin gene on chromosome ==11==. Unlike alpha-thalassemia, beta-thalassemia is commonly due to **point mutations** rather than gene deletions
<!--SR:!2026-10-15,40,290-->

Mutations may cause:

- $\beta^0$: No beta-chain production.
- $\beta^+$ : Reduced beta-chain production.

These mutations impair the production of beta-globin chains, leading to decreased synthesis of normal hemoglobin (HbA)

| Type                                     | Genetic state                                        | Severity                                  |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Beta-thalassemia trait/minor             | Heterozygous                                         | Mild anemia                               |
| Beta-thalassemia intermedia              | Variable mutations                                   | Moderate disease; transfusion need varies |
| Beta-thalassemia major (cooley's anemia) | Homozygous or compound heterozygous severe mutations | Severe transfusion-dependent anemia       |

#  Clinical Features
### General Clinical Features of Thalassemia

Across the thalassemia spectrum, the underlying globin chain imbalance leads to common manifestations driven by anemia, hemolysis, marrow expansion, and iron accumulation:

  
- **Features of Anemia:** Pallor, generalized fatigue, weakness, dyspnea, poor exercise tolerance, and tachycardia.
- **Features of Hemolysis:** Jaundice (unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia), gallstone formation, and splenomegaly.
- **Features of Bone Marrow Expansion (in moderate-to-severe forms):** Skeletal changes from expanded erythropoiesis, including frontal bossing, maxillary hypertrophy, prominent cheekbones ("chipmunk facies"), osteopenia/osteoporosis, pathologic fractures, and a "hair-on-end" pattern on skull radiographs.
- **Features of Iron Overload (from transfusions and/or increased GI absorption):** Cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, heart failure, liver fibrosis/cirrhosis, diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, hypoparathyroidism, and delayed puberty or infertility.
    

### 1. Beta-Thalassemia Major (Cooley's Anemia)
- Manifests in early infancy (usually before 2 years of age) as fetal hemoglobin declines.
- Characterized by severe pallor, failure to thrive, poor feeding, severe transfusion-dependent anemia, and growth retardation
- Massive hepatosplenomegaly, prominent craniofacial bone deformities, and recurrent infections.
- Severe systemic iron overload from chronic, lifelong blood transfusions requiring chelation therapy

### 2. Beta-Thalassemia Intermedia
- Manifests with moderate anemia where baseline survival is possible without regular, routine transfusions.
- Characterized by progressive splenomegaly, gallstones, non-healing leg ulcers, thrombotic events, and pulmonary hypertension.
- Progressive iron overload occurs even without regular transfusions due to increased gastrointestinal iron absorption.


### 3. Beta-Thalassemia Minor / Trait
- Clinically asymptomatic or presents as mild, well-tolerated microcytic anemia
- Does not require blood transfusions.
- No clinically apparent bone deformities, organ failure, or systemic iron overload under normal conditions.


### 4. Alpha-Thalassemia Silent Carrier (1-Gene Deletion)
- Completely asymptomatic with normal physical findings and normal exercise tolerance.

### 5. Alpha-Thalassemia Minor / Trait (2-Gene Deletion)
- Asymptomatic or presents with mild microcytic anemia without significant systemic complications.

### 6. Hemoglobin H (HbH) Disease (3-Gene Deletion
- Moderate-to-severe chronic hemolytic anemia with variable clinical severity.
- Prominent splenomegaly and mild-to-moderate jaundice due to active hemolysis.
- Patients may experience worsening hemolysis and acute drops in hemoglobin during oxidative stress, fever, or infections.

### 7. Hb Bart's Hydrops Fetalis (4-Gene Deletion)
- Lethal in utero or shortly after birth due to complete absence of functional alpha chains.
- Characterized by generalized fetal edema (hydrops fetalis), severe intrauterine pallor, massive hepatosplenomegaly, high-output heart failure, and skeletal/developmental malformations.


## LABORATORY DIAGNOSIS OF THALASSEMIA

### General Laboratory Approach
- **Complete Blood Count (CBC):** Reduced hemoglobin, marked microcytosis (low MCV), hypochromia (low MCH). RBC count is often disproportionately elevated relative to the degree of anemia 
- **Peripheral Blood Smear (PBS):** Microcytic hypochromic RBCs, target cells (codocytes), anisopoikilocytosis, basophilic stippling, and nucleated RBCs (nRBCs) in severe forms
- **Hemoglobin Analysis:** High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC), capillary electrophoresis, or cellulose acetate electrophoresis (pH 8.6) to quantify $\text{HbA}$, $\text{HbA}_2$, and $\text{HbF}$
- **Iron Studies:** Normal or elevated serum ferritin and transferrin saturation (distinguishes thalassemia trait from Iron Deficiency Anemia)
- **Molecular/Genetic Testing:** DNA analysis (gap-PCR, MLPA, gene sequencing) for globin gene deletions ($\alpha$) or point mutations ($\beta$)


### 1. Beta-Thalassemia Major (Cooley's Anemia)

- **CBC:** Severe microcytic hypochromic anemia (Hb often $3\text{–}4\text{ g/dL}$), markedly decreased MCV and MCH, significantly elevated RDW. Elevated leukocyte count due to automated counters misidentifying circulating nRBCs.
- **PBS:** Extreme anisopoikilocytosis, severe microcytosis, target cells, marked polychromasia, basophilic stippling, schistocytes (fragmented RBCs), and numerous circulating nRBCs
- **Hemoglobin Analysis (HPLC/Electrophoresis):** 
	- Markedly increased $\text{HbF}$ ($>90\%$) 
	- variable or slightly elevated $\text{HbA}_2$
	- absent ($\beta^0/\beta^0$) or severely reduced ($\beta^+/\beta^+$) $\text{HbA}$
- HbF Quantification : alkali denaturation test used to measure HbF levels
- **Biochemical & Hemolysis Profile:** Elevated serum ferritin (secondary to iron overload and transfusions), indirect bilirubinemia, elevated LDH, increased urinary urobilinogen/stercobilinogen.
- **Bone Marrow:** Marked erythroid hyperplasia with a reduced myeloid-to-erythroid (M:E) ratio
### 2. Beta-Thalassemia Minor / Trait
- **CBC:** Mild microcytic hypochromic anemia or borderline-normal Hb; elevated RBC count ($>5.0 \times 10^{12}\text{/L}$); low MCV ($<75\text{ fL}$) and low MCH; normal or slightly elevated RDW ($\sim 14\text{–}15\%$)
- **PBS:** Uniform microcytosis and hypochromia, frequent target cells, no prominent polychromasia, normal WBC and platelet counts.
- **Hemoglobin Analysis (HPLC/Electrophoresis):** Diagnostic elevation of HbA$_2$ (3.5-7%) , mildly increased $\text{HbF}$ ($1\%\text{–}3\%$), and the remainder being $\text{HbA}$
- **Iron Profile:** Normal serum iron, ferritin, and total iron-binding capacity (TIBC).


### 3. Beta-Thalassemia Intermedia
- **CBC:** Moderate anemia (Hb typically $7\text{–}10\text{ g/dL}$), reduced MCV and MCH.
- **PBS:** Moderate anisopoikilocytosis, target cells, occasional nRBCs.
- **Hemoglobin Analysis:** Elevated $\text{HbF}$ ($30\%\text{–}80\%$), elevated or variable $\text{HbA}_2$, variable $\text{HbA}$ depending on underlying mutations.


### 4. Alpha-Thalassemia Silent Carrier ($-\alpha/\alpha\alpha$)
- **CBC:** Normal Hb and indices, or borderline low MCV.
- **Hemoglobin Analysis:** Normal adult profile ($\text{HbA}$, $\text{HbA}_2$, and $\text{HbF}$ within reference ranges).
- **Diagnosis:** Requires alpha-globin gene DNA analysis (PCR).

### 5. Alpha-Thalassemia Minor / Trait ($--/\alpha\alpha$ or $-\alpha/-\alpha$)
- **CBC:** Mild microcytic hypochromic anemia with high RBC count, low MCV, and low MCH.
- **PBS:** Microcytosis, hypochromia, scattered target cells.
- **Hemoglobin Analysis:** Normal $\text{HbA}_2$ and normal $\text{HbF}$ (unlike $\beta$-thalassemia minor).
- **Special Tests:** Trace amounts of $\text{Hb Bart's}$ ($\gamma_4$) detectable only at birth; definitive diagnosis requires DNA analysis.

### 6. Hemoglobin H (HbH) Disease  ($--/-\alpha$)
- **CBC:** Moderate to severe hemolytic anemia (Hb $7\text{–}10\text{ g/dL}$), significantly decreased MCV and MCH.
- **PBS:** Microcytic hypochromic cells, prominent target cells, polychromasia, and basophilic stippling.
- **Supravital Staining (Brilliant Cresyl Blue):** Precipitated HbH inclusions producing a characteristic "golf-ball" appearance inside RBCs.
- **Hemoglobin Analysis:** Fast-migrating $\text{HbH}$ ($\beta_4$ tetramers) band on electrophoresis/HPLC ($5\%\text{–}30\%$), reduced $\text{HbA}$, decreased $\text{HbA}_2$.

### 7. Hb Bart's Hydrops Fetalis ($--/--$)
- **CBC:** Lethal severe anemia (Hb $3\text{–}8\text{ g/dL}$), marked microcytosis.
- **PBS:** Severe anisopoikilocytosis, marked target cells, circulating nucleated RBCs.
- **Hemoglobin Analysis:** Predominantly $\text{Hb Bart's}$ ($\gamma_4$ tetramers, $\sim 80\%\text{–}90\%$), traces of embryonic Portland Hb, complete absence of $\text{HbA}$, $\text{HbA}_2$, and $\text{HbF}$.

### NESTROFT (Naked Eye Single Tube Red Cell Osmotic Fragility Test)
NESTROFT (Naked Eye Single Tube Red Cell Osmotic Fragility Test) is a simple, low‑cost screening test that detects **decreased red cell osmotic fragility** (increased resistance to hemolysis), classically seen in **β‑thalassemia trait** and some other hemoglobinopathies.

#### Principle

- Normal RBCs placed in **0.36% buffered saline** undergo near‑complete lysis within ~30 minutes, leaving a **clear, transparent** solution.
- In **β‑thalassemia trait** (and some other conditions), RBCs are **more resistant to osmotic lysis** due to altered surface area/volume ratio and membrane properties; many cells remain intact, producing **turbidity/haziness** and a **button of cells** at the bottom after standing.
- A **positive NESTROFT** therefore indicates **decreased osmotic fragility / increased resistance to lysis**, suggestive of a thalassemic or microcytic hemoglobinopathy carrier state.

#### Reagents and materials

- **0.36% buffered saline** (commonly phosphate‑buffered NaCl, pH ~7.4)
- **Distilled water tube** (optional control for complete lysis)[
- Fresh **EDTA or capillary blood** (a drop is sufficient)
- Clean test tubes, timer, white card with a thin black line[

#### Procedure (typical method)

1. Label two tubes:
    - **BS** (buffered saline 0.36%, ~2 mL)
    - **DW** (distilled water, ~2 mL; optional, for complete lysis control)
2. Add **one drop of well‑mixed blood** to each tube, mix gently.)
3. Allow to stand at **room temperature for 30 minutes** without disturbance
4. Gently invert/shake and place each tube against a **white background with a thin black line**.
5. Observe:
    - If the **line is equally clear** through BS as through DW → **NESTROFT negative** (normal fragility).
    - If the line is **blurred/indistinct** through BS and the suspension looks hazy, often with a **red cell button** at the bottom → **NESTROFT positive** (decreased fragility).

#### Interpretation
- **Positive NESTROFT**:
    - Suggests **β‑thalassemia trait**, β‑thalassemia major/intermedia, HbE trait/disease, some other microcytic hemoglobinopathies, and occasionally iron deficiency anemia.
    - Indicates **increased RBC resistance to osmotic lysis** in 0.36% saline
- **Negative NESTROFT**:
    - Compatible with normal RBC osmotic fragility; makes β‑thalassemia trait less likely but does **not absolutely exclude** it.

Typical performance in studies: **high sensitivity (~95–100%)** but **moderate specificity (~85–90%)**, so it is good for **screening** but not definitive diagnosis.

#### Uses

- **Mass/camp screening** for β‑thalassemia carriers, especially in resource‑limited settings.
- First‑line test before confirmatory tests like **HbA₂ quantification (HPLC/electrophoresis)** and molecular testing

#### Limitations

- Not specific for β‑thalassemia trait; can be positive in other microcytic anemias and hemoglobinopathies
- Operator‑dependent visual reading; requires standardized saline concentration and timing.
- Must be followed by **quantitative HbA₂** and/or genetic testing for definitive carrier diagnosis



## Treatment

Treatment depends on severity and transfusion requirement.

### Thalassemia Trait

- Usually no specific treatment.
- Avoid unnecessary iron therapy.
- Give genetic counseling.
- Screen partner before pregnancy where relevant.

### Transfusion-Dependent Thalassemia

Management may include:

- Regular packed red-cell transfusions to correct severe anemia and suppress excessive marrow activity.
- Extended red-cell antigen matching where feasible to reduce alloimmunization risk.
- Monitoring for transfusion reactions and transfusion-transmitted infections.
- Monitoring ferritin and organ iron burden

### Iron Chelation Therapy

Chelation removes excess iron and reduces iron-related organ damage.

Common chelators:

- **Deferoxamine:** Parenteral iron chelator.
- **Deferasirox:** Oral iron chelator.
- **Deferiprone:** Oral chelator; can be useful for cardiac iron but requires neutrophil monitoring.

### Splenectomy

May be considered if hypersplenism causes:

- Rising transfusion requirement.
- Severe splenomegaly.
- Cytopenias from splenic sequestration.

After splenectomy, infection and thrombosis risk increase, so vaccination and preventive measures are important.

### Curative and Newer Therapies

- **Allogeneic hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation** can be curative in selected patients.
- **Gene therapy or gene editing** is an emerging option for selected patients with severe disease.
- Drugs such as **luspatercept** may reduce transfusion burden in some eligible beta-thalassemia patients.



## High-Yield Exam Points

- Thalassemia is a **quantitative defect** of hemoglobin synthesis.
- It causes **microcytic hypochromic anemia** with a relatively high RBC count.
- Beta-thalassemia trait classically shows **increased ==1;;HbA2==**.
- Beta-thalassemia major shows **markedly increased ==1;;HbF==** and little or no ==1;;HbA==.
- Alpha-thalassemia is commonly due to **gene deletions** on chromosome 16.
- Beta-thalassemia is commonly due to **point mutations** on chromosome 11.
- HbH is formed by **four beta chains**: $\beta_4$.
- Hb Bart’s is formed by **four gamma chains**: $\gamma_4$.
- Hb Bart’s hydrops fetalis results from deletion of **all four alpha-globin genes**.
- Iron should not be prescribed for microcytosis until iron deficiency is confirmed.
- The major long-term complication in transfusion-dependent patients is **iron overload**.
- Definitive potentially curative treatment is **hematopoietic stem-cell transplantation** in suitable patients
