# 1. What Are Enzymes?

Enzymes are biocatalysts, which accelerate the rate of biochemical reactions by reducing their activation energy.

**Enzyme structure**
![Pasted image 20260817205654.png\|367](/img/user/Attachments/Pasted%20image%2020260817205654.png)


**Characteristics** / properties
- **Protein Nature:** Most enzymes are globular proteins except ribozymes, which are RNA enzymes. Enzymes generally have a tertiary structure and can be denatured by heat and pH changes. The exception is ribozyme, which is made of RNA.
- **Catalytic Efficiency:** They accelerate reactions by multiple orders of magnitude without undergoing permanent chemical changes themselves, allowing them to be reused.
- **Extreme Specificity:**
	1. Absolute specificity
	2. Group specificity
	3. Optical specificity
	4. Linkage specificity
- **Presence of Cofactors:**
	1. **Cofactor:** Inorganic metal ions such as Zn²⁺ and Mg²⁺
	2. **Coenzyme:** Organic molecules such as NAD⁺ and FAD
	3. **Holoenzyme:** Apoenzyme + cofactor
- **Inhibition:**
	1. Competitive inhibition
	2. Non-competitive inhibition
	3. Uncompetitive inhibition

## Allosteric Site in Enzyme

Allosteric sites are different from active sites. When effector molecules such as **inhibitors** or activators bind to these sites, they induce **conformational changes** in the enzyme, which alter the shape and function of the active site, thereby **regulating enzyme activity.**

# 2. Examples of Enzyme Action

Enzymes exhibit precise specificity, performing distinct chemical actions based on their structure and classification.

**Digestive and Substrate-Specific Actions**

- **Lipase:** Hydrolyzes lipids (triglycerides) into fatty acids and glycerol.
- **Urease:** A nickel-dependent enzyme that hydrolyzes urea into ammonia (NH₃) and CO₂.
- **Lactase:** A disaccharidase enzyme produced in the small intestine that hydrolyzes lactose into the monosaccharides galactose and glucose.
- **Amylase:** Splits starch molecules into dextrins and maltose.

**Metabolic and Biochemical Transformations**

- **Carbonic Anhydrase:** Catalyzes:   CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO3
  This allows efficient gas transport and bicarbonate reabsorption.
- **Xanthine Oxidase:** Converts hypoxanthine into xanthine and subsequently into uric acid.
- **UDP-Glucuronosyltransferase:** Converts unconjugated bilirubin into conjugated bilirubin.

**Clinical and Diagnostic Actions**

- **ALT (SGPT):** Functions as a primary marker for acute viral hepatitis and direct hepatocyte injury due to its confinement in hepatocyte cytoplasm.
- **AST (SGOT):** Used to detect ischemic hepatitis or acetaminophen poisoning, although it is less specific than ALT because it is also found in cardiac and skeletal muscle.

# 3. Enzyme Models

Enzyme models, also known as theories of catalytic mechanisms, describe how enzymes interact with substrates to facilitate chemical reactions.

**1\. Fischer's Lock and Key Model**
1. The active site is rigid.
2. The substrate is like a key fitting into a lock.
3. **Limitation:** It cannot explain enzyme flexibility, conformational shifts, or allosteric regulation.

**2\. Koshland's Induced Fit Theory**

1. The active site is highly flexible.
2. Binding of a substrate induces conformational changes in the active site, moulding the enzyme structure to create a perfect fit.

# 4. Classification of Enzymes

In accordance with the International Union of Biochemistry (IUB) system established in 1961, enzymes are systematically categorized into six major classes based on their chemical mechanisms.

Each enzyme is assigned a four-digit **Enzyme Commission (EC) number**, which denotes its class, subclass, sub-subclass, and specific enzyme.

## Six Major Classes

1. **Oxidoreductases — EC 1**
	- Catalyze oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions involving the gain or loss of electrons or hydrogen.
	- These enzymes are fundamental to metabolic pathways like cellular respiration, photosynthesis, and detoxification
	- **Dehydrogenases:** Remove hydrogen atoms and transfer them to cofactors like NAD⁺ or FAD. eg: **Lactate dehydrogenase:** Converts lactate to pyruvate during respiration.
	- **Oxidases:** Use oxygen (O₂) as an electron acceptor, producing water or hydrogen peroxide. eg: **Glucose oxidase:** Converts glucose to gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide.
	- **Peroxidases:** Break down peroxides using hydrogen peroxide as an electron acceptor. eg: **Catalase:** Breaks down harmful hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen.
	- **Oxygenases:** Directly add oxygen atoms into a substrate molecule. eg: **Cytochrome c oxidase:** A key complex in the mitochondrial electron transport chain.
2. **Transferases — EC 2**
	- Catalyze the transfer of a functional group such as methyl, phosphate, or amino groups from one substrate to another.
	- **Main Types of Transferases**
		- **Kinases (Phosphotransferases):** Transfer phosphate groups from ATP to a specific substrate. eg : **Hexokinase:** Phosphorylates glucose in the first step of glycolysis.
		- **Transaminases (Aminotransferases):** Move amino groups between amino acids and keto acids. eg: **Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT):** Plays a vital role in amino acid metabolism and is a key marker of liver health.
		- **Methyltransferases:** Transfer methyl groups (-CH₃) to proteins, DNA, or other molecules. eg: **DNA Methyltransferase:** Catalyzes the addition of methyl groups to DNA, critical for epigenetic regulation.
		- **Glycosyltransferases:** Transfer activated carbohydrate residues to accept molecules. eg: **Peptidyl Transferase:** Form peptide bonds between amino acids during translation in the ribosome.
3. **Hydrolases — EC 3**
	- Catalyze the cleavage of chemical bonds such as ester, ether, peptide, or glycosidic bonds by the addition of water.
	- **Main Types of Hydrolases**
		- **Esterases (including Lipases):** Cleave ester bonds, such as breaking down fats into fatty acids and glycerol. eg:
		  (1)***Pepsin and Trypsin:*** Digestive proteases that break down dietary proteins in the stomach and small intestine........
		  (2) ***Acetylcholinesterase*:** Hydrolyses the neurotransmitter acetylcholine to terminate nerve signals at synapses.
		- **Glycoside Hydrolases (Glycosidases):** Hydrolyse glycosidic bonds to break down complex carbohydrates into sugars. eg:
		  (1) ***Amylase:*** Breaks down starch into sugars, starting in the mouth with saliva..........
		  (2) ***Lactase*:** Cleaves lactose into glucose and galactose, the lack of which causes lactose intolerance
		- **Peptidases (Proteases):** Cleave peptide bonds to break proteins down into amino acids. eg:
		- **Nucleases (Phosphodiesterases):** Break phosphodiester bonds to degrade nucleic acids (DNA or RNA).
4. **Lyases — EC 4**
	- Catalyze the cleavage of bonds by mechanisms other than hydrolysis, often leaving double bonds, or the addition of groups to double bonds without water.
	- **Main Types of Lyases**
		- **Decarboxylases:** Remove a carboxyl group (-COOH) from a molecule, releasing it as carbon dioxide (CO₂). eg ***Pyruvate Decarboxylase:*** Converts pyruvate into acetaldehyde, a critical step in ethanol fermentation.
		- **Dehydratases:** Remove a water molecule (H₂O) to create a double bond.
		- **hydratases**:  eg: ***Fumarase (Fumarate Hydratase):*** Reversibly adds water to fumarate to form malate in the Krebs cycle.
		- **Aldolases:** Cleave or form carbon-carbon bonds in aldol reactions. eg: ***Fructose-Bisphosphate Aldolase:*** Cleaves fructose 1,6-bisphosphate into two three-carbon sugars during glycolysis.
		- **Synthases:** Join two molecules together without utilizing ATP (unlike ligases, which require ATP).
		- **nucleotidyltransferases/cyclases** : - eg: ***Adenylate Cyclase*** Converts ATP to cyclic AMP (cAMP), a vital second messenger in cellular signaling.
5. **Isomerases — EC 5**
	- Catalyze structural, geometric, or spatial rearrangements within a single molecule to convert it into an isomer.
	- Main Types of Isomerases
		- **Epimerases and Racemases:** Invert the asymmetry at a specific carbon atom to change stereochemistry (e.g., converting D-isomers to L-isomers).
		- Intramolecular **Oxidoreductases:** Catalyze the oxidation of one part of a molecule paired with the reduction of another part (e.g., interconverting aldoses and ketoses).
		- Intramolecular Transferases **(Mutases):** Shift a functional group from one position to another within the same molecule.
		- **Cis-Trans Isomerases:** Rearrange the spatial layout of groups around a double bond (interconverting cis and trans configurations).
	- **Common Examples**
		- **Phosphoglucose Isomerase:** Converts glucose-6-phosphate into fructose-6-phosphate during the second step of glycolysis.
		- **Triosephosphate Isomerase (TPI):** Reversibly interconverts DHAP and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, essential for efficient glucose metabolism.
		- **Phosphoglycerate Mutase:** Shifts a phosphate group from the 3-carbon position to the 2-carbon position in glycerate during glycolysis.
		- **DNA Topoisomerases:** Alter the topological state of DNA by untangling and unwinding the double helix during replication and transcription.
6. **Ligases — EC 6**
	- Catalyze the covalent joining of two molecules coupled with the hydrolysis of a high-energy phosphate bond in ATP.
	- **Key point:** ATP utilization is characteristic of synthetases.
	- **Common Examples and Functions**
		- **DNA Ligase:** Links DNA strands together by forming phosphodiester bonds, critical for DNA replication and repair.
		- **Pyruvate Carboxylase:** Converts pyruvate to oxaloacetate in gluconeogenesis by adding a carbon atom from bicarbonate.
		- **Aminoacyl-tRNA Synthetase:** Charges tRNA molecules with their correct amino acids for protein synthesis.
		- **Glutamine Synthetase:** Combines glutamate and ammonia to form glutamine, playing a central role in nitrogen metabolism.
		- Carbamoyl Phosphate Synthetase I
		- Argininosuccinate Synthetase

## Classification Based on Where They Function

- **Intracellular:** Function within cells.
  - Example: Glycolytic enzymes.

- **Extracellular:** Secreted by cells to function outside the cells.
  - Example: Digestive enzymes.

## Classification Based on ATP Requirements

- **Synthetase:** ATP-dependent; falls under the ligase class. Example: Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase.

- **Synthase:** Does not require ATP. Example: Glycogen synthase.

## Classification Based on Structure

- **Monomeric:** Made of a single polypeptide chain. Examples: Trypsin, Ribonuclease.

- **Oligomeric:** Contains multiple polypeptide chains. Example: Lactate dehydrogenase.


# 5. Enzyme Nomenclature

- **Basis of Naming:** Enzymes are named according to the type of chemical reaction they catalyze, their specific substrate, or a combination of both.

## Suffixes Used in Naming

- **"-ase":** Standard suffix used to denote an enzyme.
  - Examples: Lactase, Amylase, Lipase, Protease.

- **"-in":** Found in some of the earliest studied digestive enzymes.
  - Examples: Pepsin, Trypsin, Chymotrypsin.

## Prefixes Used in Naming

The prefix indicates the specific type of reaction the enzyme catalyzes.

- **Oxidase:** Catalyzes redox (oxidation-reduction) reactions.
- **Hydrolase:** Catalyzes the addition of water to break one component into two parts.

## Combined Nomenclature

Substrate identity is frequently combined with the reaction type to name an enzyme.

Examples:

- Pyruvate carboxylase
- Lactate dehydrogenase

## Classification Principle

The classification of enzymes depends primarily on the reaction they catalyze.

Consequently, if two different enzymes act on the exact same substrate but catalyze fundamentally different reactions, they will have different Enzyme Commission (EC) classifications.

# 6. Enzyme Unit

## Measurement of Enzyme Activity

Instead of weighing enzymes, scientists measure their activity. This is because:

- Different enzyme samples can vary in purity.
- Some enzyme molecules may be completely inactive.
- Activity directly reflects actual catalytic performance.

## International Unit (IU or U)

- **Definition:** The amount of enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of one micromole (1 μmol) of substrate into product per minute under specified assay conditions.
- **Formula:**

  `1 IU = 1 μmol/min`

- **Example:** If an enzyme converts 500 μmol of substrate per minute, its activity is 500 IU.

## Katal (kat)

- **Definition:** The SI-derived unit of catalytic activity, based on the amount of substance converted per second.
- **Formula:**

  `1 kat = 1 mol/s`

- **Key Components:**
  - Amount of substance = mole
  - Time = second

# 7. Cofactors in Enzymes

## 1. Classification of Cofactors

### A. Inorganic Cofactors (Metal Ions)

- **Definition:** Non-protein, inorganic metal ions essential for the activity of specific enzymes.
- **Functional Roles:**
  - Charge stabilization
  - Facilitating electron transfer
  - Structural roles
  - Proper substrate orientation

#### Key Examples

- **Magnesium (Mg²⁺):** Required by many enzymes involved in ATP-related reactions.
- **Zinc (Zn²⁺):** Present in enzymes such as carbonic anhydrase.
- **Iron (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺):** Heavily involved in electron-transfer pathways.
- **Copper (Cu²⁺):** Found structurally/functionally in several oxidizing enzymes.
- **Others:** Manganese (Mn²⁺) and Calcium (Ca²⁺).

### B. Organic Cofactors (Coenzymes)

- **Definition:** Carbon-containing organic molecules that facilitate chemical transformations.
- **Key Characteristics:** They typically participate transiently in the reaction, acting as carrier or transfer molecules for specific chemical groups or electrons.
- **Vitamin Derivation:** Many coenzymes are directly derived from B-complex vitamins.

### C. Prosthetic Groups

- **Definition:** A non-protein component of an enzyme that is tightly or permanently bound, often via covalent bonds, to the protein structure.
- **Key Characteristics:** Unlike transient coenzymes, prosthetic groups stay attached through multiple catalytic cycles and are regenerated directly on the enzyme.
- **Examples:**
  - Heme groups in catalase and cytochromes
  - FAD
  - FMN
  - Biotin

# 8. Factors Affecting Enzymatic Activity

### 7.1 Temperature

- **Acceleration:** Rising temperature increases kinetic energy and collision frequency, speeding up the reaction.
- **Optimum:** Standard human optimum is **35°C to 40°C**, specifically **37°C**.
- **Denaturation:** Temperatures above 40°C–45°C cause thermal denaturation of the enzyme's protein structure, which is **permanent and irreversible**.
- **Inactivation:** Low temperatures render enzymes **inactive**. This inactivation is **temporary and reversible**.
- **Curve:** Plotting velocity vs. temperature yields a **bell-shaped curve**.
- **Temperature Coefficient (Q₁₀):** Represents the change in rate for every 10°C rise. For most enzymes, **Q₁₀ = 2**, meaning velocity doubles up to the optimum.

### 7.2 pH (Hydrogen Ion Concentration)

- **Optimum:** Standard range for most enzymes is **6.0 to 8.0**.
- **Effect:** Deviations alter active-site charges, disrupting substrate binding and eventually causing denaturation.
- **Curve:** Plotting velocity vs. pH yields a **bell-shaped curve**.

### 7.3 Enzyme Concentration

- Velocity is **directly proportional** to enzyme concentration when substrate is in excess.
- **Curve:** Plotting velocity vs. enzyme concentration yields a **straight line (linear)**.
- **Clinical Use:** Forms the basis for using enzyme activity as a diagnostic tool in clinical biochemistry.

### 7.4 Substrate Concentration

- **Saturation:** Velocity increases with substrate concentration until all enzyme active sites are occupied (saturated).
- **Curve:** Plotting velocity vs. substrate concentration yields a **rectangular hyperbola**.

#### Kinetics

- **First-Order:** At low substrate concentration, velocity is proportional to substrate concentration.
- **Zero-Order:** At high substrate concentration ([S] >> Km), velocity is constant (Vmax) and independent of substrate concentration.

### 7.5 Product Concentration

- High product accumulation leads to a **decline in enzyme activity** (product/feedback inhibition).
- **Mechanism:** Products bind to the active or allosteric sites, altering enzyme conformation.

### 7.6 Inorganic Activators

- **Cations:** Ions such as Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺, Mn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺, and Ca²⁺ are required as cofactors or activators.
- **Anions:** **Chloride ion (Cl⁻)** specifically acts as an essential activator for **amylase**.

### 7.7 Light & Radiation

- Exposure to high-energy **UV, beta, gamma, or X-rays** inactivates enzymes by forming peroxides or causing oxidation of catalytic residues.
- **Example:** UV radiation inhibits salivary amylase.

# 9. Active Site and its properties
- **Definition:** The active site is the specific region, pocket, cleft, or groove of an enzyme where the substrate binds and where the catalytic reaction takes place.
- **Functional Role:** The 3D arrangement of the active site determines which substrate can bind, how strongly it binds, and which chemical reaction occurs.
## Types of Residues in the Active Site
- **1. Binding Residues:**
    - Facilitate substrate binding to the enzyme.
    - Determine substrate specificity.
    - Interact reversibly with the substrate through multiple weak non-covalent interactions.
- **2. Catalytic Residues:**
    - Directly participate in the chemical transformation of substrate into product.
    - Help break or form chemical bonds.
    - Function by donating or accepting protons or stabilizing reaction intermediates.

## Non-Covalent Interactions at the Active Site
Substrate binding is maintained by multiple weak, reversible, non-covalent forces:
- **Hydrogen Bonds:** Aid in positioning the substrate correctly and establishing binding specificity.
- **Ionic Interactions:** Form between oppositely charged groups on the active site and the substrate.
- **Hydrophobic Interactions:** Enable non-polar groups to associate away from the surrounding aqueous environment.
- **Van der Waals Forces:** Weak attractions that contribute when molecules are positioned in close physical contact.
    
## 4. Key Properties of the Active Site
### A. Specificity
- **Overview:** Enzymes specifically recognize a particular substrate or a group of structurally related molecules due to the precise spatial arrangement of amino acids within the active site.
- **Types of Specificity:**
    - **Absolute Specificity:** The enzyme acts exclusively on one particular substrate.
    - **Group Specificity:** The enzyme acts on substrates that share a particular functional group.
    - **Bond Specificity:** The enzyme recognizes a specific type of chemical bond.
    - **Stereospecificity:** The enzyme recognizes only a specific stereoisomer.
### B. Stereospecificity
- The asymmetric 3D conformation of the active site allows enzymes to distinguish between optical isomers, such as $\text{D-}$ and $\text{L-}$ enantiomers.
- This property is critical in biochemical pathways because biological molecules are stereochemically defined.
### C. Chemical Complementarity
- The active site possesses a chemical environment complementary to the charge and polarity of the substrate.
- It may contain positively charged, negatively charged, polar, or non-polar/hydrophobic groups.
- _Example:_ A negatively charged region of the active site interacts with a positively charged group on the substrate.
### D. Catalytic Activity & Activation Energy
- Catalytic residues convert bound substrates into products.
- **Thermodynamic Principle:** The enzyme does **not** alter the overall reaction equilibrium.
- **Mechanism:** It accelerates reaction velocity by **lowering the activation energy**.
### E. Saturation Kinetics (Michaelis-Menten Relationship)
- As substrate concentration increases, progressively more active sites are occupied, increasing the reaction velocity.
- When all available active sites are occupied, the enzyme reaches **saturation**.
- Beyond saturation, further increases in substrate concentration produce little to no change in reaction rate.
- **Kinetic Parameters:**
    - **$\text{V}_{\max}$:** Maximum achievable reaction velocity when all active sites are saturated.
    - **$\text{K}_m$:** The substrate concentration at which the reaction velocity is half of $\text{V}_{\max}$ ($\frac{1}{2}\text{V}_{\max}$).

## 6. Clinical & Biological Importance
- **Pharmacological Target:** Many pharmaceutical drugs are designed to target and inhibit enzyme active sites.
- **Mechanism of Drug Action:** Drug molecules can compete with the native substrate, bind tightly, block catalytic residues, and prevent product formation.
- **Significance:** Characterizing active site architecture is essential for pharmacology, drug discovery, and clinical biochemistry.
# 8. Enzyme Inhibition Mechanisms
Enzyme inhibition is the process by which the activity of an enzyme is reduced or stopped. 
Inhibitors are molecules that interact with enzymes and decrease the rate of enzyme-catalyzed reactions. 
Inhilbition helps regulate metabolic pathways in cells ( Many drugs, toxins, and regulatory molecules act as enzyme inhibitors. 
inhibitors may bind to enzyme active site or other regulatory sites.

**Classification**
1. Reversible
	1. competitive or substrate analogue
	2. non competitive
	3. uncompetitive
2. Irreversible
	1. substrate analogue or affinity labels
	2. group specific
	3. suicide or mechanism based inactivation

**Reversible Inhibitors**
- Bind to enzymes through non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions). 
- Do not undergo chemical reactions with the enzyme. 
- Their bindíng is temporary and can be reversed. 
- Can be removed by dilution or dialysis, restoring enzyme activity.

**Competitive Inhibition** 
- Inhibitor competes with the substrate for the enzyme's active site 
- Forms an enzyme-inhibitor (El) complex, preventing substrate binding and halting the reaction temporarily. 
- Binding is reversible, so the inhibitor can dissociate and allow catalysis to resume. 
- Increasing inhibitor concentration increases inhibition 
- High substrate concentration can overcome inhibition Example -Malonate competes directly with succinate for binding to SDH's active site.

**Non Competitive Inhibition** 
- Inhibitor binds to an allosteric (non-active) site 
- Binding can occur to either the free enzyme or the enzyme-substrate complex, reducing overall activity. 
- Increasing substrate concentration cannot overcome inhibition, since substrate binding is not blocked. 
- Inhibitor alters the enzyme's structure or catalytic ability, preventing product formation. 
- Example - Fluoride preserves blood for glucose analysis.

**Uncompetitive Inhibition** 
- Inhibitor binds only to the enzyme-substrate (ES) complex, not to the free enzymes .  
- No structural similarity to the substrate is required, since binding is to the ES complex rather than the active site. 
- Binding distorts the ES complex, preventing the reaction from proceeding to product formation. 
- Increasing substrate concentration does not overcome inhibition, because more ES complexes actually increase inhibitor binding. 
- Example -Heavy metals like lead, mercury, and cadmium can inhibit enzymes, disrupting body processes and harming health.

**Irreversible Inhibitors**
- Usually form covalent bonds with the enzyme. 
- Cause permanent or long-lasting enzyme inactivation. 
- Inhibition cannot be reversed by dilution or dialysis. 
- Often modify essential amino acids in the active site.


**Allosteric Inhibition**
- An effector binds to an **allosteric site**, altering active-site shape and stopping substrate binding.



# 9. Clinical & Diagnostic Enzymology

## 9.1 Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT / SGPT)

- **Alternative Name:** Serum Glutamic-Pyruvic Transaminase (SGPT).
- **Class:** Transferase.
- **Tissue Source:** Primarily confined to hepatocyte cytoplasm (highly liver-specific).
- **Clinical Significance:** Primary marker for acute viral hepatitis and direct hepatocyte injury.

## 9.2 Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST / SGOT)

- **Alternative Name:** Serum Glutamic-Oxaloacetic Transaminase (SGOT).
- **Class:** Transferase.
- **Tissue Source:** Mitochondria and cytoplasm of hepatocytes; also heavily present in cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, kidneys, and RBCs.
- **Clinical Significance:** Highly elevated in ischemic hepatitis ("shock liver"), acetaminophen poisoning, and acute viral hepatitis.
- **AST:ALT Ratio:** A ratio **> 2:1** (with absolute AST < 300 IU/L) is highly specific for **Alcoholic Liver Disease**. A ratio **< 1** suggests NAFLD/NASH or chronic hepatitis.
- **Hemolysis Avoidance:** Hemolyzed samples must be rejected because lysed RBCs mechanically release massive amounts of intracellular AST, falsely elevating results.

## 9.3 Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)

- **Tissue Source:** Hepatocyte canalicular membrane, **bone, placenta, and intestine**.
- **Clinical Significance:** Strikingly elevated in **cholestatic/biliary tract obstructions**, such as:
  - Gallstones
  - Pancreatic head tumors
  - Primary Biliary Cholangitis

## 9.4 Gamma-Glutamyl Transpeptidase (GGT)

- **Tissue Source:** Biliary tract epithelial cells; absent in bone.
- **Clinical Significance:**
  - Highly sensitive for biliary injury.
  - Associated with alcohol-induced microsomal enzyme induction.
  - Used to distinguish elevated ALP of hepatic origin from bone origin.

## 9.5 Lipase & Amylase

- **Lipase:**
  - Synthesized in the pancreas.
  - Specific for fats.
  - Highly elevated in **acute pancreatitis**.

- **Amylase:**
  - Synthesized in salivary glands and pancreas.
  - Specific for starch.
  - Requires chloride (Cl⁻) as an activator.

## 9.6 Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH)

- **Tissue Source:** Present in almost all tissues and therefore non-specific.

### Isoenzymes

LDH is a tetramer with five isoenzymes (LDH-1 to LDH-5) composed of Heart (H) and Muscle (M) subunits.

- **LDH-1 (H₄):** Localized primarily in the heart.
- **LDH-5 (M₄):** Localized in the liver and skeletal muscles.

### Clinical Significance

- Elevated in general tissue damage and hemolysis.
- Acts as a tumor burden/prognosis marker in lymphomas.

## 9.7 Carbonic Anhydrase

- **Tissue Source:** RBCs and renal tubules.

### Clinical Significance

1. **In RBCs:**
   - Catalyzes:
   
     `CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃`
   
   - The reaction occurs at very high speed.

2. **In Renal PCT:**
   - Brush-border carbonic anhydrase facilitates approximately **85% of bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) reabsorption**.

## 9.8 Xanthine Oxidase (XO)

- **Tissue Source:** Liver and intestines.
- **Clinical Significance:** Catalyzes:

  `Hypoxanthine → Xanthine → Uric Acid`

- Uric acid is the final purine waste product.
- It is the clinical target of **Allopurinol** and **Febuxostat** for the treatment of gout. . 

## 9.9 Prostatic Acid Phosphatase (PAP)

- **Tissue Source:** Prostate epithelial cell lysosomes.
- **Clinical Significance:** Marked elevation indicates **metastatic prostate cancer**.

## 9.10 UDP-Glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1A1)

- **Tissue Source:** Hepatocytes.
- **Clinical Significance:** Conjugates insoluble unconjugated bilirubin to water-soluble conjugated bilirubin.
- Deficiency or impairment leads to:
  - **Gilbert syndrome**
  - **Crigler-Najjar syndrome**
