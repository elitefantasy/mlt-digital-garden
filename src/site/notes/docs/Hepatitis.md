# Classification
There are primarily five types (A, B, C, D, E), plus a lesser-known G virus. 
Hepatitis F was previously identified but removed as it was discovered to be a mutant part of Hepatitis B.

* **Hepatitis A Virus (HAV):** RNA virus, Picornaviridae family (Enterovirus 72), non-enveloped, icosahedral symmetry.  
* **Hepatitis B Virus (HBV):** **The only DNA virus** among the group. Hepadnaviridae family, enveloped. It exists in three morphological forms: spherical, tubular, and complete (known as the Dane particle).  
* **Hepatitis C Virus (HCV):** RNA virus, Flaviviridae family, enveloped, spherical.  
* **Hepatitis D Virus (HDV):** RNA virus, Deltaviridae family, enveloped. It is a **defective virus** (requires HBV to cause infection).  
* **Hepatitis E Virus (HEV):** RNA virus, Hepeviridae family, non-enveloped.  
* **Hepatitis G Virus (HGV):** RNA virus, Flaviviridae family.

❓ the only hepatitis type caused by DNA virus family is :: Hepatitis B Virus

# Transmission

* **Fecal-Oral Route:** ==1;;Hepatitis A and E== are transmitted when the virus sheds in feces and contaminates food and water, which is then consumed by another person.  
* **Parenteral, Sexual, & Vertical Routes:** Hepatitis ==1;;B, C, D, and G== transmit primarily via blood/blood products, contaminated needles/syringes (parenteral), sexual intercourse, or from an infected pregnant mother to her fetus via the placenta (vertical/perinatal).  
* **Average Incubation Periods:**  
  * Hepatitis A: 30 days  
  * Hepatitis E: 40 days  
  * Hepatitis C: 50 days  
  * Hepatitis B & D: 60 to 90 days

# Clinical Outcomes: Acute vs. Chronic

* **Acute Hepatitis:** Caused by **all five viruses** (A, B, C, D, E). Acute infections generally have a 98-99% recovery rate. However, 1-2% of acute cases can progress to **fulminant hepatitis** (severe, often fatal). HAV is the most common cause of acute hepatitis in children, while HEV is the most common in adults.  
* **Chronic Hepatitis:** Caused only by ==Hepatitis B, C, and D. Hepatitis A and E *never* cause chronic infections. Because HAV and HEV do not cause chronicity, they never result in a carrier state, cirrhosis, or hepatocellular carcinoma.  ==
* **Complications of Chronicity:** Chronic infections are rarely cured and lead to three primary fates: an asymptomatic Carrier state, Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC/liver cancer), or Cirrhosis. While HBV is more prevalent globally, HCV has the highest rate of converting into a chronic state (80% of HCV cases become chronic vs. 10% for HBV).

# Detailed Viral Profiles & Lab Diagnosis

## Hepatitis A

* **Features:** Abrupt onset with jaundice, fever, and loss of appetite.  
* **Diagnosis:** Tested via stool or blood. The virus alone is found in the stool. In the blood, you will find the virus alongside antibodies.  
* **Antibodies:** IgM anti-HAV indicates a recent/acute infection. IgG anti-HAV indicates a past infection or recovery (this antibody remains forever).
* which hepatitis is infectious hepatitis :: HAV ( feco oral route transmission), Most infectious hepatitis

## Hepatitis B
- HBV is an enveloped DNA virus that infects hepatocytes.
- 3 antigens: **HBsAg** (surface), **HBeAg** (envelope), and **HBcAg** (core, deep inside). Only HBsAg and HBeAg leak into the blood; HBcAg never enters the blood.  
* produces 4 antibodies: Anti-HBs, Anti-HBe, IgM Anti-HBc, and IgG Anti-HBc.   
* **Serological Marker Interpretation:**  
	* **HBsAg vs. Anti-HBs:** Mutually exclusive. HBsAg means active *infection*. Anti-HBs means *recovery* or successful *vaccination*.  
	* **HBeAg vs. Anti-HBe:** Mutually exclusive. HBeAg indicates *high infectivity* (highly contagious). Anti-HBe indicates *low infectivity*.  
	* **Anti-HBc:** IgM indicates an *acute* infection; IgG indicates a *chronic* or past infection.

## Hepatitis C
- HCV is an enveloped RNA virus that primarily infects the liver.
* **Diagnosis:** Antigens include Core, Envelope, and NS1-NS4 transmembrane proteins. In lab tests, 3rd generation antibodies (against NS5) appear earliest, followed by 2nd generation (C200/C33) and 1st generation (C100).

❓ which is the only hepatitis virus that causes **extra hepatic manifestation** ? ::  Hepatitis C, (involving other organs), such as mixed cryoglobulinemia, glomerulonephritis, and arthritis.  

|Marker|Meaning|
|---|---|
|Anti-HCV|Exposure or infection; may persist after clearance|
|HCV core antigen|Active viral infection|
|HCV RNA|Current viraemia and direct evidence of virus|
|ALT|Nonspecific liver injury marker; not a suitable replacement for HCV testing|

Anti-HCV usually becomes detectable approximately **30–60 days after infection**. HCV RNA may be detectable earlier, and HCV antigen generally appears before antibody



## Hepatitis D

* **Unique Feature:** It is "defective" and requires the helper envelope antigen (HBsAg) from Hepatitis B to survive and infect.  
* **Coinfection vs. Superinfection:**  
	* *Coinfection:* HBV and HDV enter a healthy person simultaneously. 90% of these cases result in acute hepatitis with full recovery. Lab profile shows Anti-HDV \+ **IgM** Anti-HBc.  
	* *Superinfection:* HDV enters a person who is already a chronic carrier of HBV. The prognosis reverses: 80% convert to severe chronic hepatitis. Lab profile shows Anti-HDV \+ **IgG** Anti-HBc.

# Microscopic Pathology (Liver Biopsy)

**Acute Hepatitis Morphology (Same for A, B, C, D, E)** If a biopsy is performed during an acute phase, all 5 viruses show identical hepatocellular injury with four distinct characteristics:

1. **Balloon Degeneration:** Hepatocytes swell and develop granular, pink (eosinophilic) cytoplasm.  
2. **Councilman Bodies:** Cells show intense dark pink cytoplasm with a pyknotic (absent) nucleus.  
3. **Dropout Necrosis:** Only the cellular outline remains; everything inside is necrosed.  
4. **Bridging Necrosis:** Complete rows (cords) of hepatocytes undergo necrosis.  
* Additionally, the portal triad shows **Triaditis** (inflammatory infiltrate of lymphocytes), and there is **Kupffer cell hyperplasia** throughout the sinusoids.

**Chronic Hepatitis Morphology (Same for B, C, D)** Chronic morphology is distinctly different from acute morphology and features specific changes in different zones of the lobule:

* **At the Limiting Plate:**  
  1. *Piecemeal Necrosis:* The limiting plate (the very first hepatocytes in the cord) is destroyed piece by piece.  
  2. *Interface Hepatitis:* Lymphocytes replace the destroyed limiting plate, creating junctional inflammation.  
* **At the Portal Triad:**  
  1. *Bile Duct Proliferation:* Multiplication of bile ducts (many ducts instead of one).  
  2. *Triaditis:* Intense lymphocytic inflammation in the portal triad background.  
* **In the Hepatocytes:** Councilman bodies and Kupffer cell hyperplasia remain present.  
* **Fibrosis (Unique to Chronic):** Instead of just bridging necrosis, chronic hepatitis features **Bridging Fibrosis** (collagen scarring). It occurs in three patterns:  
  1. *Centro-Central:* Fibrous band between two central veins.  
  2. *Porto-Portal:* Fibrous band between two portal triads.  
  3. *Centro-Portal:* Fibrous band between a central vein and a portal triad.
