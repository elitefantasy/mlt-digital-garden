## b. Anticoagulants
Storage Durations by Solution

- Acid Citrate Dextrose (ACD): 14 days
- Citrate Phosphate Dextrose (CPD): 21 days
- Citrate Phosphate Dextrose Adenine (CPDA-1): 35 days, with storage temperatures between 2 to 6 degrees Celsius
- CPDA with SAGM: 42 days

### Functions of Anticoagulant Components

- Citrate and Citric Acid: These act as the primary anticoagulant by chelating (binding to) calcium, which stops the blood clotting cascade. Additionally, citric acid slows the metabolic rate of red blood cells to conserve their energy.
- Dextrose: This sugar serves as the primary energy source for red blood cells, helping them survive during storage.
- Adenine: This component aids cells in synthesizing Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP), ensuring they maintain sustained energy levels.
- Phosphate (Sodium Di-phosphate): This acts as a buffer to maintain an optimal pH between 7.35 and 7.45, preventing the red blood cells from dying in an overly acidic or alkaline environment.
    

### Storage Degradation and Transfusion Criteria
- Stored blood cannot be kept indefinitely; as it ages, red blood cells lose energy, waste products accumulate, and cell membranes weaken.
- The World Health Organization mandates that after transfusion, at least ==75== percent of the red blood cells must remain alive in the recipient's circulation for 24 hours.
- Transfusions are considered ineffective if the survival rate falls significantly below this threshold

## C. Blood bank collection criteria 
### C.1 HB Criteria
- Must be greater than 12.5 g/dL.
- **Testing Method:** Blood banks use a rapid on-site technique called the Copper Sulfate (CuSO4) Method, also known as the Specific Gravity Method.
- **Scientific Principle:** The Copper Sulfate reagent is formulated to have a specific gravity of 1.053, which perfectly corresponds to a hemoglobin level of 12.5 g/dL.

Procedure & Interpretation: 
1. A drop of the donor blood is dropped into the CuSO4 solution from a height of 1 cm.
2.  If the blood drop sinks (typically within 10 to 20 seconds), it indicates the hemoglobin is heavier than the solution (greater than 12.5 g/dL), meaning the donor is safe to donate.
3.  If the drop floats on top, the hemoglobin is below 12.5 g/dL, and the donor is rejected for being anemic.

Collection Volume: 350 ml or 450 ml

## D. Types of Blood Donors

Blood banks categorize donors into three distinct types:
- Voluntary Donors: Individuals who donate blood freely for human welfare without knowing who the recipient will be. They are the safest and most crucial group, providing about 90 percent of the blood bank supply.
- Professional (Remunerated) Donors: Individuals who donate blood in exchange for money. They are considered highly risky as they may hide medical histories to get paid; stringent history-taking is required.
- Replacement Donors: Individuals who donate blood specifically because their friend or relative requires a transfusion, acting as a direct exchange to replenish the blood bank inventory.
    

## E. Types of Blood Donation

1. Autologous donation
	- Donor and recipient are the same person; blood is collected in advance for elective surgery or procedures.
	- Advantage: No risk of alloimmunization or transfusion-transmitted infection from another person, very safe immunologically.
2. Allogeneic (homologous) donation
	- Donor and recipient are different individuals; blood collected for use in the general blood bank and given to unknown recipients.
	- This is the common voluntary blood donation type used in most hospital transfusion practice.
3. Directed donation
	- A specific donor (often family or friend) donates blood intended for a specific known recipient.
	- Used when families request a known donor, though less common where a strong voluntary blood supply exists.
## E. Post Donation Protocol
After successfully donating blood, donors must be provided with refreshments (such as juice or sweet items) before they are allowed to leave the facility. This is a critical safety measure to prevent the donor from experiencing hypovolemic shock or fainting due to the sudden loss of blood volume.
