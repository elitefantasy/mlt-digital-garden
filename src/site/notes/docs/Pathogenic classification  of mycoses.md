### Malasezia furfur / tinea Versicolor
#### Morphology
**A. Microscopic Morphology**
- **Yeast Form:** Oval to spherical budding yeast cells.
- **Hyphal Form:** Short, unbranched, curved hyphae.
- **Classic Appearance:** Pathognomonic **"Spaghetti and Meatballs"** (or "banana and grapes") pattern formed by the combination of short hyphae and round yeast cells.
    
**B. Macroscopic / Cultural Morphology**
- **Colony Appearance:** Cream to yellowish, smooth surface.
- **Consistency:** Soft, buttery consistency.
- **Growth Speed:** Slow-growing yeast-like colonies.

#### Pathogenesis & Pathophysiology
Pathogenic Stages:
1. **Normal Commensal Stage:**
    - Resides as a normal flora in sebaceous gland-rich zones (scalp, face, chest).
    - Exists predominantly in the non-pathogenic yeast form.
2. **Triggering / Transition Phase:**
    - Environmental and host triggers stimulate overgrowth:
        - Increased sebum production (e.g., during puberty)
        - Excess sweating (_hyperhidrosis_)
        - High environmental temperature and humidity
        - Application of oily cosmetics or topical oils
        - Immunosuppression
3. **Morphological Conversion & Proliferation:**
    - Yeast form shifts into the **short hyphal form** (associated with virulence and tissue disruption).
    - Proliferates within the **stratum corneum** (outermost layer of the epidermis)
    - **Note:** It is a superficial pathogen and **does NOT invade deeper skin layers or dermis**.
4. **Enzymatic Virulence Factors:**
    - **Lipases:** Hydrolyze sebum triglycerides into free fatty acids, altering skin lipids and providing metabolic nutrients for growth.
    - **Phospholipases:** Damage host cell membranes.
    - **Proteases:** Facilitate skin surface colonization and tissue breakdown.
    - **Indole Compounds:** Produce immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects that evade host clearance.
    - **Azelaic Acid:** Inhibits the enzyme **tyrosinase** (involved in melanin synthesis), leading directly to **hypopigmentation**.
        

#### Host Immune Response
- **Innate Immunity:** Physical epidermal barrier, antimicrobial fatty acids in sebum, and competition from normal resident microbiota.
- **Adaptive Immunity:** Involves a $\text{Th}1$ cell-mediated immune response resulting in a mild local inflammatory reaction.
- **Immunocompromised Host:** Impaired cell-mediated immunity leads to more severe, widespread, or persistent infections.

#### Clinical Manifestations

1. **Pityriasis Versicolor (Tinea Versicolor)**
- **Description:** Most common clinical presentation; non-contagious superficial fungal infection.
- **Lesion Characteristics:** Well-demarcated, finely scaly macules/patches.
- **Color Variation ("Versicolor"):** Can range from **hypopigmented** (light) to **hyperpigmented** (dark brown or pink).
- **Sun Exposure Effect:** Hypopigmented areas become more apparent after sun exposure because the affected areas cannot synthesize melanin and fail to tan.
- **Symptoms:** Usually asymptomatic or accompanied by mild pruritus (itching).
- **Anatomical Distribution:** Chest, back, shoulders, neck, and upper arms.
    


 2. **Seborrheic Dermatitis**
- **Localization:** Scalp, eyebrows, nasolabial folds.
- **Clinical Presentation:** Erythematous patches with oily, yellowish, greasy scales (dandruff-like flakes), pruritus, skin peeling, and localized hair loss in severe chronic cases.


 3. **Malassezia Folliculitis (Pityrosporum Folliculitis**)
- **Description:** Inflammation of hair follicles due to yeast proliferation inside the follicular duct.
- **Lesions:** Pruritic follicular papules and pustules on the chest, back, and upper arms.
    

#### Risk Factors
- Hot, humid tropical climates
- Excessive sebum production (seborrhea, puberty)
- Excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis)
- Occlusive clothing or heavy oily skin products
- Poor personal hygiene
- Immunocompromised states (e.g., HIV, corticosteroid therapy)
- **Age Group:** Most frequently affects adolescents and young adults due to peak sebaceous gland activity.
    

#### Laboratory Diagnosis

|Step|Diagnostic Method|Key Observations / Results|
|---|---|---|
|**1. Sample Collection**|Skin scrapings|Collected from the active border of hyper/hypopigmented scaly lesions using a sterile scalpel blade or cellophane tape.|
|**2. Direct Microscopy**|10%–20% $\text{KOH}$ Mount|Reveals **short, thick, curved hyphae** along with **round, budding yeast cells** presenting the characteristic **"Spaghetti and Meatballs"** pattern.|
|**3. Wood's Lamp Examination**|UV Light (~365 nm)|Lesions exhibit a characteristic **yellowish to golden-yellow fluorescence**.|
|**4. Culture**|Specialized Lipid-Enriched Media|**Plain Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) does NOT support growth.**<br><br>Requires lipid supplementation.<br><br>**Media:** SDA overlaid with olive oil (or Tween 40/60), Dixon's agar, or Leeming-Notman agar (LNA).<br><br>**Result:** Slow-growing, smooth, creamy-yellow, buttery colonies. _(Not routinely required for clinical diagnosis)._|

#### Differential Diagnosis
1. **Vitiligo:** Lacks fine scaling and shows complete depigmentation (chalk-white) under Wood's lamp without fungal elements.
2. **Leprosy (Tuberculoid / Indeterminate):** Shows loss of sensation (anesthesia) and nerve thickening.
3. **Post-Inflammatory Hypopigmentation:** Secondary to previous eczema/psoriasis; history of prior primary lesion.
4. **Tinea Corporis:** Caused by dermatophytes; typically presents with active, elevated, expanding annular borders with central clearing.
    

#### Treatment Strategies
A. Topical Antifungal Therapy (First-Line for Mild/Localized Cases)
- **Topical Azoles:** Ketoconazole shampoo or cream ($2\%$).
- **Topical Antiseptics/L Commonly Used:** Selenium sulfide lotion ($2.5\%$), Zinc pyrithione.

B. Systemic Antifungal Therapy (For Severe, Recurrent, or Extensive Cases)
- **Oral Antifungals:**
    - **Itraconazole**
    - **Fluconazole**
- _Note: Oral Terbinafine is generally ineffective against Malassezia species._

## 2.1\. Introduction to Cutaneous Mycoses

-   **Definition:** Fungal infections that extend deeper into the epidermis, invade keratinized tissues (skin, hair, and nails), and cause disease.
-   **Terminology:** Also referred to as **ringworm** or **tinea**.
-   **Dermatophytes:** The causative fungi are termed dermatophytes.
    -   They are **keratinophilic** ("keratin loving").
    -   They digest keratin found in the skin, nails, and hair using the **keratinase enzyme**.

### A. Tinea Pedis
-   **Common Name:** Athlete’s foot.
-   **Definition:** infection of foot
-   **Symptoms:**
    -   Itching or burning sensation on the skin of the foot
    -   Peeling or flaking skin
    -   Dry skin
    -   Blisters or thick patches of dry red skin
-   **Causative Organisms:** Three species together account for the vast majority of cases worldwide:
    -   _Trichophyton rubrum_
    -   _Trichophyton mentagrophytes
    -   _Epidermophyton floccosum_
-  **Tinea Pedis Patterns:** Presents in three specific patterns: _Interdigital_ (between toes), _Moccasin_ (diffuse plantar distribution), and _Vesiculobullous_.

### B. Tinea Cruris
-   **Common Name:** Jock itch.
-   **Definition:** infection of groin area
-   **Occurrence:** Most commonly observed in adult men.
-   **Primary Sites:** Groin region, inner and upper thighs, and male genitalia.
-   **Symptoms:**
    -   Red or pink ring-shaped rash
    -   Dry and roughened skin
    -   Small blister formation
-   **Most Common Causes:**
    -   _Trichophyton rubrum_
    -   _Epidermophyton floccosum_

### C. Tinea Unguium
-   **Common Name:** Onychomycosis.
-   **Definition:** Fungal infection of the nails (e.g., toenails).
- **features**: nail thick, discoloration, brittle, distortion
- **pathogenesis**: Fungal inoculation -> Entry through distal/lateral nail -> Colonization of nail bed/plate -> Keratin degradation -> Subungual hyperkeratosis -> Nail thickening and dystrophy
-   **Common Causative Agents:**
    -   _Trichophyton rubrum_
    -   _Trichophyton interdigitale_

### D. Tinea Corporis
-   **Definition:** infection of non hairy skin ( trunk and limbs)
-   **Clinical Presentation:**
    -   **Annular plaques** with central clearing, leading scale, and a ring-shaped appearance.
    -   Mainly occurs on the trunk and limbs.
    -   Lesions may be single or multiple, of varying sizes, and may coalesce.
-   **Causative Organisms:**
    -   _Microsporum canis_ (a frequent cause of human infection).
    -   Micro-morphology of _Microsporum canis_: Spindle-shaped, thick-walled macroconidia with more than 6 cells, along with few pyriform to claviform microconidia.

### E. Tinea Capitis -> hair ka infection / scalp infection
-   **Common Name:** Scalp ringworm.
-   **Definition:** Fungal infection of the scalp involving both the skin and hair.
-   **Pathogenesis:** Infection begins after the fungus invades the keratinised stratum corneum of the scalp, grows downwards into the hair follicle and hair shaft, and penetrates the hair cuticle.
- common in **children**
-   **Clinical Symptoms:** Hair loss(alopecia), dry scaly areas, redness, and itching.
-   **Causative Agents:**
    -   _Trichophyton tonsurans_: An anthropophilic dermatophyte; most common cause in the United States.
    -   Other anthropophilic causes include _Trichophyton soudanense_, _Trichophyton schoenleinii_, and _Microsporum audouinii_.


**Scalp infection types**
**Kerion**: boggy(raised) lesions on scalp, pus filled, soft 
**Favus**: yellow cup like crust(scutula) ; _Trichophyton schoenleinii_.
**Ectothrix**: arthrospore formation occurs on surface of hair shaft _Examples:_ _Trichophyton tonsurans_, _Trichophyton violaceum_ (associated with "black dot" tinea capitis).
**Endothrix**:- Arthrospore formation occurs within the hair completely filling hair shaft. _Example:_ _Microsporum canis_.

### Tinea Barbae:

### Tinea Faciei: 
Fungal infection localized to the **non-bearded areas of the face.**
### Tinea Manuum: 
Dermatophyte infection involving the **palmar** aspect of the hands.
### Tinea Imbricata: 
Characterized by **widespread concentric**, overlapping rings of scaly lesions on the skin.


## exam ready ppints on Lab Diagnosis of Dermatophytes

| Dermatophyte | Key Microscopic Feature | Key Cultural Feature | Characteristic Identifier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **T. rubrum** | Numerous pyriform microconidia along hyphae; few pencil-shaped macroconidia | White-to-cream colony with **red/wine-red reverse** | **Red reverse** on culture |
| **T. mentagrophytes** | Numerous microconidia in **clusters**; spiral hyphae; some macroconidia | White-to-cream, may develop yellowish-brown reverse | **Spiral hyphae** + cluster microconidia |
| **M. canis** | Few microconidia; **numerous thick-walled spindle-shaped macroconidia** (>6 cells) | White-to-yellowish fluffy colony with yellow pigment | **Green fluorescence** under Wood's lamp + spindle macroconidia |
| **E. floccosum** | **Microconidia absent** | Slow-growing khaki/olive to yellowish colony | **No microconidia** + club-shaped macroconidia (2-5 cells) |

 Mnemonics & Key Pearls
- **T. rubrum** → "Reverse is **red**" (urease-negative)
- **T. mentagrophytes** → "Clusters + **spirals**" (urease-positive)
- **M. canis** → "Fluorescent green + **spindle**" (zoophilic, strong inflammation)
- **E. floccosum** → "No **micro**" (only skin/nails, no hair invasion)


 Quick Diagnosis by Specimen Type
- **KOH prep**: Look for hyaline branching septate hyphae; add 10–20% KOH, warm 10–15 min
- **Culture (SDA, 25–30°C, 1–3 weeks)**: Observe colony texture, pigment, and reverse color
- **LPCB mount**: Identify microconidia/macroconidia shape for species-level ID
- **Wood's lamp**: M. canis → apple-green fluorescence (tinea capitis screening)
- **Urease test**: T. mentagrophytes → positive; T. rubrum → negative


## 2.3\. Laboratory Diagnosis of Dermatophytosis
### Specimen Collection
**A. Skin Infection (Tinea corporis, cruris, pedis)**
Procedure:
- Clean lesion with 70% alcohol 
- Allow to dry 
- Scrape from active margin (red scaly border) 
- Use sterile scalpel blade 
- Collect epidermal scales 
- Why active margin? Because fungal growth is maximum there.

**B. Hair Infection (Tinea capitis)** 
- Pluck infected hair with sterile forceps 
- Collect broken hair stubs 
- Collect scalp scales 
- If kerion present → collect pus material

**C. Nail Infection (Tinea unguium)** 
- Clean nail surface 
- Clip affected nail 
- Scrape subungual debris 
- Collect discolored brittle area



### KOH Preparation Protocol
1.  Place the clinical specimen on a clean glass slide.
2.  Add 1–2 drops of **10–20% KOH** (which digests background host keratin while leaving fungal cell walls intact).
3.  Apply a coverslip, gently warm/heat (optional), and allow to stand for 10–15 minutes.
4.  Examine systematically under 10× and 40× objectives for hyaline branching septate hyphae, arthroconidia, and hair invasion patterns.

### Wood Lamps
- UV light (365 nm) 
- Some species fluoresce green Example: 
	- Some Microsporum species → Apple green fluorescence
- Used mainly in: Tinea capitis screening

### Culture Media & Conditions
-   **Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA):** Maintained at an acidic **pH of 5.6**; formulated with peptone and dextrose.
-   **Incubation Parameters:** Incubate at **25–30°C** for **1–3 weeks**; inspect weekly for colony texture and reverse pigmentation.
-   **Slide Culture Technique:** Used to preserve the intact natural architecture and arrangement of conidia for clear microscopic evaluation.
-   **Lactophenol Cotton Blue (LPCB) Mount:** Prepared by teasing fungal colonies to visualize specific microconidia, macroconidia, and specialized hyphae.

### LPCB
LPCB is used to study fungal morphology from culture. It helps visualize: Septate hyphae Microconidia Macroconidia Conidiogenous structures

Basic principle Fungal colony -> Small amount of growth -> LPCB -> Microscopic examination -> Conidial morphology -> Species identification

### Hair Perforation Test
**Principle** 
Some dermatophytes produce enzymes that can penetrate and digest keratin, creating wedge-shaped perforations in the hair shaft. 

**Procedure** 
1. Sterile human or animal hair is placed in a nutrient solution (commonly sterile water with a small amount of yeast extract). 
2. The test fungus is inoculated onto the hair. 
3. The preparation is incubated at 25-28°C for 2-4 weeks. 
4. The hairs are examined under a microscope.

**Interpretation** 
- Positive test: Wedge-shaped perforations or erosion of the hair shaft are seen. 
- Negative test: No perforations are observed. 

**Clinical significance** 
The test is commonly used to differentiate
- Positive: Trichophyton mentagrophytes
- Negative: Trichophyton rubrum

This distinction is particularly useful because these two species can. appear similar in culture.

### Urease Test
Useful particularly for differentiating members of the T. mentagrophytes/T. rubrum group. 

Dermatophyte -> Urease production -> Urea → Ammonia -> pH ↑ -> Color change 

T. mentagrophytes: commonly urease positive. 
T. rubrum: typically urease negative.

**Advantages** 
Rapid identification
Higher specificity
Useful for difficult-to-identify isolates

## 2.4 Dermatophyte Classifcation by habitat

-   **1. Anthropophilic Dermatophytes:**
    -   **Habitat & Transmission:** Primarily infect **humans**; transmission occurs mainly person-to-person.
    -   **Lesion Type:** Usually produce chronic, less inflammatory infections.
    -   **Species:**
        -   _Trichophyton:_ _T. rubrum_, _T. mentagrophytes_, _T. schoenleinii_, _T. tonsurans_, _T. violaceum_
        -   _Microsporum:_ _M. audouinii_
        -   _Epidermophyton:_ _E. floccosum_
-   **2. Zoophilic Dermatophytes:**
    -   **Habitat & Transmission:** Primarily associated with **animals** and birds; humans acquire infection through direct or indirect animal contact.
    -   **Lesion Type:** Usually trigger a significantly stronger host inflammatory response.
    -   **Species:**
        -   _Trichophyton:_ _T. equinum_, _T. verrucosum_
        -   _Microsporum:_ _M. canis_, _M. equinum_
        -   _Epidermophyton:_ None (no zoophilic species)
-   **Geophilic Dermatophytes:**
    -   **Habitat & Transmission:** Inhabit **soil** (involved in decomposition of keratinous debris) and occasionally act as parasites; transmitted through contact with contaminated soil.
    -   **Lesion Type:** Typically induce distinct inflammatory lesions in humans.
    -   **Species:**
        -   _Trichophyton:_ _T. ajelloi_
        -   _Microsporum:_ _M. gypseum_
        -   _Epidermophyton:_ _E. stockdaleae_

## 2.5\. Additional Microscopic Morphology & Hyphal Structures
Dermatophytes are hyaline, septate molds with over 40 recognized species:
-   **Hyphal Characteristics:**
    -   Hyaline (non-pigmented) and septate (cross-walls present).
    -   Branching filaments with a diameter of 2–4μm.
    -   Form an interconnected fungal network termed **mycelium**.
-   **Specialized Hyphal Structures:**
    -   **Spiral hyphae**
    -   **Racquet hyphae**
    -   **Pectinate bodies**
    -   **Favic chandeliers**
    -   **Chlamydoconidia (resting spores)**
-   **Spore Arrangement Details:**
    -   **Microconidia:** Small, single-celled, round/oval to pyriform (pear-shaped); arranged along hyphae, in clusters, or in grape-like configurations.
    -   **Arthroconidia:** May develop within infected hair.

## 2.6\. Pathogenesis & Mechanism of Infection
entry into host through contact(fungal element), trauma etc

after contact fungal **arthroconidia**
1.  **Step 1: Attachment:** Fungal elements / **arthroconidia** adhere to the surface of the **keratinized** tissue.  and germinates, germ tube develop
2.  **Step 2: Keratinase Production:** Fungi secrete active keratinase enzymes to digest keratin. Other enzymes -> protease, lipases, hydrolytic enzymes. 
3.  **Step 3: Invasion:** **Fungal hyphae** invade and **grow** strictly within the **non-living stratum corneum** and **spread *centrifugally***. fungal biomass increases, <mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">fungal growth characteristics are</mark>: centrifugal growth, peripheral fungal activity, central clearing develop, scaling develop
4.  **Step 4: Host Response:** Elicitation of a mild to strong host inflammatory reaction, producing the characteristic advancing red, scaly margin.

Why does ring lesion develop 
Dermatophytes commonly grow outward from the initial site. The center may partially clear while the peripheral margin remains active.

## 2.7\. Host Immune Response
-   **Innate Immunity:**
    -   Physical barrier provided by an intact skin layer.
    -   Inhibitory fatty acids present in sebum.
    -   Rapid recruitment of neutrophils.
-   **Adaptive Immunity:**
    -   Driven by **cell-mediated immunity (T-cell response)** and delayed-type hypersensitivity.
-   **Clinical Correlation with Immune Status:**
    -   **Weak Cell-Mediated Immunity:** Leads to chronic, widespread dermatophytosis.
    -   **Strong Inflammatory Response:** Leads to severe acute inflammatory manifestations such as a **Kerion** (a painful, boggy, inflammatory swelling of the scalp).
    -   **Immunocompromised Hosts:** In rare situations, dermatophytes can breach typical borders and invade deeper subcutaneous tissues.


# 3. Subcutaneous Mycoses  
* Subcutaneous mycoses represent a group of chronic fungal infections that extend beyond the epidermis to involve the deep dermis, subcutaneous tissues, fascial planes, and bones . 
* Unlike superficial infections, these pathogens reside as saprophytes in soil or on vegetation and cannot actively penetrate intact skin
* They are typically introduced into the host's deeper tissues through traumatic inoculation, such as a rose thorn prick, wood splinter, animal scratch, or minor cut, often during agricultural or outdoor manual labor




## 3.2. Risk Factors and etiology
A. High-Risk Populations & Demographics
- **Primary Target Group:** Most frequently affects individuals between **30 and 50 years of age**, as this age group experiences maximum exposure to occupation-related injuries.
- **Geographic Risk:** Rural communities and agricultural regions carry the highest burden of disease.
- **Occupational Hazard:** Outdoor workers, farmers, gardeners, and agricultural laborers are at highest risk due to frequent contact with soil and vegetation.

B. Risk Factors
- **Traumatic Injury:** Direct inoculation through soil or plant debris. Most patients fail to recall the initial minor injury, leading to significant diagnostic delays.
- **Environmental Exposure:** Contact with contaminated soil, decaying vegetation, thorns, and splinters.
- **Behavioral Factors:** Barefoot walking and lack of protective clothing during outdoor manual work.
-  **immunosuppressive conditions**

        
A. Mode of Acquisition
- Thorn pricks
- Wood splinters
- Minor cuts, abrasions, and penetrating injuries

1. **True Pathogens:** Fungi capable of invading host tissues in an **immunocompetent host** (e.g., _Histoplasma capsulatum_, _Coccidioides immitis_).

## 3.7. Differential Diagnosis & Diagnostic Principles
- **Bacterial Mimicry:** Bacterial infections such as **Actinomycetoma** (caused by actinomycetes like _Streptomyces somaliensis_, which produce yellow/white granules) closely mimic fungal subcutaneous mycetoma.
- **Diagnostic Imperative:** Precise microbiological identification is essential because bacterial mycetomas are managed with targeted antibiotics, whereas fungal infections require antifungal therapy or surgical intervention.
- **Diagnostic Methods:**
    - **Serology:** Has very limited diagnostic value.
    - **Clinical Evaluation:** Most cases are initially suspected on clinical grounds.
    - **Confirmation:** Definitive diagnosis requires direct histopathological demonstration of characteristic fungal structures (e.g., sclerotic bodies, grains, sporangia) or isolation in culture.
        

## 3.8. Principles of Treatment & Management
1. **Challenges in Medical Management:** Subcutaneous mycoses are notoriously difficult to treat effectively with medication alone.
2. **Determinants of Therapy:** Selection of treatment depends on:
    - Anatomical site of infection
    - Severity and extent of tissue invasion
    - Specific etiologic pathogen identified
    - Drug toxicity, route of administration, and financial cost
3. **Evidence Base:** Therapeutic guidelines are largely based on open-label trials, retrospective reviews, and clinical case reports.
4. **Recurrence & Surgery:**
    - Criteria for stopping systemic antifungal therapy are poorly defined, making **recurrence very common**.
    - Surgical intervention, including **excision or amputation**, is frequently required for refractory cases or advanced destructive disease.

### Pathogenesis
The pathogenesis of _Sporothrix schenckii_ centers on traumatic inoculation of the fungus into skin/subcutaneous tissue, followed by a temperature‑dependent shift from mold to yeast and a host cell‑mediated immune response that determines whether infection stays localized or disseminates.

#### Entry and initial events
- **Route of infection:** Most cases follow **traumatic inoculation** of conidia from contaminated soil, plants, thorns, splinters, or decaying organic matter into the dermis/subcutis
- **Zoonotic transmission:** In some regions (notably Brazil), **cat scratches/bites** transmit _Sporothrix_ (especially _S. brasiliensis_), acting as an alternative inoculation route
- **Incubation:** Typically **1–3 weeks** after inoculation for cutaneous disease, depending on inoculum size

#### Dimorphism and tissue adaptation
- **Thermodimorphism:** In the environment (≤25–30°C), _S. schenckii_ grows as a **mold** producing conidia; at host temperature (~37°C), it converts to a **budding yeast** form in tissues
- This **mold→yeast transition** is critical for survival in the host and is a core element of its pathogenicity

#### Adhesion and invasion
- _S. schenckii_ produces **surface adhesins** that recognize host extracellular matrix (ECM) glycoproteins in skin/subcutis.
- Main ECM targets: **fibronectin, laminin, type II collagen**.
- Key adhesins
	- **Fibronectin adhesins**
	    - Located on the **yeast cell surface**.
	    - Mediate **adherence of yeast to host tissues** → important for initial implantation and persistence.
	- **Laminin receptors**
		- Present on both hyphae and yeast forms.
		- Bind to laminin in the ECM, aiding attachment and invasion.
	- **Integrin-like and lectin-like adhesins**
		- Recognize specific carbohydrate/glycoprotein patterns on host ECM.
		- Contribute to firm attachment to cutaneous tissues.
- Pathogenic significance
	- These adhesins:
		- Promote strong adherence of fungus to skin and subcutaneous tissue after traumatic inoculation.
		- Facilitate local invasion and lymphatic spread, enhancing disease dissemination.

#### Immune interaction and granulomatous inflammation
- After inoculation, the host mounts a **cell‑mediated immune response** involving **macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, and CD4+ T lymphocytes**, leading to a **granulomatous reaction** over 2–3 weeks.
- A **Th1‑type response** with **IFN‑γ** activation enhances macrophage fungicidal activity and helps control infection.
- Paradoxically, some cell‑wall components (e.g., Gp70) both aid fungal adherence and stimulate protective Th1 immunity

#### Virulence factors that support persistence
_S. schenckii_ uses several traits to resist host defenses and persist in tissue:
- **Melanin production:** Protects conidia/yeast from oxidative stress, UV, and resistance to macrophage phagocytosis ;;;;; plays a major role in causing cutaneous sporotrichosis by enhancing invasiveness
- **Thermotolerance:** Allows growth at 37°C and survival within the host. ;;; At 35°C, it can cause lymphatic sporotrichosis and at 37°C, A primary nodule/ulcer at the inoculation site is followed by **nodular lesions along draining lymphatics**, reflecting local spread and granulomatous inflammation
- Adhesins
- **Antioxidant defenses:** Molecules such as **ergosterol peroxide** help neutralize reactive oxygen species during phagocytosis.
- **Hydrolytic enzymes and other cell‑wall components:** Contribute to tissue invasion and immune modulation.

# 4. Systemic Mycoses (Deep Mycoses) 
* **Description**: Caused by primary, virulent dimorphic pathogens acquired by inhalation; sets up pulmonary focus and disseminates.  
* **Agents/Examples**:  
	* Histoplasmosis: *Histoplasma capsulatum*  
	* Blastomycosis: *Blastomyces dermatitidis*  
	* Coccidioidomycosis: *Coccidioides* species  
	* Paracoccidioidomycosis: *Paracoccidioides*  

#  5. Opportunistic Mycoses
* **Description**: Cause severe disease primarily in immunocompromised hosts (e.g., HIV/AIDS, cancer, diabetes).  
* **Agents/Examples**:  
	* Candidiasis: *Candida* species (e.g., *Candida albicans*)  
	* Cryptococcosis: *Cryptococcus neoformans* and *Cryptococcus gattii*  
	* Aspergillosis: *Aspergillus* species (e.g., *A. fumigatus*, *A. flavus*, *A. niger*)  
# Mycotoxicosis
* **Description**: Diseases resulting from the ingestion or inhalation of preformed toxic fungal metabolites (mycotoxins) rather than direct tissue invasion.  
* **Agents/Examples**:  
	* Aflatoxicosis: *Aspergillus flavus* (produces Aflatoxin)  
	* Ergotism: *Claviceps purpurea* (produces Ergot alkaloids)
