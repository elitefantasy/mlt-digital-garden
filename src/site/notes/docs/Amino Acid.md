# Amino Acids 

## Definition and basic structure

Amino acids are organic molecules that combine to form peptides and proteins. Each standard amino acid has an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen atom, and a variable side chain, called the `R` group, attached to the central alpha carbon

![amino acid structure.jpg\|453](/img/user/Attachments/amino%20acid%20structure.jpg)

${H_2N-CH(R)-COOH}$


The `R` group distinguishes one amino acid from another and determines its charge, polarity, solubility, and role in protein structure.

At physiological pH, amino acids usually exist as **zwitterions**: the amino group accepts a proton and becomes positively charged, while the carboxyl group loses a proton and becomes negatively charged.


${^{+}H_3N-CH(R)-COO^{-}}$

---
- Amino acids with aliphatic side chains: Glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, and isoleucine.
- Amino acids with side chains containing hydroxylic (OH) groups: Serine, threonine, tyrosine
- Amino acids with a side chain containing sulfur atoms: Cysteine and methionine
- Amino acids with a side chain containing acidic groups or their amides: Aspartic acid, aspargine, glutamic acid, glutamine.
- Amino acids with side chains containing basic groups: Arginine, lysine, and histidine.
- Amino acids containing aromatic rings: Histidine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan
- Proline is an imino acid 

## Important properties
- **The Solubility of Amino Acids** :  soluble in polar solvents such as water and ethanol. However, they are insoluble in nonpolar solvents such as benzene, ether, and hexane, etc.
- **Amphoteric nature:** Amino acids can behave as acids or bases because they contain both acidic {-COOH} and basic {-NH_2} groups.
- **Zwitterion formation:** At a particular pH, an amino acid has both positive and negative charges but may have no net charge.
- **Isoelectric point (pI):** The pH at which an amino acid has zero net electrical charge. At pI, its solubility is usually lowest and it does not migrate in an electric field.
- **Absorption of light**: most amino acid do not absorb visble light. However tryptophan , tyrosine and phenylalanine absorb high wavelength UV light
- **Optical activity:** Most amino acids are optically active because their alpha carbon is asymmetric. **Glycine** is the exception because its `R` group is hydrogen, so it has no chiral carbon.
- **L-isomers:** Amino acids incorporated into human proteins are mainly in the L-configuration.
- **Proline:** Proline has a cyclic side chain that links back to its amino nitrogen; it is structurally unusual and can introduce bends in protein chains
- **alpha-R group and functional group of amino acid determine the property of amino acid**
- **color reaction of amino acid**: amino acid react with **ninhydrin reagent** to form a blue complex. amino acid also react with sanger's reagent to form a yellow colored complex
- **use of chromatography technique for seperation of amino acid** 

## Peptide bond

A **peptide bond** is an amide linkage formed when the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of another amino acid, releasing one molecule of water.


${Amino\ acid_1 + Amino\ acid_2 \rightarrow Dipeptide + H_2O}$


The peptide bond is represented as:


${-CO-NH-}$


A chain of amino acids is called a **polypeptide**. Proteins are one or more polypeptide chains folded into a specific functional shape.



## Nutritional classification

Humans require 20 standard amino acids for protein synthesis. Nine are **essential**, meaning they must be obtained from diet because the body cannot synthesize them in sufficient amounts.

|Type|Amino acids|
|---|---|
|Essential|Histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine|
|Nonessential|Alanine, asparagine, aspartate, glutamate, serine|
|Conditionally essential|Arginine, cysteine, glutamine, glycine, proline, tyrosine|

Conditionally essential amino acids may need to be supplied in the diet during growth, severe illness, trauma, burns, infection, or metabolic stress

## Metabolic classification

### Glucogenic amino acids

These produce intermediates that can enter gluconeogenesis and ultimately form glucose.

Examples include:
- Alanine
- Glycine
- Serine
- Valine
- Aspartate
- Glutamate
- Methionine
- Histidine
- Arginine
- Proline

### Ketogenic amino acids

These produce acetyl-CoA or acetoacetate and can contribute to ketone-body formation.

- **Only ketogenic:** Leucine and lysine
- **Both glucogenic and ketogenic:** Isoleucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, threonine

**Exam point:** Leucine and lysine are the only purely ketogenic amino acids.

## Functions of amino acids

Amino acids are not only protein building blocks. They also form important biological compounds.

- Form structural and functional proteins, including enzymes, antibodies, hemoglobin, transport proteins, and receptors.
- Help growth, tissue repair, and maintenance of muscles and other tissues
- Act as precursors of hormones and neurotransmitters.
- Supply energy during fasting or prolonged exercise after removal of the amino group.
- Participate in synthesis of nucleotides, heme, creatine, glutathione, nitric oxide, and urea.
- Maintain acid–base balance through buffering action.
- Transport and remove ammonia safely, mainly as glutamine and alanine.

# Amino Acid Metabolism: 
Amino acid metabolism integrates nitrogen handling (transamination, deamination, urea cycle) with carbon skeleton catabolism (glucogenic/ketogenic entry into TCA/gluconeogenesis/ketogenesis) and anabolism (non-essential AA synthesis, one-carbon metabolism, specialized products).[

## 1. Overview and Fates of Amino Acids

Amino acids in the body come from:

- **Dietary protein digestion**
- **Tissue protein breakdown**
- **De novo synthesis** (non-essential amino acids)

Fates of amino acids:
- **Protein synthesis** (major use)
- **Synthesis of nitrogenous compounds** (heme, nucleotides, neurotransmitters, creatine, NO, thyroid hormones, melanin)
- **Energy production** via catabolism (carbon skeletons → TCA/gluconeogenesis/ketone bodies; nitrogen → urea)


## 2. Nitrogen Metabolism: Removal and Disposal

### A. Transamination (Amino Group Transfer)

- **Definition**: Transfer of α-amino group from an amino acid to an α-keto acid (usually α-ketoglutarate), forming a new amino acid (glutamate) and a new α-keto acid.
- **Enzymes**: **Aminotransferases (transaminases)**:
    - **ALT (alanine aminotransferase)**: Ala + α-KG ⇌ pyruvate + Glu
    - **AST (aspartate aminotransferase)**: Asp + α-KG ⇌ oxaloacetate + Glu
- **Cofactor**: **Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)**, derived from vitamin B₆.
- **Significance**:
    - Collects amino groups onto **glutamate**
    - Reversible; links AA metabolism to TCA cycle (α-KG, OAA, pyruvate)

### B. Oxidative Deamination (Ammonia Release)

- **Main reaction**: **Glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH)** in liver mitochondria:
$$\text{Glutamate} + \text{NAD(P)}^+ + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \alpha\text{-ketoglutarate} + \text{NH}_4^+ + \text{NAD(P)H} + \text{H}^+$$
- Produces **free ammonium (NH₄⁺)**, which is toxic and must be converted to urea.
- **Regulation**:
    - Activated by **ADP** (energy need)
    - Inhibited by **GTP/ATP** (energy surplus)

### C. Ammonia Transport to Liver
- **From peripheral tissues**:
    - **Glutamine** (via glutamine synthetase: Glu + NH₄⁺ + ATP → glutamine) – non-toxic carrier.
    - **Alanine** (via transamination of pyruvate) – part of **glucose–alanine cycle**.
- In liver:
    - Glutaminase: glutamine → glutamate + NH₄⁺
    - GDH: glutamate → α-KG + NH₄⁺
    - NH₄⁺ enters **urea cycle**.

## 3. Urea Cycle (Ornithine Cycle)

### Purpose
Convert toxic **ammonia** to non-toxic **urea** for renal excretion; occurs mainly in **liver** (partly mitochondrial, partly cytosolic).
### Steps and Enzymes
![Urea Cycle.png\|594](/img/user/Attachments/Urea%20Cycle.png)
1. **Carbamoyl phosphate synthesis** (mitochondria)
    - **Enzyme**: **Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS I)**
    - **Substrates**: NH₄⁺ + CO₂ + 2 ATP → carbamoyl phosphate
    - **Activator**: **N-acetylglutamate (NAG)** (allosteric; signals high AA catabolism)
2. **Citrulline formation**
    - **Enzyme**: **Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC)**
    - Carbamoyl phosphate + ornithine → citrulline
3. **Argininosuccinate formation** (cytosol)
    - **Enzyme**: **Argininosuccinate synthetase**
    - Citrulline + aspartate + ATP → argininosuccinate
    - Aspartate provides the **second nitrogen** of urea.
4. **Arginine + fumarate formation**
    - **Enzyme**: **Argininosuccinate lyase**
    - Argininosuccinate → arginine + fumarate
    - Fumarate enters TCA cycle (link to energy metabolism).
5. **Urea formation**
    - **Enzyme**: **Arginase**
    - Arginine + H₂O → urea + ornithine
    - Ornithine re-enters mitochondria.

**Net reaction**:

$$2\text{NH}_4^+ + \text{CO}_2 + 3\text{ATP} \rightarrow \text{urea} + 2\text{ADP} + \text{AMP} + \text{PP}_i + \text{fumarate}$$


(One N from NH₄⁺, one N from aspartate)

### Regulation
- **CPS I** is rate-limiting; activated by **N-acetylglutamate** (synthesized when glutamate and acetyl-CoA are high, i.e., high AA catabolism).
- High-protein diet, fasting, and glucocorticoids **induce urea cycle enzymes**.

## 4. Carbon Skeleton Catabolism: Glucogenic vs Ketogenic

After removal of the amino group, the remaining **α-keto acid (carbon skeleton)** is degraded to intermediates that enter central metabolism.

### A. Glucogenic Amino Acids
- Yield **pyruvate** or **TCA cycle intermediates** (α-KG, succinyl-CoA, fumarate, oxaloacetate) → can form **glucose** via gluconeogenesis]
- Examples:
    - Ala, Ser, Gly, Cys → pyruvate
    - Asp, Asn → oxaloacetate
    - Glu, Gln, His, Pro, Arg → α-KG
    - Met, Thr, Val, Ile → succinyl-CoA
    - Phe, Tyr → fumarate (also acetoacetate)

### B. Ketogenic Amino Acids

- Yield **acetyl-CoA** or **acetoacetyl-CoA** → can form **ketone bodies** and fatty acids, **not glucose**.
- **Purely ketogenic**: **Leucine**, **Lysine**
- **Both glucogenic & ketogenic**: **Ile, Phe, Trp, Tyr**

### C. Entry Points into TCA / Gluconeogenesis

- **Pyruvate** → OAA → gluconeogenesis or TCA
- **α-KG, succinyl-CoA, fumarate, OAA** → TCA intermediates → gluconeogenesis (anaplerotic)

## 5. Amino Acid Anabolism (Non-Essential AA Synthesis)

Non-essential amino acids are synthesized from **central metabolic intermediates** using amino groups from **glutamate/glutamine** via transamination or specific pathways.

### A. From α-Ketoglutarate

- **Glutamate**: α-KG + NH₄⁺ + NADPH → Glu (via **glutamate dehydrogenase**)
- **Glutamine**: Glu + NH₄⁺ + ATP → glutamine (**glutamine synthetase**)
- **Proline**: from glutamate (via glutamate-γ-semialdehyde)
- **Arginine**: from glutamate (via ornithine; in urea cycle intermediates; essential in growth)

### B. From Pyruvate

- **Alanine**: pyruvate + Glu ⇌ Ala + α-KG (**ALT**)
- **Serine**: from 3-phosphoglycerate (glycolytic intermediate) → 3-phosphohydroxypyruvate → phosphoserine → serine
- **Glycine**: from serine via **serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT)** (also generates N⁵,N¹⁰-methylene-THF)[

### C. From Oxaloacetate

- **Aspartate**: OAA + Glu ⇌ Asp + α-KG (**AST**)
- **Asparagine**: Asp + glutamine + ATP → asparagine (**asparagine synthetase**)

## 6. One-Carbon (Folate) Metabolism Linked to Amino Acids

One-carbon metabolism integrates **folate cycle** and **methionine cycle** to transfer single carbon units for **nucleotide synthesis**, **methylation reactions**, and **amino acid interconversions**.

### A. Folate Cycle

- **Tetrahydrofolate (THF)** carries one-carbon units at different oxidation states (methyl, methylene, formyl).
- Key sources of one-carbon units:
    
    - **Serine → glycine** via **SHMT**:
        
        ```
        \text{Serine} + \text{THF} \rightarrow \text{Glycine} + \text{N}^5,\text{N}^{10}\text{-methylene-THF}
        ```
        
    - **Histidine**, **glycine**, **formate** also contribute.
- Uses of one-carbon-THF:
    - **Purine synthesis** (N¹⁰-formyl-THF)
    - **dTMP synthesis** (N⁵,N¹⁰-methylene-THF → dUMP → dTMP via thymidylate synthase)
    - **Methionine regeneration** (N⁵-methyl-THF → methionine)

### B. Methionine / SAM Cycle

- **Methionine** + ATP → **S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)** (**methionine adenosyltransferase**)
- **SAM**: universal **methyl donor** for:
    - DNA, RNA, histone methylation (epigenetics)
    - Phospholipids, neurotransmitters, creatine, etc.
- After methyl donation: SAM → **S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH)** → **homocysteine**
- **Homocysteine** fates:
    - **Remethylation** to methionine:
        - Homocysteine + N⁵-methyl-THF → methionine (enzyme: **methionine synthase**, B₁₂-dependent)
    - **Transsulfuration** to cystathionine → cysteine (requires B₆)
**Clinical link**:
- **Folate/B₁₂ deficiency** → impaired remethylation → ↑ homocysteine (risk for vascular disease, neural tube defects).

**Alkaptonuria** is a rare **autosomal-recessive inherited disorder of tyrosine metabolism**. It occurs due to deficiency of the enzyme **homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase** (also called homogentisic acid oxidase), causing accumulation of **homogentisic acid**


${Tyrosine \rightarrow Homogentisic\ acid \xrightarrow{\text{HGD}} Maleylacetoacetate}$


Because the enzyme is deficient, homogentisic acid builds up and oxidizes into a dark pigment called **ochronotic pigment**. This pigment deposits in cartilage and connective tissues, a process called **ochronosis**.

#### Classical features
- Urine becomes dark brown or black after standing in air.
- Blue-black pigmentation of ear cartilage, sclera, and other connective tissues.
- Early degenerative arthritis, especially affecting the spine and large joints.
- Kidney or prostate stones may occur

## 9. Regulation and Integration with Whole-Body Metabolism

- **Fed state (high insulin)**:
    
    - ↑ Protein synthesis
    - ↑ AA uptake into muscle
    - ↓ Proteolysis and AA catabolism
- **Fasting / high glucocorticoids**:
    
    - ↑ Muscle proteolysis → release of Ala and Gln
    - ↑ Hepatic AA catabolism → gluconeogenesis & urea synthesis
    - ↑ Urea cycle enzyme expression
- **High-protein diet**:
    
    - ↑ Urea cycle activity
    - ↑ Gluconeogenesis from AA carbon skeletons

## High-Yield Points for Exams

- **First step in AA catabolism**: transamination → glutamate → oxidative deamination → NH₄⁺.
- **Urea cycle**: 2 nitrogens (NH₄⁺ + aspartate), 3 ATP equivalents, fumarate links to TCA.
- **Glucogenic AA** → glucose; **ketogenic AA** → ketone bodies/fatty acids; **Leu & Lys** = purely ketogenic.
- **One-carbon metabolism**: serine → glycine + methylene-THF; SAM = universal methyl donor; B₉/B₁₂/B₆ critical.
- **PKU, MSUD, alkaptonuria, homocystinuria** are classic inborn errors; know enzyme, accumulated metabolite, and key clinical features.






## High-yield revision points

- Amino acids contain amino, carboxyl, hydrogen, and `R` groups attached to alpha carbon.
- Glycine is the only achiral standard amino acid.
- Amino acids exist as zwitterions at physiological pH.
- Peptide bonds join amino acids in proteins.
- Essential amino acids must come from the diet.
- Leucine and lysine are only ketogenic amino acids.
- Cysteine forms disulfide bonds.
- Methionine is an important methyl-group donor.
- Phenylalanine is converted to tyrosine.
- Glutamine is an important transport form of ammonia.
